## For the Person With FND

Functional weakness, functional limb weakness and functional paralysis describe difficulty producing voluntary movement even though some movement pathways remain available. **Hoover’s sign** is one way a clinician may demonstrate this difference in a weak leg.

During one part of the examination, pushing the affected heel down may be weak. During another part, the same hip-extension muscles may work more strongly and automatically while the opposite leg lifts against resistance. This is not a test of honesty or effort. It may show that movement is available under one condition but is not reliably accessible during deliberate movement. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

Hoover’s sign does not apply to every kind of weakness and is not perfectly sensitive. Pain, difficulty understanding the task, neglect, severe weakness in both legs and other neurological or musculoskeletal problems may interfere with interpretation. The complete history and neurological examination still matter.

Do not repeatedly perform this test on yourself or ask someone at home to interpret it. The useful question for a clinician is: “Which positive sign supported my diagnosis, and what did it show in my examination?”

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

A moment of stronger automatic movement does not mean the person can produce that movement whenever they choose. Do not use the sign as a challenge—“You moved then, so do it now”—or secretly repeat the test. If the person wants help, record the clinician’s explanation and what the finding may offer as a rehabilitation starting point.

New weakness, a changed pattern or weakness accompanied by other urgent symptoms still requires appropriate medical assessment. A prior FND diagnosis does not establish the cause of every later episode of weakness.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Technique outline: Hoover’s sign

1. Confirm that the technique is appropriate for unilateral leg weakness and that pain, injury, comprehension or bilateral severe weakness will not make the result unsafe or uninterpretable.
2. Position the patient comfortably lying on their back. Explain that the examination compares two ways the same movement is produced.
3. Establish the direct task: ask the patient to press the heel of the affected leg down into the examination surface while the examiner assesses voluntary hip extension.
4. For the automatic comparison, place a hand beneath the affected heel and ask the patient to lift the opposite leg by flexing that hip against the examiner’s resistance.
5. Observe or feel whether downward pressure from the affected heel returns during the opposite-leg task.
6. Interpret the contrast with the complete neurological examination. A clear return of automatic hip extension despite weak direct extension may support functional leg weakness.
7. When clinically appropriate, explain the finding to the patient as preserved automatic movement—not as proof of voluntary control.

This outline is not a substitute for supervised clinical training. The original prospective study reported moderate sensitivity and high specificity in a suspected-stroke cohort, but included only eight participants ultimately diagnosed with functional weakness. It should not be converted into a universal accuracy claim. (*citations* [2](#citation-2))

### Media contributor brief

**People and setup:** A neurologist or suitably trained clinician, a patient who has separately consented to public recording or an actor/volunteer, an examination bed and a second camera operator.

**Essential shots:**

1. A wide side view showing both legs and the clinician’s position.
2. A labelled “direct movement” phase showing the affected heel asked to press down.
3. A labelled “automatic movement” phase showing the opposite hip flexing against resistance.
4. A close-up or diagram showing the examiner’s hand beneath the affected heel; do not rely on the close-up alone.
5. A final clinician explanation: “This contrast may support functional weakness. It does not mean the symptom is chosen, and it is interpreted with the rest of the examination.”

Keep both phases in their true order. Do not edit together different attempts to exaggerate the contrast. Do not identify the participant’s affected side incorrectly when footage is mirrored.

**Suggested alternative image:** Two matching side-view diagrams labelled “direct hip extension” and “automatic hip extension during opposite hip flexion,” with arrows showing heel pressure.

## Research and Sources

### Evidence notes

- Broad clinical reviews place Hoover’s sign among positive signs used to support a diagnosis of functional limb weakness while emphasizing that diagnosis rests on the full presentation. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))
- The prospective suspected-stroke study defined the sign as weak voluntary hip extension with normal involuntary extension during opposite hip flexion against resistance. (*citations* [2](#citation-2))
