## For the Person With FND

Functional seizures are real episodes that may include changes in movement, awareness, sensation, responsiveness or memory. They can resemble epileptic seizures, fainting and other episodic conditions. No single behaviour—closed eyes, shaking, crying, a long event or remembering part of it—proves that an event is functional.

The most reliable assessment begins with a detailed account from you and anyone who witnessed the event. A clinician may review a safely obtained home video. When it is feasible, the diagnostic standard is to record a **typical event** using video-EEG, so the person’s behaviour and the brain’s electrical activity can be interpreted together. More than one type of event may need to be recorded. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

A normal routine EEG between events does not rule out epilepsy. Some people have both functional seizures and epilepsy, so the diagnosis should identify which event type was recorded and how certain the team is.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

During an event, follow the person’s safety plan, protect them from injury and seek urgent help when the situation calls for it. Recording is secondary to care. If a spontaneous event can be filmed safely, record the whole person and surrounding context, note the time, and do not restrain them, test their response in painful ways or try to make the episode continue.

Describe what you observed in ordinary language: what happened first, whether the person responded, how breathing and colour looked, how long the event lasted and what recovery was like. Do not decide the diagnosis from a video yourself.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Technique outline: typical-event assessment with video-EEG

1. Create an event inventory. Ask whether there is one recurring type or several, and document the sequence, duration, frequency, triggers, injuries, recovery and treatment already given for each.
2. Obtain the patient’s account and, with permission, a factual witness account. Review an available home video only if it was recorded without delaying care or provoking the event.
3. Consider epilepsy, syncope, sleep disorders, migraine, movement disorders, metabolic causes and cardiac causes according to the presentation. Do not let an established FND diagnosis end this differential.
4. When feasible and clinically appropriate, arrange video-EEG monitoring intended to capture every typical event type under consideration. Record whether the patient and witness recognize the captured event as typical.
5. Interpret clinical semiology and simultaneous EEG together. An event without an ictal EEG correlate is not automatically functional; some epileptic seizures have limited or obscured scalp-EEG changes.
6. State the level of diagnostic certainty, which event type the conclusion applies to, and whether coexisting epilepsy remains present or possible.
7. Explain the positive evidence clearly and provide a practical event-management and follow-up plan.

### Media contributor brief

The preferred first contribution is an **annotated process diagram**:

1. Patient and witness describe each usual event type.
2. Video and EEG are recorded at the same time.
3. The captured event is confirmed as typical.
4. A specialist interprets behaviour, EEG and clinical context together.

If an authentic clinical recording is used, it must come from an event that occurred during clinically indicated monitoring and have separate, specific permission for public use. Show the synchronized video and EEG context before any short excerpt. Never induce, prolong or repeat a seizure solely to create educational media, and never present an isolated gesture as diagnostic.

## Research and Sources

### Evidence notes

- The American Academy of Neurology guideline emphasizes respectful communication, evaluation for coexisting epilepsy and other disorders, and video-EEG of all typical event types where feasible. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))
- The general clinical review describes video-EEG as the diagnostic gold standard while recognizing that access and certainty vary. (*citations* [2](#citation-2))
