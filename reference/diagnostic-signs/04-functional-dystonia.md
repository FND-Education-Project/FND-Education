## For the Person With FND

A hand that stays curled or a foot that turns inward can make ordinary things awkward, painful or impossible. You may feel a part of your body pulling or twisting into a position, either for a short time or for much longer. This page explains assessment of these postures and movements, including functional dystonia. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

### What this page covers



> **Fixed posture:** A position that persists at rest. **Mobile posture:** A position that changes as the movement continues. These describe what is seen, rather than its cause.

**Dystonia** describes involuntary muscle activity that produces abnormal postures or movements. Functional dystonia may appear as a fixed posture, often involving a hand or foot, or as variable episodes of posturing. Pain, swelling and overlap with complex regional pain syndrome may complicate the picture. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

At the appointment, it helps to explain the task you are trying to do: getting a shoe on, washing your palm, reaching something or finding a comfortable sleeping position. These are examples to help start the conversation, not effects that everyone has.

### Episodic and fixed presentations

Functional dystonia can be long-lasting or fixed, but it can also come and go in episodes or become much more pronounced during a flare. A posture may therefore be absent at one visit, occur only in particular tasks or environments, or remain for a prolonged period. Pain, fatigue, movement, attention or other symptoms may change the pattern for some people, but none of these features establishes the diagnosis by itself. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [9](#citation-9))

### What the clinician is trying to understand

There is no single bedside manoeuvre that confirms functional dystonia. Diagnosis depends on the onset and course, the exact posture, how it changes across tasks, other positive functional signs, and careful comparison with recognized forms of non-functional dystonia. Functional and non-functional dystonia can also coexist. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

The clinician may ask to observe a comfortable everyday movement alongside a requested movement. A useful finding is a particular, explainable difference between comparable actions. Simply moving better once, being in pain or having an unfamiliar posture is not enough. Some fixed functional postures do not change with distraction. The clinician should say what the actual finding was and why it matters. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

### Fixed does not always mean permanently shortened

A **contracture** is a lasting restriction caused by shortening or stiffness of tissues around a joint. That differs from a posture maintained by muscle activity, although both can occur together. Pain can also limit movement. An assessment may need to separate these contributions; it should stay within comfortable, agreed limits. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

Other explanations include inherited dystonias, medicine-related dystonia and other neurological or musculoskeletal conditions. Some have specific treatments. The history and examination guide whether imaging, blood tests or genetic assessment could answer a particular question. Normal tests alone do not identify functional dystonia. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [9](#citation-9))

Research explores how movement control, sensation and attention interact. No routine brain scan or laboratory measurement currently explains an individual's functional dystonia or confirms it by itself. The clinical explanation can be useful even while the precise mechanism remains uncertain. (*citations* [5](#citation-5))

### Daily life deserves its own assessment

The team needs to know how reliably you can manage activities, what support you use and what happens after exertion. A change in posture during an examination leaves those questions open. You can ask: “How will we assess what I need at home as well as the movement you saw today?” See [assessment and everyday function](../diagnostic-concepts/10-assessment-and-everyday-function.md). (*citations* [14](#citation-14))

One published rehabilitation case recorded walking, mood and work/social functioning separately. They did not all follow the same course after discharge. It is one person's report, but it illustrates why comfort, participation and wellbeing deserve attention alongside visible movement. (*citations* [13](#citation-13))

At the onset of a familiar painful posture, protect the limb and find the supported position already recommended by the treating team. Do not force, pull or aggressively stretch the limb back to a normal-looking position. Use your [agreed recovery plan](../recovery-techniques/04-functional-dystonia.md). New fixed posturing, major swelling, colour or temperature change, injury or rapidly changing pain requires reassessment.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

Do not pull, straighten or manipulate a fixed limb for a photograph or demonstration. Ask what positioning is comfortable and follow the person’s clinical plan. Visible changes in posture over time are useful history, but they do not allow a supporter to decide whether the dystonia is functional.

### Before the appointment

Ask what the person wants help remembering or explaining. With permission, bring a short account of how the posture began, whether it stays fixed or comes and goes, and how it affects activities. Note medicine changes and any injury or swelling. Existing photographs or a safely recorded spontaneous episode may help; symptoms do not need to be brought on for a recording.

### During the assessment

Help communicate pain, light or sound sensitivity, difficulty sitting or standing, and the need for breaks. Ask before touching the affected area. The person can keep needed support while the clinician works out which observations are safe and informative. An assessment that cannot be completed should be recorded as limited, not interpreted as unwillingness.

### When posturing appears or worsens

An episode may be brief, recur in bouts, or settle into a posture that lasts much longer. Help the person reach a safe supported position, remove fall or impact hazards, and ask before touching the affected area. Use only a therapist-agreed cue or support; trying to physically correct the posture can increase pain or cause injury.

Useful observations after safety comes first include how the posture began, whether it was fixed or moving, the task or setting, pain and swelling, other symptoms, duration and recovery.

### Leaving with an explanation

If the person wants, write down the finding supporting the diagnosis, what remains uncertain and who will follow up. A useful question is: “What have we learned about the posture, and what still needs assessing about daily life?” The [shared diagnostic explanations](../diagnostic-concepts/README.md) offers further preparation.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Episodic and prolonged presentations

Explicitly phenotype the time course: fixed versus mobile posture, intermittent attacks, task-specific episodes, duration, frequency, warning symptoms, pain, sensory change, precipitating context and recovery. Functional motor symptoms may be recurrent or persistent. Sudden onset, intermittency, fixed posture and pain are each insufficient to diagnose functional dystonia in isolation. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4))

> **Phenotype:** The observable pattern of symptoms and signs. **Differential diagnosis:** The possible explanations being compared.

Use the updated dystonia classification to describe distribution, timing and associated neurological/systemic findings before assigning cause. Review relevant medicine exposure, inherited/acquired dystonia, task-specific and paroxysmal syndromes, and musculoskeletal mimics. Rapid onset can also occur with medication exposure or rare inherited/metabolic disorders. (*citations* [9](#citation-9))

### Positive findings and their limits

The [detailed pattern-based assessment](functional_dystonia/01-pattern-based-history-and-movement-examination.md) expands the former examination outline. Document the precise comparison, pain and mechanical limitations, supports used, and why the result is not explained by ordinary task dependence. Preserve uncertainty when the examination cannot distinguish competing explanations.

A recent review of published misdiagnoses highlights the risk of relying on unfamiliarity or incongruity alone. Its selected cases cannot estimate how often FND is misdiagnosed in practice or validate a mandatory number of signs. Seek specialist review when findings are equivocal. (*citations* [12](#citation-12))

### Function, pain and care-team roles

Neurology should explain the diagnostic evidence and reassessment plan. Physiotherapy and OT can document positioning, comfortable movement, task performance, assistance and environmental barriers. Pain/rehabilitation input may be needed for suspected CRPS or contracture. Mental-health support should address the person's own needs and preferences. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [14](#citation-14))

The Stephen specialist-clinic study found disability and mental-health burden alongside functional dystonia. Retrospective records and referral selection limit inference; employment, benefits and psychiatric history are not diagnostic signs or evidence of motive. (*citations* [10](#citation-10))

Ask what is possible repeatedly and safely outside the appointment, including personal care and sustained mobility. Record assistance and recovery time where relevant. A successful movement does not justify withdrawing support without a separate functional assessment.

### Evidence notes

Clinical differentiation is difficult, particularly in fixed painful presentations. The detailed page separates specialist guidance, historical cohorts, prediction studies and preliminary laboratory work. Original source numbers 1–8 remain stable; retaining them does not mean each primary paper has received a fresh full-text review.

The updated classification is consensus, not a diagnostic-accuracy study. The 2020 rehabilitation report illustrates separately measured outcomes in one person and cannot establish expected benefit. A fuller dystonia-specific quality-of-life and caregiver-burden synthesis remains a review gap. (*citations* [9](#citation-9), [13](#citation-13))
