## For the Person With FND

You may know what you want to say but find that sounds repeat, words stop, speech becomes effortful or your voice will not come out. Your voice may sound strained, unusually quiet or different in pitch. A change in accent or the rhythm of speech can also be unsettling. These descriptions help identify what needs assessment; none establishes a functional diagnosis by itself.

Functional speech and voice symptoms may affect fluency, articulation, accent, volume, pitch, voice quality or the ability to start speaking. They are grouped on this page because the clinical literature assesses them as **functional communication disorders**, often through speech-and-language therapy.

The assessment compares how communication works across different situations. A difficulty may be present in conversation but change during automatic sequences, reading, singing, laughing, coughing or another task. The exact comparison depends on the symptom. A useful contrast shows that a more effective speech or voice pattern is available under some conditions; it does not mean the person is choosing the difficulty. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

A change between tasks is not enough by itself. Fatigue, pain, hearing, language background, medication and structural or neurological disorders may all affect communication.

### Understanding the explanation

Ask the clinician to describe the particular comparison that supports the diagnosis and what else has been considered. Functional communication symptoms can coexist with neurological or structural disease. Psychological stress is not a required explanation for every person. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

### Being understood in everyday life

A clear phrase during a short appointment does not answer whether you can hold a conversation, use the phone, speak through a working day or make yourself understood in an emergency. Ask the team to record what remains difficult, how long communication takes, fatigue or recovery afterwards, and the support you need. These are discussion prompts, not a diagnostic score.

Tell the team about effects on relationships, study, work, confidence or emotional wellbeing if they matter to you. Distress deserves attention without being treated as proof of what caused the symptoms. General fluency guidance distinguishes observable stuttering from its impact; its population is not an FND-specific sample. (*citations* [7](#citation-7))

> **Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC):** Ways to support or replace speech, such as writing, a communication board or a speech-generating device.

Assessment should include a reliable way to communicate while speech is difficult. The choice depends on your language, vision, movement and preferences. ASHA supports access to AAC when communication needs are unmet, regardless of cause; this is communication-access guidance, not evidence that a device treats FND. (*citations* [4](#citation-4))

### Speech symptoms may come in episodes or last much longer

Stuttering, slurred or effortful speech, loss of voice, word blocking or inability to speak may appear only at certain times, last through a longer flare, or remain persistent. Some people find that fatigue, time pressure, competing conversation, sensory overload or another FND episode makes communication harder. These patterns vary and do not mean the person can simply choose to speak normally in a quieter moment.

At the onset of a familiar episode, reduce communication demand: pause, move to a quieter or less pressured setting if possible, use writing, text-to-speech or another established backup, and use one speech-language strategy that has already proved helpful. Do not force repeated words or demand normal speech. Sudden new difficulty speaking or understanding speech can be a stroke warning, even without other symptoms or if it resolves. Seek emergency assessment through the local emergency service; do not assume a previous FND diagnosis explains it. (*citations* [5](#citation-5))

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

Do not demand singing, repetition or “normal speech” to test the person. Give them time, reduce interruptions and use their preferred backup communication when needed. If a speech-and-language therapist has identified a helpful task, use it only in the agreed therapeutic way rather than as proof that the symptom can be switched off.

### Supporting the assessment

Ask how the person wants to communicate and whether they want help with the history. Give them space to answer; do not finish their words unless invited. With consent, bring a representative recording or written examples of what happened and how it affected daily activities. Never record secretly or provoke symptoms to obtain a sample.

Ask the team for a clear account of the finding, remaining uncertainty and follow-up. If a communication aid is used, check that it remains available for questions and consent during the appointment.

### When speech or voice changes suddenly

A short episode may need only time and one familiar cue; another may continue for hours or become part of a longer flare. Reduce questions, noise and time pressure. Ask one thing at a time, allow extra response time and switch to the person’s backup communication without treating it as giving up.

If the person has an agreed speech cue—such as rhythm, an automatic phrase or another speech-language strategy—offer it once rather than repeatedly coaching. Note the setting, duration, associated symptoms and recovery when that information would help their clinician.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

**Featured technique:** Speech-and-language assessment comparing communication across several natural and structured tasks.<br>
**Diagnostic method:** Look for a positive pattern of internal inconsistency or preserved automatic function that fits a functional communication disorder.<br>
**Media needed:** Continuous, captioned audio-video samples recorded with the same microphone and camera position.

### Motor and vocal tic-like symptoms

Involuntary sounds, words or phrases may be vocal/phonic tic-like symptoms. They are not automatically a speech-production or voice impairment; communication support can still help. [See the dedicated functional-tics page](17-functional-tics-and-tic-like-symptoms.md). (*citations* [8](#citation-8))

### Episodic and prolonged presentations

Document whether the communication problem is persistent, intermittent or context-dependent and record duration, frequency, warning symptoms, fatigue and sensory/cognitive load, social or task demands, relationship to other FND symptoms and recovery. Functional communication symptoms may be highly variable, but variability by itself is not diagnostic.

For episodic symptoms, assess a representative sample when possible and consider safely recorded audio/video if the episode is not present in clinic. Convert the best preserved output or useful cross-task change into a brief onset strategy that can be used in real settings. Ensure the plan includes communication access when speech is unavailable; augmentative communication should not be withheld to force recovery.

### Define the communication problem before selecting tasks

Separate voice production, articulation, fluency, language formulation and comprehension in the working description. Do not infer language comprehension or decision-making ability from reduced speech. Record the person's usual languages and communication style, onset and course, and relevant hearing, medication and neurological history.

Speech-language pathology leads the communication profile; neurology contributes when the pattern or history suggests neurological disease, and laryngology/ENT evaluates suspected voice pathology. Voice terminology is broader than FND: muscle tension or a structurally normal larynx does not automatically establish an FND diagnosis. (*citations* [3](#citation-3))

Document the exact positive comparison, competing explanations, level of confidence and plan for any unexplained feature. A change following a cue is an observation, not a diagnostic treatment trial. Do not make access to communication depend on demonstrating improvement.

## Research and Sources

### Evidence notes

- The 2021 international consensus is the principal clinical framework, including Stone and colleagues. It provides expert recommendations, not a validated diagnostic battery. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))
- Chung and colleagues illustrate speech and voice presentations with six clinical cases and a literature review. This descriptive series cannot supply generalizable sensitivity, specificity or prognosis. Its older psychological-causation terminology should not be read as establishing a necessary cause. (*citations* [2](#citation-2))
- ASHA voice and AAC guidance supports coordinated assessment and communication access. Its fluency page explicitly excludes acquired neurogenic and functional fluency disorders; it is used here for adjacent assessment and participation principles, not FND validation. (*citations* [3](#citation-3), [4](#citation-4), [7](#citation-7))

### Emerging research: digital speech markers

A 2025 pilot compared a picture-description task in 30 adults with functional speech disorder and 47 healthy controls. Acoustic and combined models reported AUC 0.98. AUC measures discrimination across thresholds; it does not mean 98% clinical diagnostic accuracy. Neurological speech-disorder controls and independent replication are needed. Only the publisher abstract was available for this update, so classifier methods were not fully appraised. This remains research, not a new recommended clinical test. (*citations* [6](#citation-6))
