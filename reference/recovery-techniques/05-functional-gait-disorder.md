# Recovery Techniques for Functional Gait Disorder

> **Working draft:** This page was automatically generated and is looking for contributors and reviewers.

**Also described as:** functional walking disorder and functional gait abnormality.

Functional gait rehabilitation often changes the conditions of walking—rhythm, direction, speed, attention or support—to bring out a more automatic step. A movement that becomes easier under one condition can be used as a bridge, not as a trick or a test of whether the difficulty is real.

**Evidence labels:** **R** research-supported · **C** clinical consensus · **E** emerging. Most named gait strategies come from consensus; trials test broader FND physiotherapy programs.

## For the person with symptoms

### Techniques used in rehabilitation

1. **Rhythmic weight shift into steps — C.** With a stable support and appropriate supervision, move weight from side to side in a continuous rhythm. Let the unloaded foot slide or step toward a target, then gradually link the movements into walking.
2. **Change speed — C.** Some patterns improve with deliberately faster walking; others with slow, continuous walking. A therapist selects the safer option and prevents the speed change from becoming a fall risk.
3. **Foot sliding or “skiing” — C.** Slide each foot rather than lifting it, then gradually make the slide shorter and the step more ordinary. This can reduce overcontrol of step initiation.
4. **Backward or sideways walking — C.** A different direction may access a more automatic pattern. Use it as a short, supervised bridge back to forward walking.
5. **External rhythm — C.** Walk to counting, a metronome, music or a companion’s steady pace. The cue should simplify walking, not make the person chase an unsafe speed.
6. **Exaggerated movement — C.** High steps, marching or another intentionally different pattern may interrupt the usual gait. The movement is then shaped toward an efficient everyday step.
7. **Light hand weights or another task — C.** Carrying light, safe objects, passing a ball or holding a conversation may move attention away from the mechanics of walking. This is attempted only after basic balance is safe.
8. **Stairs as a bridge — C.** Some people step more automatically on stairs than on level ground. A therapist may use rails and close supervision to learn from that pattern; stairs are not a home experiment when falls are possible.
9. **Treadmill, mirror or body-weight support — C.** These can supply continuous rhythm, visual information or fall protection. They belong in an appropriately staffed setting.
10. **Dual-task walking — C.** Once walking is safe, add a simple cognitive or hand task and progress gradually. The aim is automaticity in real life, not maximum distraction.
11. **Graded community walking — C.** Build distance and environmental complexity in planned increments: quiet indoor route, outdoor path, busier setting. Include recovery time and an agreed response to symptom flares.
12. **Mobility aids and fall planning — C.** Use the aid that gives safe participation now, with training and review. The future plan may be graded reduction, optimized long-term use, or both in different settings.
13. **FND-informed physiotherapy and relapse planning — R/C.** Combine selected techniques with education, goals, strength/endurance work when needed, and a written plan for setbacks.

### A low-risk starting practice

If a clinician has confirmed that supported standing is safe, use a solid surface and practise a few gentle side-to-side weight shifts while looking at an external target. Stop if the knee buckles, dizziness rises, pain changes sharply or control deteriorates. If safety has not been assessed, start with a seated rhythm task and arrange physiotherapy rather than attempting gait drills alone.

### Ask for reassessment when

Walking difficulty is new, rapidly worse, follows a fall or head injury, or comes with new one-sided weakness, severe headache, fainting, chest pain, fever, loss of bladder/bowel control or altered awareness. Repeated falls, injuries, pressure areas from equipment and shrinking community participation also warrant review.

## For family, friends and supporters

- Learn how much physical help is actually needed; pulling or over-supporting can destabilize both people.
- Keep paths clear and practise only in the agreed setting.
- Use one cue—rhythm, destination or task—rather than a stream of corrections about each body part.
- Treat aids as safety and participation tools, not symbols of success or failure.
- Help record functional gains such as reaching a room or shop, even when the gait does not look completely typical.

## For clinicians

Identify the individual gait phenotype and positive functional signs, then demonstrate preserved or more automatic movement where possible. Choose transformations that are safe for that phenotype and rapidly translate them into ordinary walking. Measure falls, walking capacity, participation, confidence and aid use as well as observed gait.

Assess vestibular, sensory, orthopedic, medication, cardiovascular and other neurological contributors when indicated. Prescribe aids and body-weight support according to risk. Coordinate community practice and relapse planning; avoid unsupervised challenge for a person with unpredictable collapse or injury risk.

## Research and evidence

The technique list is derived mainly from specialist physiotherapy consensus (**FND-CIT-0028**). The Physio4FMD trial supplies the largest program-level test: its primary physical-function outcome was not clearly superior at 12 months, while several secondary and self-rated outcomes favored specialist care (**FND-CIT-0029**). A smaller combined physiotherapy/CBT trial reported benefits but needs replication (**FND-CIT-0030**). Neither trial isolates backward walking, rhythm or any other single gait drill.

No community-only technique was added. Repeated user reports largely matched the rhythm, direction-change, attention and graded-walking methods already described in professional sources.
