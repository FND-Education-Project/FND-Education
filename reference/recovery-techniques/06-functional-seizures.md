# Recovery Techniques for Functional Seizures

> **Working draft:** This page was automatically generated and is [looking for contributors and reviewers](https://fnd-education-project.github.io/FND-Education/).

**Also described as:** dissociative seizures, psychogenic nonepileptic seizures (PNES), nonepileptic attacks and functional/dissociative seizures.

Treatment begins with a clear positive diagnosis, an individualized safety plan and continuing care. Functional seizures are real episodes. They are not epileptic seizures, but epilepsy and functional seizures can coexist, so a person’s medication and emergency plan must be based on their own diagnosis.

**Evidence labels:** **R** research-supported · **C** clinical consensus · **E** emerging · **L** repeated community report. Community techniques below are not research findings.

## For the person with symptoms

### Foundations of treatment

1. **Clear diagnostic explanation and follow-up — R/C.** Review the evidence for the diagnosis, how it differs from epilepsy, what uncertainty remains and who will continue care. Understanding the diagnosis is part of treatment, but explanation alone is not always enough.
2. **Individual episode-safety plan — C.** Write down the person’s established pattern, usual duration, injury prevention, when emergency help is needed, and what supporters should and should not do. Keep the plan accessible.
3. **Warning, trigger and pattern mapping — C.** If episodes have a warning, record body sensations, thoughts, situations, sleep, pain and recovery for a limited period. Look for a point where an intervention is possible; avoid continuous monitoring that increases fear.
4. **Seizure-focused psychological treatment — R/C.** CBT-informed treatment commonly works on warning recognition, episode-interruption skills, avoidance, emotion or arousal regulation, and return to life roles. Other psychological approaches may be selected to match the person rather than assuming one cause. See the [functional-seizure CBT resource map and unified booklet outlines](functional_seizures/README.md) for existing manuals, evidence decisions, and a proposed five-booklet practice series.
5. **Breathing-control training — E.** A small pilot tested clinician-taught breathing control for people whose breathing pattern changed before episodes. This is emerging, not a proven universal technique, and should be taught and reviewed rather than improvised during severe breathing symptoms.
6. **Treat coexisting conditions — C.** Epilepsy, PTSD, anxiety, depression, migraine, pain, sleep problems and other conditions may need their own care. Treating them can improve health even when seizure frequency does not immediately change.
7. **Medication review — C.** When epilepsy is not present, antiseizure medication does not treat functional seizures and should be reviewed by the prescriber. Do not stop it abruptly or without checking whether there is another indication.
8. **Graded return to activity — C.** Plan a safe return to study, work, exercise and social activity instead of waiting for perfect seizure control. Driving, water, heights, machinery and caregiving require individualized risk advice.

### Community-reported warning techniques

The following two closely related ideas appeared in at least two independent patient-community resources. They were not found as established techniques in the treatment research reviewed.

9. **Cold or textured sensory grounding at a familiar warning — L.** While already seated or otherwise safe, hold a cool—not painfully cold—object through a cloth, or focus on a textured object. Describe its temperature, edges and texture aloud or silently. Do not apply ice directly to skin, use pain as a stimulus, immerse the face, or delay emergency care.
10. **A fixed visual or sensory anchor — L.** At a familiar warning, choose one visible object or one safe sensory detail and describe it precisely: color, shape, position, sound or contact with the chair. Practise when well so the cue is familiar. It may help some people orient to the present; it is not a way to “snap out of” an episode on command.

Use either community technique only for an established, assessed functional-seizure pattern. It does not treat epilepsy and must not replace the person’s emergency plan. If the warning is too short, awareness is impaired, or the technique increases distress, omit it.

### Ask for urgent help or reassessment when

Follow the person’s individualized plan. Emergency assessment is appropriate for a first seizure-like event, serious injury, breathing or color change that is not part of the known plan, pregnancy-related concern, prolonged unresponsiveness without an agreed response, repeated events without usual recovery, or a substantially new pattern. Do not assume every future episode is functional.

## For family, friends and supporters

- Protect from immediate injury, cushion the head if needed, loosen restrictive clothing and time the episode.
- Do not restrain, put anything in the mouth, inflict pain, or give food, drink or medication during impaired awareness.
- Use the agreed calm response. Reduce the audience and stimulation when this is part of the plan.
- Know the individualized emergency thresholds, especially if epilepsy also occurs.
- Afterward, offer orientation and the agreed recovery steps without interrogation or blame.

## For clinicians

Communicate a positive diagnosis and shared formulation, review video-EEG or other evidence where relevant, and evaluate coexisting epilepsy and psychiatric/medical conditions. Provide a written episode and emergency plan. Refer for psychological intervention and maintain neurological follow-up rather than discharging solely because events are nonepileptic.

The [2026 American Academy of Neurology functional-seizure guideline](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214466) specifically reinforces shared decision-making about treatment, asking how functional seizures affect occupational and social functioning, clearly explaining the diagnosis and treatment plan, and providing continuity of care. These are parts of treatment, not optional additions after the diagnosis has been delivered.

Discuss antiseizure-medication taper only when epilepsy and other indications have been addressed. Include supporters with consent. Track episode frequency, injuries, emergency use, disability, participation and quality of life; frequency alone can miss meaningful improvement.

## Research and evidence

The 2026 American Academy of Neurology guideline finds psychological interventions possibly effective and recommends against antiseizure medication or benzodiazepine treatment for functional seizures without epilepsy or another indication (**FND-CIT-0010**). A small multicenter pilot of seizure-focused CBT reported within-group seizure reductions (**FND-CIT-0032**). The larger CODES trial did not find a statistically significant advantage on its primary 12-month monthly-seizure-frequency outcome, although several secondary outcomes improved (**FND-CIT-0033**). Breathing control has only small pilot evidence (**FND-CIT-0034**).

The two **L** techniques are included as repeated community experience, not clinical evidence. Three independent patient-community resources describe grounding using temperature, texture, vision or other senses: [FND Hope](https://fndhope.org/living-fnd/healthy-living/other-techniques/), [Functional Neurological Disorder Association New Zealand](https://www.fnda.nz/recovery/grounding), and [FND Positive](https://www.fndpositive.org/post/using-grounding-techniques-with-pnes) (accessed August 25, 2026). Their repetition shows that people use the approach; it does not establish effectiveness.
