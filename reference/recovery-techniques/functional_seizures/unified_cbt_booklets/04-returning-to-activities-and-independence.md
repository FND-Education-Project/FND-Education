# Booklet 4 Outline — Returning to Activities and Independence

> **Reader-facing promise:** “Build life outward in safe, manageable steps instead of waiting for every seizure to stop first.”

**Proposed length:** 14–16 pages. **Main job:** address fear, avoidance, lost confidence, and reduced participation with graded behavioural experiments while respecting genuine medical, environmental, and legal risks.

## What the reader should be able to do afterward

- distinguish a medically necessary restriction, a useful temporary adaptation, and fear-driven avoidance;
- choose one valued activity and divide it into realistic steps;
- state a prediction, run a safe experiment, and learn from the result;
- measure participation, confidence, recovery, and support needs as well as seizures; and
- request practical school, work, or community support without treating accommodation as defeat.

One action can change category as health and circumstances change. | Prevents simplistic “push through fear” messaging and validates accessibility. | Three overlapping umbrellas, with fictional examples that reviewers can revise. |
| 5. Choose what matters | Start with a valued role or activity, not the activity a therapist thinks should matter. Ask: “What would this make possible?” | Values increase relevance and make functional outcomes concrete. | A values compass with family, learning, work, creativity, movement, community, and a blank segment. |
| 6–7. Build a graded ladder | Break one activity into steps that vary by duration, distance, company, time, or complexity. | 8. Make a prediction | Write what is feared: “I will definitely have a seizure,” “No one will know what to do,” “I will not recover,” or “I will be judged.” Rate belief without arguing with it. | A specific prediction makes the experiment informative and reveals whether practical planning is needed. | A speech bubble beside an “evidence to gather” clipboard. | 9. Plan the experiment | Choose the step, safety conditions, supporter role, skill, duration, exit plan, and outcome measures. | 13. ## Worksheets to create

### Safety–adaptation–avoidance sorter

The reader lists paused or changed activities and asks: “What exact harm am I preventing?”, “Who gave this advice and when is it reviewed?”, “Could an adaptation reduce the risk?”, and “What prediction is keeping me from a lower-risk step?” Include “needs professional advice” as an outcome.

### Activity ladder and experiment card

Provide six flexible rows, not a fixed ten-step hierarchy. Each row includes the step, support, safety condition, prediction, what will be measured, and review decision. A note explains that a smaller step is good experiment design, not failure.

### Participation dashboard

Track time in valued activity, independence/choice, confidence, recovery time, injuries, emergency use, and seizure impact. Let the reader choose three; do not create one total “recovery score.”

## Lower-demand options

- Use imagery or a planning conversation before an in-person step, while making clear that imagined practice is not the end goal when real practice is safe.
- Shorten duration rather than abandoning the whole activity.
- Change environment, transport, sensory load, or timing.
- Begin with an activity at home that reconnects the person with a valued role.
- Coordinate with occupational therapy, physiotherapy, school, work, or another clinician when physical disability, falls, cognition, fatigue, pain, or complex risk affects the plan.

## Evidence and safety notes for the author

- Reducing avoidance and graded exposure were parts of CODES, but individual exposure exercises were not independently proven.
- CODES mediation work suggests avoidance, anxiety, and depression may explain some changes in some outcomes; mediation is not proof that avoidance caused every person's seizures.
- ReACT included prompt return to school in a paediatric programme; keep this as promising age-specific evidence rather than a universal timeline.
- Driving rules and other legal restrictions vary by diagnosis and jurisdiction. Link to local official guidance or ask the treating team; never print one worldwide time limit.
- Do not turn rehabilitation into compulsory risk-taking, exercise, independence, or withdrawal of benefits/accommodations.

## Reviewer questions

- Can a reader tell the difference between a behavioural experiment and unsafe exposure?
- Are adaptations valued rather than portrayed only as temporary obstacles?
- Does the supporter guidance reduce overprotection without withdrawing care?
- Does every example allow for persistent seizures and coexisting disability?
- Would the worksheet be useful to a school, employer, OT, physiotherapist, or therapist without disclosing unnecessary private information?
