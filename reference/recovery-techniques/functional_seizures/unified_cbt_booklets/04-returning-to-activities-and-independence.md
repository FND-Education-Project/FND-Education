# Booklet 4 Outline — Returning to Activities and Independence

> **Reader-facing promise:** “Build life outward in safe, manageable steps instead of waiting for every seizure to stop first.”

**Proposed length:** 14–16 pages. **Main job:** help the reader understand how fear, necessary safety precautions, accessibility needs, and avoidance can become tangled together after frightening seizures. The booklet then teaches a respectful way to resume a valued activity through small, safe experiments. It never asks the reader to ignore medical advice or prove courage by taking a dangerous risk.

For the conceptual teaching, worked examples, and language cautions behind this outline, read the [in-depth content-creator guide](04-content-creator-guide-returning-to-activities-and-independence.md).

## 1. What the reader should be able to do afterward

### 1.1 Distinguish three different reasons an activity may have changed

The reader should be able to tell the difference between a necessary safety restriction, a useful adaptation, and an activity that has mainly been lost because a frightening prediction now feels certain. These categories can overlap, and the answer can change as health or circumstances change.

### 1.2 Choose a goal that matters to them

The reader should identify one valued activity or role, explain what returning to it would make possible, and divide it into steps that are meaningful but manageable.

### 1.3 Use a behavioural experiment

The reader should be able to state a prediction, plan a safe test, notice all of the results, and decide whether to repeat, change, or stop the experiment. They should understand that having a seizure during an experiment does not automatically make the experiment a failure.

### 1.4 Keep symptom and life outcomes separate

The reader should be able to record participation, confidence, recovery time, injury risk, choice, support needs, and seizure frequency as separate outcomes. Seizure frequency or severity must not be used to judge effort or to infer what changed in the other areas.

### 1.5 Ask for practical support

The reader should be able to request useful school, work, home, transport, or community adaptations without treating accommodation as defeat or dependence.

## 2. Proposed page sequence

### 2.1 Page 1 — Cover: recovery can include doing more

Teach that recovery can mean getting more of life back, including when symptoms have not fully stopped. A person might still have seizures but return to cooking with adaptations, attend part of a class, visit a friend, or direct the help they need. This prevents the booklet from setting up a false choice between complete remission and no recovery.

Illustration idea: show one person considering several ordinary destinations—a kitchen, park, classroom, workplace, or community centre—with more than one accessible route to each. Do not show one route as the heroic or correct route.

### 2.2 Page 2 — Put a firm safety boundary around the exercises

Explain that driving, swimming or bathing alone, heights, open flames, machinery, road use, and sole responsibility for a vulnerable person can carry serious consequences if awareness or control is lost. The booklet cannot decide when these activities are safe. The reader needs individualized advice and must follow local law.

Illustration idea: place high-consequence activities inside a clearly labelled “check with the appropriate professional first” area. Beside it, show lower-risk activities that may be suitable for personal experiments. The point is not that the latter are risk-free; it is that experimentation must match the possible consequence.

### 2.3 Page 3 — Explain how life can gradually become smaller

Use a fictional example. After having a seizure in a supermarket, Sam stops shopping alone. This gives immediate relief from the fear of another public seizure. Later Sam avoids smaller shops, then cafés, then walking around the block without a supporter. The original choice was understandable and may have been necessary at first. The problem is that Sam receives fewer chances to learn what is manageable, practise a response plan, or regain confidence.

Teach the CBT idea carefully: immediate relief can make an avoidance response more likely next time even when avoidance makes life harder in the long term. This is a possible maintaining loop, not an accusation and not a universal cause of functional seizures.

Illustration idea: show a circle of daily life gently contracting across several panels. Add several possible exits, such as accurate safety advice, an adaptation, a smaller step, an onset skill, and agreed support.

### 2.4 Page 4 — Compare safety, adaptation, and avoidance

Define each idea using the same activity. For example, not swimming alone after episodes involving loss of awareness may be a necessary safety restriction. Swimming in a supervised accessible session may be an adaptation. Avoiding even sitting beside a pool because the person predicts that any anxiety will certainly cause a seizure may be a testable fear-based restriction. Make clear that a booklet cannot assign these labels without enough medical and situational information.

Illustration idea: use three overlapping umbrellas labelled “protect from a known harm,” “change how the activity is done,” and “step away because of a feared prediction.” Include a question mark where professional advice is needed.

### 2.5 Page 5 — Begin with a valued role, not a therapist's preferred task

Ask the reader what they want the activity to give back: privacy, friendship, contribution, pleasure, movement, income, learning, parenting, or something else. “Walk for ten minutes” can feel arbitrary; “walk to the corner so I can meet my child after school” explains why the effort matters.

Illustration idea: a compass with family, learning, work, creativity, movement, community, pleasure, and a blank space. Avoid implying that paid employment or physical independence is the highest form of recovery.

### 2.6 Pages 6–7 — Turn one valued activity into a flexible graded ladder

Show how a task can be changed by duration, distance, company, setting, time of day, sensory load, or complexity. A return to a community class might begin by reading the timetable, then visiting the building when quiet, attending the first fifteen minutes with a supporter nearby, and later extending the time. The first step should create useful learning without being trivial or overwhelming.

Explain that the ladder is a planning tool, not a test of worth. A step can be repeated, split, adapted, or postponed when there is a sound reason. The direction of travel matters more than rigidly climbing every rung.

Illustration idea: use a wide ramp with adjustable sections rather than steep stairs. Show side routes for “repeat,” “make smaller,” “add an adaptation,” and “ask for reassessment.”

### 2.7 Page 8 — Write the prediction before testing it

Teach the reader to write the feared outcome precisely. “Going out is unsafe” is too broad to test. “If I stand in the small shop for five minutes, I am certain I will have a seizure and nobody will help” identifies both a symptom prediction and a practical concern. The reader can rate how strongly they expect it without being asked to call the thought irrational.

Illustration idea: place the prediction in a speech bubble beside a clipboard headed “What evidence could this outing give me?”

### 2.8 Page 9 — Design an experiment, not a dare

The plan should name the step, genuine safety conditions, the supporter's role, an optional skill, how long to try, what will be measured, and when to stop. Explain that the goal is to learn, not to force a no-seizure result. An experiment that is too large only shows that the test was poorly sized.

Illustration idea: a recipe card whose ingredients are “step, safety, support, skill, measure, and review.”

### 2.9 Page 10 — Look at every result

Use four possible endings to the same experiment: no warning occurred; a warning occurred and the person remained in the situation; a seizure occurred but recovery was quicker and the plan worked; or the task was stopped for a valid medical or environmental reason. Ask what matched the prediction, what did not, what helped, and what should change.

Teach that behavioural experiments update knowledge. They do not guarantee symptom control, and a seizure is information rather than proof of personal failure.

Illustration idea: four short comic endings followed by four different review notes. Avoid ticks and crosses that define only “no seizure” as success.

### 2.10 Page 11 — Let support make participation possible without taking control away

Describe a supporter who knows the plan, remains calm, helps with agreed safety tasks, and allows the person to do the parts they have chosen to practise. Support can move from close to more distant only through consent and review. Abruptly withdrawing help to force independence is unsafe and abandoning.

Illustration idea: a “support dial” adjusted by both people, moving between types of agreed help rather than simply from help to no help.

### 2.11 Page 12 — Apply the method to school, work, and community life

Give practical examples: a named contact, a short seizure-response plan, a gradual return, a quiet recovery space, adapted transport, remote or hybrid steps, flexible timing, and agreed communication. Explain that only necessary information should be shared.

Illustration idea: a simple timetable showing an ordinary plan, an accessible option, and a recovery option. Include jobs and learning that are not office-based.

### 2.12 Page 13 — Redefine independence

Teach that independence can mean directing support, making choices, controlling personal information, and participating with adaptations. Doing everything alone is not the only valid outcome. A person who chooses and manages reliable help may have more real autonomy than someone left unsupported.

Illustration idea: place the reader at the centre of a support map, choosing what each person does and what information each receives.

### 2.13 Page 14 — Review and resize the ladder without shame

Offer five legitimate next decisions: repeat the step, make it smaller, add an adaptation, move to a larger step, or pause and seek advice. Decisions should follow evidence about symptoms, safety, fatigue, pain, cognition, and circumstances—not pressure to progress on a fixed schedule.

Illustration idea: a flexible route with clearly equal exits labelled “repeat,” “split,” “adapt,” “advance,” and “check.”

### 2.14 Page 15 — Make a two-week plan

Ask the reader to choose one meaningful activity, no more than two initial steps, one prediction, the safety and support conditions, and two or three outcomes. Include planned recovery time so that the exercise does not silently create a boom-and-bust schedule.

Illustration idea: a low-demand calendar with a few practice boxes and deliberate rest or ordinary-life spaces.

### 2.15 Page 16 — Produce a portable activity plan

Summarize the activity, its value, the current step, known safety restrictions, adaptations, support, measures, and review date. This page should be useful in a conversation with a clinician, occupational therapist, physiotherapist, school, or employer while omitting private formulation details.

Illustration idea: a low-ink one-page plan with generous writing space.

## 3. Worksheets to create

### 3.1 Safety–adaptation–avoidance reflection

The reader lists activities that have stopped or changed and answers: “What exact harm am I trying to prevent?”, “Who gave this advice?”, “When will the advice be reviewed?”, “Could an adaptation reduce the risk?”, and “Is there a lower-risk prediction I could test?” One possible answer must be “I need professional advice before deciding.”

### 3.2 Flexible activity ladder

Provide six optional spaces rather than demanding ten steps. Each space names the activity, conditions, support, and intended learning. Explain with an example how one step can be split in two without moving backward.

### 3.3 Behavioural-experiment card

Prompts should cover the prediction, confidence in the prediction, safety boundary, planned test, what happened, what was learned, and the next decision. Include an example in which a seizure occurs but the experiment still produces useful learning.

### 3.4 Participation dashboard

Offer time in valued activity, choice or independence, confidence, recovery time, injuries, emergency use, support needed, and seizure impact. The reader chooses only the measures relevant to the goal; do not combine them into one recovery score.

## 4. Lower-demand and accessibility options

### 4.1 Reduce the size, not the meaning, of the step

Shorten the time or simplify the setting while preserving contact with the valued role. If a community visit is too demanding, making a related phone call or preparing materials at home may keep the role alive.

### 4.2 Change the environment

Consider time of day, transport, light, noise, seating, mobility aids, a recovery space, or a companion. An adaptation is part of good experiment design, not contamination of the test.

### 4.3 Use rehearsal when direct practice is not yet appropriate

Imagery, photographs, route planning, or a spoken rehearsal may prepare the reader for a real-world step. Make clear that rehearsal is not meant to become permanent avoidance when real practice is safe and desired.

### 4.4 Coordinate complex plans

Involve occupational therapy, physiotherapy, the treating clinician, school, work, or another relevant professional when falls, physical disability, pain, fatigue, cognitive problems, safeguarding, or legal restrictions affect the activity.

## 6. Evidence and safety notes for the content creator

### 6.1 Describe components honestly

Reducing avoidance and using graded exposure were included in the CODES programme, but those individual components were not independently proven. Say that the method is a reasoned part of a studied treatment, not that exposure by itself has been shown to stop functional seizures.

### 6.2 Do not turn a group association into a personal cause

CODES mediation work suggests that changes in avoidance, anxiety, or depression may help explain changes in some outcomes for some participants. It does not prove that avoidance caused any one person's seizures.

### 6.3 Keep age-specific evidence in its lane

ReACT included prompt return to school in a paediatric programme. Present this as promising youth-specific evidence, not a universal timetable for adults or children.

### 6.4 Never override law, medical restrictions, or disability rights

Driving and other restrictions differ by jurisdiction and individual risk. Link to local official guidance or the treating team. Do not turn rehabilitation into compulsory risk-taking, exercise, withdrawal of aids, loss of accommodations, or proof of eligibility.

## 7. Questions for clinical, lived-experience, and accessibility review

### 7.1 Safety and meaning

Can a reader distinguish a safe behavioural experiment from hazardous exposure? Is the valued activity chosen by the reader rather than imposed by the author?

### 7.2 Disability and support

Are adaptations treated as valid tools? Does supporter guidance reduce unwanted takeover without abruptly removing care?

### 7.3 Persistent symptoms

Does every example remain useful when seizures continue or coexisting disability limits the next step?

### 7.4 Practical use

Could the worksheets support a useful conversation with school, work, occupational therapy, physiotherapy, or a therapist without disclosing unnecessary private information?
