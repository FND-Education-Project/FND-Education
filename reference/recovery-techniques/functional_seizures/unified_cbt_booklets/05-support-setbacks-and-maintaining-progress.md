# Booklet 5 Outline — Support, Setbacks, and Maintaining Progress

> **Reader-facing promise:** “Agree on helpful support, prepare for symptom changes, and protect safety, access, and the person’s priorities—whether seizures change or not.”

**Proposed length:** 14–16 pages. **Main job:** help the reader and chosen supporters agree on what to do before, during, and after a familiar functional seizure; recognize symptom changes without assuming every change is “just FND”; and maintain worthwhile support whether remission occurs or not. The booklet must never teach relatives to ignore, punish, test, or control the person.

For the conceptual teaching, dialogue examples, and language cautions behind this outline, read the [in-depth content-creator guide](05-content-creator-guide-support-setbacks-and-maintaining-progress.md).

## 1. What the reader should be able to do afterward

### 1.1 Direct their own support

The reader should be able to say what a supporter may do, what they should avoid, what information they may share, and how the plan will be changed. Support is based on consent and safety, not on another person taking ownership of treatment.

### 1.2 Separate four moments that require different responses

The reader should distinguish immediate danger, the familiar episode itself, early recovery, and a later optional review. Safety actions, calming, rest, and problem-solving should not all be attempted at once.

### 1.3 Respond to symptom return without judging the person

The reader should be able to recognize a familiar increase in symptoms without treating it as evidence about what the person knows, tried, accepted, or achieved. Any review or reuse of the plan is optional and should serve the person rather than test them.

### 1.4 Retain medical vigilance

The reader should understand that a new, substantially changed, injured, severe, or otherwise concerning event needs individualized medical guidance. A functional-seizure diagnosis must not become a reason to dismiss every future symptom.

### 1.5 Maintain worthwhile care even when seizures persist

The reader should be able to name continuing goals such as safety, symptom relief, participation, accessibility, recovery time, communication, and supporter wellbeing. Persistent symptoms are not proof of unwillingness or failed effort.

## 2. Proposed page sequence

### 2.1 Page 1 — Cover: agree on roles before a frightening moment

Teach that support is easier and more respectful when the person and supporter make decisions while everyone is calm. The person with seizures should direct the plan as far as they are able and decide who may receive it.

Illustration idea: two people writing a small plan together, with the person who has seizures holding the pen, pointing to choices, or using their preferred communication method.

### 2.2 Page 2 — Name the four moments

Introduce a simple sequence: first check immediate danger; then respond to the person's familiar episode according to the agreed plan; then support early recovery; later, if wanted, reflect on what happened. Explain that the correct action in one moment may be unhelpful in another. Questions that are useful tomorrow may be overwhelming during the episode.

Illustration idea: a four-panel horizontal or vertical timeline using a shield, calm hand, recovery chair, and conversation symbol. A seizure depiction is unnecessary.

### 2.3 Page 3 — Teach immediate safety without inventing a universal rule

The final booklet should direct the reader to their individualized seizure plan: protect from injury, remove nearby hazards, time the event if useful, do not restrain the person, and do not put anything in their mouth. Explain that emergency thresholds depend on the known pattern, injuries, breathing, pregnancy, other diagnoses, local advice, and whether the event is new or different.

Illustration idea: a compact “do / avoid / follow my plan” card. Do not print one worldwide duration threshold as if it fits every reader.

### 2.4 Page 4 — Explain what calm and predictable support actually looks like

Use a short example. Instead of several people asking questions and giving different commands, one agreed person says, “You are safe. I am here. We are following your plan,” reduces the audience, and uses an agreed cue only if the person requested it. Calm does not mean disbelief. Reducing extra stimulation does not mean withholding care.

Illustration idea: show a stimulation dial moving down and one clear speech bubble. Avoid portraying the supporter as stopping the seizure through perfect words.

### 2.5 Page 5 — Explicitly reject reward, punishment, and forced ignoring

Explain that functional seizures are involuntary. Supporters must not shame, threaten, punish, test, film without consent, deliberately abandon, or withdraw necessary help. If the person wants to change a pattern of support, such as having someone complete every post-seizure task, the change should be gradual, agreed, safe, and aimed at restoring choice—not at proving the symptom undeserving of care.

Illustration idea: cross out a reward-and-punishment chart and replace it with a collaborative “what helps, what overwhelms, what protects safety?” plan.

### 2.6 Page 6 — Personalize early recovery

Ask about light, sound, touch, privacy, reassuring words, orientation, rest, and the pace of returning to activity. Eating, drinking, or taking medication must wait until the person is fully alert and it is safe under their plan. Some people want quiet; others want one familiar voice. The booklet should offer choices rather than one correct recovery routine.

Illustration idea: a menu with “yes,” “no,” and “ask me” options beside sensory and communication icons.

### 2.7 Page 7 — Move reflection out of the crisis

Teach supporters not to interrogate, analyse triggers, demand use of a technique, or debate whether an episode was functional while it is happening. At an agreed later time, neutral questions might be: “Did anything in the plan help?”, “Was anything unhelpful?”, and “Do you want to change one thing?” The person may choose not to review every episode.

Illustration idea: two cards under a clock—“now: safety and agreed support” and “later: optional learning.”

### 2.8 Page 8 — Describe symptom return without turning it into a judgment

Explain that seizures can increase after illness, disrupted sleep, pain, treatment changes, a demanding period, loss of routine, or for no identifiable reason. They can also recur or worsen regardless of what the person knows, tries, accepts, or has achieved. The symptom change cannot measure effort, learning, treatment participation, or personal progress.

Illustration idea: show changing weather around the same person while their knowledge, effort, and worth remain unscored. Avoid a route, ladder, “back to zero” meter, or any image that turns symptom severity into a progress marker.

### 2.9 Page 9 — Separate a familiar setback from a changed medical pattern

Give two examples. “My usual warning and usual episode happened more often during influenza” may fit the familiar pattern but still deserves support. “This event had a new one-sided weakness, a significant injury, or a form unlike my clinician-reviewed episodes” should not automatically be interpreted through the CBT plan. The reader follows their medical advice and seeks reassessment when indicated.

Illustration idea: two paths, “familiar event changed in amount” and “event itself is new or substantially different,” both leading to the person's individualized plan rather than to a self-diagnosis.

### 2.10 Page 10 — Review conditions without demanding one cause

Invite a short review of sleep, illness, pain, medication changes, activity load, practice, environment, support, and major life events. The purpose is to find something adjustable or decide that more advice is needed. “I cannot identify why” must remain a valid answer.

Illustration idea: compare two simple weather maps of the person's circumstances, leaving an area labelled “unknown.” This shows changing conditions without suggesting that emotion alone creates seizures.

### 2.11 Page 11 — Offer choices without making symptom control an obligation

The reader may choose one explanation to revisit, one skill to rehearse, one manageable valued activity to protect, one supporter agreement to restore, or no exercise at all. They do not need to complete every worksheet again. Failure to stop a seizure says nothing about effort, and any option should be kept only if it is useful to the person.

Illustration idea: four refresh cards, with instructions to choose one first.

### 2.12 Page 12 — Keep different outcomes separate

Use an example in which seizure frequency has not changed but episodes are shorter, injuries and emergency visits are lower, recovery takes hours rather than days, and the person attends a valued activity again. Explain that these outcomes may matter to the person and must be recorded separately from symptom remission. One outcome must not be used to infer another.

Illustration idea: several independent dials for frequency, severity, warning use, recovery, emergency care, participation, confidence, and quality of life. Do not combine them into a single score.

### 2.13 Page 13 — Explain what continued care can mean when improvement is limited

Teach that continued symptoms do not prove that the diagnosis is wrong, that the person rejected it, or that they failed to work hard enough. Appropriate goals may include safer episodes, relief of pain or fatigue, treatment of coexisting conditions, accessible daily life, participation, mental-health support, reliable follow-up, and help for supporters.

Illustration idea: several equally respected paths labelled “symptoms,” “safety,” “participation,” “access,” and “support,” with no path shown as a consolation prize.

### 2.14 Page 14 — Include supporter wellbeing without making supporters the therapist

Supporters may need rest, reliable information, emotional support, and boundaries. They are not responsible for diagnosing episodes, delivering all therapy, monitoring the person continuously, or replacing emergency and clinical services. Boundaries must be planned so that they do not abruptly remove essential care.

Illustration idea: two overlapping circles, each containing individual needs and a shared area labelled “our agreed plan.”

### 2.15 Page 15 — Build a maintenance plan that can be used under stress

The page should collect the person's familiar pattern, immediate safety instructions, preferred episode and recovery response, early signs of a setback, one skill to refresh, a minimum valued activity, supporter roles, clinical contacts, reassessment prompts, and the next planned review. Use short prompts and large writing areas because cognitive load may be high during a setback.

Illustration idea: a foldout-style one-page plan with clear sections rather than dense prose.

### 2.16 Page 16 — Close the series without promising cure

Show how the five booklets produce a concise functional-seizure handbook: an explanation and goals page, a personal pattern map, tested skill cards, an activity plan, and a supporter and maintenance plan. State that the handbook can change as the person learns more.

Illustration idea: a folder containing the five reader-created pages, not five completed tests or certificates.

## 3. Worksheets to create

### 3.1 My episode and recovery preferences

Include immediate injury prevention, agreed words or cues, touch, privacy, sensory environment, early recovery, return to activity, and actions to avoid. Add who may receive the plan and how consent or changes will be recorded.

### 3.2 Familiar setback / changed-event checkpoint

The person and clinician should pre-fill the familiar pattern and individualized thresholds. The booklet supplies headings, not a universal emergency rule. It should warn against both automatic emergency escalation for every familiar episode and automatic dismissal of every event as FND.

### 3.3 One-part refresh page

Provide four choices—understanding, skill, activity, or support—and ask the reader to select only one first. Prompts should name what helped before, the smallest useful version, who can help, and when to review.

### 3.4 Maintenance and continuing-care plan

Use prompts such as: “Progress I want to protect,” “support I want,” “supporter needs,” “who continues my care,” “what we will measure,” “when we review,” and “what should prompt reassessment.”

## 4. Lower-demand and accessibility options

### 4.1 Complete only the portable response plan

If the full booklet is too demanding, prioritize a one-page plan completed with a clinician, advocate, or trusted supporter.

### 4.2 Offer communication choices

Allow checkboxes, pictures, supported conversation, or recorded audio instead of written sentences. Do not assume the person can answer questions immediately after an event.

### 4.3 Protect privacy

Keep personal formulation and trauma material out of school, work, and emergency copies unless the person explicitly chooses to include it. A response plan usually needs actions, not a private life history.

### 4.4 Account for unsafe relationships

If a supporter is coercive, frightening, or unreliable, develop the plan with a clinician or advocate. Joint exercises are not appropriate simply because someone is a relative or partner.

## 6. Evidence and safety notes for the content creator

### 6.1 Be honest about supporter evidence

Supporter involvement appears in major treatment programmes, but this exact response plan has not been independently proven. Describe it as a practical framework to personalize and clinically review, not as a guaranteed way to reduce seizures.

### 6.2 Do not misuse behavioural language

ReACT changed family responses and used rewards within a paediatric protocol. The unified adult booklet may encourage ordinary healthy activity and agreed independence, but it must reject punishment, shame, forced ignoring, and withdrawal of necessary care. It must never suggest that seizures are intentional attempts to obtain attention.

### 6.3 Preserve medical and swallowing safety

Eating, drinking, or medication during impaired awareness can be unsafe. New or substantially changed events, injuries, breathing concerns, or other individualized warning signs follow the person's medical plan rather than an automatic CBT interpretation.

### 6.4 Persistent symptoms require care, not blame

If treatment has little effect, the response is to review diagnosis, formulation, treatment fit and access, coexisting illness, adverse effects, goals, and supports. Do not equate non-remission with poor motivation or “not accepting” FND.

## 7. Questions for clinical, lived-experience, supporter, and accessibility review

### 7.1 Consent and dignity

Does the plan clearly separate calm support from ignoring the person? Is consent visible in cues, touch, information sharing, and any change to help?

### 7.2 Medical vigilance

Can the familiar-versus-changed checkpoint be individualized without inviting self-diagnosis or giving false reassurance?

### 7.3 Sustainable roles

Is supporter wellbeing included without transferring clinical responsibility to family or friends? Are unsafe relationships accounted for?

### 7.4 Limited recovery

Does the ending offer worthwhile goals, accommodations, and continuing care to people whose seizures persist?
