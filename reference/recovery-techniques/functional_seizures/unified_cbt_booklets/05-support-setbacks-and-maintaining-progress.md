# Booklet 5 Outline — Support, Setbacks, and Maintaining Progress

> **Reader-facing promise:** “Agree on helpful support, prepare for setbacks, and keep meaningful progress visible—even when seizures continue.”

**Proposed length:** 14–16 pages. **Main job:** turn individual learning into a consent-based supporter response, post-episode recovery plan, progress review, and maintenance plan that remains useful in partial or limited recovery.

## What the reader should be able to do afterward

- tell supporters what to do, what not to do, and what information may be shared;
- separate immediate safety, calm episode response, post-episode recovery, and later reflection;
- recognize an expected setback while retaining vigilance for a new or changed medical pattern;
- review several outcomes and refresh a skill before abandoning the whole plan; and
- identify what continuing care and quality-of-life support are needed if seizures persist.

| 2. Four different moments | Immediate danger, the familiar episode, early recovery, and later review need different responses. | Prevents safety actions, questions, coaching, and problem-solving from being mixed together. | Four-panel timeline with distinct icons and no seizure depiction. |
| 3. Avoid a universal emergency time threshold. |
| 4. Calm, predictable episode response | Reduce panic, crowds, and repeated commands when this matches the plan. Use an agreed cue only if the person wants one. | 5. Do not make support a reward or punishment system | Do not punish, shame, threaten, deliberately ignore, or withdraw necessary help. Review support together and change it gradually when safe. | Prevents coercive interpretations of behavioural principles and protects the relationship. | A prominent crossed-out reward chart beside a collaborative “what helps?” checklist. |

| 8. What a setback can mean | More seizures after illness, disrupted sleep, pain, change, loss of routine, or no obvious reason can be a setback—not proof that all progress is lost. | Normalizes variability and counters all-or-nothing thinking. | A hiking path that loops and continues; avoid a cliff or “back to zero” graphic. |

## Worksheets to create

### My episode and recovery preferences

Sections: immediate injury prevention; agreed words or cue; touch; privacy/audience; sensory environment; early recovery; when to resume activity; and what not to do. Include the person's consent about who receives the plan and how updates are agreed.

### Familiar setback / medical-change checkpoint

The person and clinician should pre-fill the known pattern and individualized thresholds. The booklet supplies headings but no universal event-duration rule. It reminds readers that both automatic emergency escalation and automatic dismissal as FND can cause harm.

### My maintenance and continuing-care plan

Prompts: “Progress I want to protect,” “one skill to refresh,” “smallest valued activity I will keep,” “support I want,” “supporter needs,” “who continues care,” “when we review,” and “what should prompt reassessment.”

## Lower-demand options

- Complete only the one-page response plan with a clinician or supporter.
- Use checkboxes, pictures, or recorded audio instead of written sentences.
- Keep private formulation material out of school, workplace, or emergency copies.
- Use a brief post-episode card and postpone review when fatigue, headache, pain, confusion, or distress is high.
- If supporter relationships are unsafe, coercive, or conflictual, develop the plan with a clinician or advocate rather than relying on joint exercises.

## Evidence and safety notes for the author

- Supporter involvement appears in major treatment programmes, but the specific response plan is not independently proven and must be individualized.
- CODES qualitative work supports clear diagnosis resources, individualization, and having a range of strategies. Qualitative reports describe experience; they do not quantify treatment effect.
- ReACT changed family responses and used rewards within a paediatric protocol. The unified booklet will retain collaborative reinforcement of healthy activity but reject punitive wording, withdrawal of necessary care, or any implication that seizures are deliberate behaviour.
- Eating, drinking, or medication during impaired awareness is unsafe. Recovery instructions must specify full alertness and the person's medical plan.
- Persistent or worsening symptoms require continuing care and reconsideration, not blame or automatic discharge.

## Reviewer questions

- Does the response plan clearly separate calm support from ignoring the person?
- Is consent visible in supporter cues, information sharing, and changes to help?
- Can the medical-change checkpoint be individualized without inviting self-diagnosis?
- Is supporter wellbeing included without transferring clinical responsibility to family or friends?
- Does the ending offer legitimate goals and continued care to people whose seizures persist?
