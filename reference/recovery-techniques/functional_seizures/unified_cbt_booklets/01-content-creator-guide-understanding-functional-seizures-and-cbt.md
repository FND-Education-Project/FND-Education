This guide teaches the ideas behind [Booklet 1: Understanding Functional Seizures and CBT](01-understanding-functional-seizures-and-cbt.md). It is written for an author, illustrator, reviewer, or educator who may never have received CBT and should not be expected to infer a therapy model from a list of page titles.

The examples are fictional composites. They show how to explain a concept; they are not templates for deciding why a particular person has seizures.

## 1. The central teaching task

### 1.1 The reader needs two foundations at the same time

The first foundation is diagnostic: functional seizures are real, involuntary episodes caused by altered functioning of brain networks rather than by deliberate action. A positive diagnosis should be based on clinical features, often supported by video-EEG when appropriate, rather than merely on tests that did not find epilepsy.

The second foundation is therapeutic: CBT offers a structured way to notice relationships among body sensations, attention, meanings, emotions, actions, and circumstances, then test whether changing one reachable part improves symptoms or life. CBT is not proof that the symptoms are imaginary or “only psychological.”

If the booklet teaches CBT before establishing the first foundation, a reader may hear: “The doctor thinks this is all in my head.” If it explains the diagnosis but never makes CBT concrete, the reader may hear: “I was referred to generic counselling because medicine has nothing else to offer.” The booklet must connect the two without claiming that CBT explains every seizure.

### 1.2 A useful one-sentence description

An accessible starting sentence is:

> CBT is a practical, collaborative way to map what happens around a problem and test whether changing one part of the pattern helps.

“Collaborative” means that the therapist and patient build and revise the explanation together. “Map” means they organize information without pretending to have discovered one hidden cause. “Test” means they try a small, safe change and observe the outcome. “Helps” leaves room for more than complete seizure cessation.

## 2. What CBT is not

### 2.1 It is not positive thinking

CBT does not require a person to replace every frightening thought with a cheerful one. It asks whether a prediction is specific, whether it fits all the evidence, and whether a safe experiment can produce new information.

For example, “I am certain that if I feel dizzy in a shop I will have a seizure and no one will help” contains several testable parts. The goal is not to write “Everything will be fine.” A more useful response is: “Dizziness is one warning for me, but it has not always led to a seizure. I can visit the small shop for five minutes with an agreed safety and support plan, then review what happened.”

### 2.2 It is not a search for the thought that caused the seizure

Sometimes a thought, memory, emotion, or threat interpretation is relevant. Sometimes bodily illness, pain, fatigue, sensory load, learned warning responses, or no obvious precipitant is more prominent. Many factors can interact. The author should never imply that every episode begins because the person “thought wrong.”

### 2.3 It is not proof of a psychological origin

Psychological treatment is used for many problems that are not invented and not purely psychological, including pain, insomnia, and coping with cancer. The reason to offer CBT is that learning, attention, prediction, behaviour, distress, and social responses can be reachable parts of a functional-seizure pattern—not because symptoms must have been created by emotional trauma.

### 2.4 It is not a test of whether the reader accepts the diagnosis

A patient can understand that seizures are functional and still have doubts, grief, anger, or questions. These are subjects for discussion, not evidence of resistance. Treatment response also must not be used retrospectively to “prove” the diagnosis: improving with CBT does not prove that symptoms were psychological, and not improving does not disprove FND or prove poor effort.

## 3. The CBT map in functional-seizure language

### 3.1 Body signals

These can include dizziness, altered vision, rising sensations, pain, shaking, breathlessness, numbness, derealization, fatigue, or a hard-to-name sense that something is changing. They may be warnings, consequences, background conditions, or unrelated symptoms. The author should describe them as observations, not automatically as anxiety.

### 3.2 Attention

Attention is what the brain is selecting and emphasizing. After frightening seizures, a person may understandably scan constantly for a warning. This can make ordinary or ambiguous sensations feel louder and more urgent. In other people, awareness narrows or disconnects rapidly and there is little opportunity to notice a warning. Attention is one possible mechanism, not a universal explanation.

### 3.3 Meaning and prediction

The meaning attached to a sensation can change the whole sequence. “This is uncomfortable” and “This means I will collapse in public and never recover” do not create the same level of threat. Predictions also concern other people: “They will think I am faking,” “Nobody will know what to do,” or “My family will panic.” Some predictions are realistic concerns that need planning; they should not all be challenged as distorted thinking.

### 3.4 Emotion and nervous-system state

Fear, shame, frustration, helplessness, or emotional numbing may become part of the pattern, but emotion is not required for a functional seizure to be real. The accessible explanation is that a brain and body can move into a high-alert, overloaded, or disconnected state for many reasons. The author should not teach that stress is always present or that relaxation is the cure.

### 3.5 Action

Actions include sitting down safely, using a cue, calling a supporter, leaving a place, cancelling an activity, seeking emergency care, monitoring symptoms, or returning to ordinary activity. An action can be protective in one context and restrictive in another. CBT asks what an action does immediately and what it teaches or costs over time.

### 3.6 Environment and other people

Noise, heat, crowds, conflict, work demands, inaccessible transport, financial strain, clinician responses, and supporter reactions may all matter. Including environment prevents a falsely individualistic message in which the patient must change their thoughts while genuine barriers remain untouched.

### 3.7 Consequences

The episode may lead to injury, exhaustion, pain, lost work, fear, attention from others, medical procedures, or relief from an impossible demand. A consequence is simply what follows; it is not evidence of intention. In particular, attention or escape after a seizure must never be described as proof that the person produced it for gain.

## 4. How a feedback loop differs from a root cause

### 4.1 Use a loop to show something changeable

Consider this fictional sequence:

1. Maya notices a wave of dizziness in a crowded shop.
2. She predicts, “A seizure is definitely starting, and I will be trapped.”
3. Her attention narrows onto every internal sensation and the exits.
4. Alarm rises and the sensations become more intense.
5. She leaves immediately and asks her partner to finish the shopping.
6. The fear drops quickly, which makes immediate leaving feel essential next time.
7. Because Maya no longer enters shops, she gets no chance to learn which warnings pass, which skill is usable, or whether a smaller shop can be managed with a plan.

This loop suggests several possible intervention points: an onset skill, a less catastrophic and more precise prediction, a quieter shopping time, a supporter plan, or a graded return. It does not show what originally caused Maya's disorder. Perhaps the first seizure followed an infection; perhaps it followed trauma; perhaps no clear beginning is known. A current maintaining process and an original cause are different questions.

### 4.2 Every loop needs more than one exit

A visual with one exit labelled “change your thoughts” will overstate cognitive control. Show possible changes in the body, attention, action, environment, and support. Also show “seek medical advice” when a signal is new or substantially changed. This makes the formulation collaborative and testable rather than moralistic.

## 5. What a formulation is

### 5.1 A formulation is a working explanation, not a verdict

A formulation organizes what may predispose a person to problems, what may have precipitated the onset, what appears to maintain current difficulties, and what protects or helps them. It should explain why a particular intervention is being tried now.

The ordinary-language version can be:

> “This is our current best map of what seems to make episodes more likely, what follows them, and where we might have some influence. We will change the map when your experience does not fit it.”

### 5.2 The formulation must contain evidence and uncertainty

Suppose a patient reports episodes after poor sleep on three occasions but many other episodes without poor sleep. The formulation can say, “Poor sleep may increase vulnerability,” not “Your seizures are caused by poor sleep.” If there is no clear link with emotion, do not insert one to make the map look complete.

### 5.3 The formulation should produce an understandable next step

A map is only useful when it answers, “Given this pattern, what could we safely try?” If warning monitoring appears to increase alarm, the experiment may be brief scheduled recording plus an attention-shifting skill. If no warning is available, onset interruption may not be a fair goal; the plan may focus on safety, recovery, supporter response, and participation instead.

## 6. How treatment can help without promising seizure freedom

### 6.1 Separate possible routes of improvement

CBT-informed treatment may help a person understand episodes, reduce fear and avoidance, use warning periods differently, respond more consistently, return to activity, communicate needs, or treat coexisting anxiety or depression. Any of these may reduce seizure burden for some people, but none guarantees it.

### 6.2 Make outcomes multidimensional

Use a fictional example. Before treatment, Jordan has four seizures per month, two days of recovery after each, frequent injuries, and several emergency visits. Later Jordan still has four seizures per month, but recovery is two hours, injuries and emergency visits are rare, and a weekly art group is possible again. This is meaningful improvement, though not symptom remission.

Another reader may place seizure freedom first. The booklet should allow that priority while also tracking what happens before remission is reached.

## 7. How to explain the research faithfully

### 7.1 Say what was studied, not merely that “CBT works”

The large adult CODES trial studied a specific dissociative-seizure CBT programme plus standardized medical care. The primary seizure-frequency outcome at twelve months was not statistically significantly different from standardized medical care alone, while several secondary outcomes favoured the CBT arm. This means the study does not support a simple promise that CBT will stop seizures, but it also does not mean that nothing improved.

A small randomized paediatric ReACT trial found a strong short-term seizure outcome in its studied group. It used a different theory and protocol, involved children and families, and needs replication and longer-term context. Do not blend the adult and child results into one universal effect size.

### 7.2 Components are not independently proven just because they appear in a manual

Grounding, attention exercises, formulation, graded activity, supporter work, and relapse planning may appear within a treatment package. A package trial generally cannot tell us which single page or skill caused an outcome. Use wording such as “included in a studied programme” or “may help with this part of the pattern.”

### 7.3 Evidence limits belong beside the hope

The reader-facing message can be:

> “Some people become seizure-free. Others have fewer or less disruptive seizures, recover faster, return to activities, or find that a particular CBT approach is not helpful. We cannot predict your result from a booklet. Your goals and response should be reviewed with you.”

## 8. How to write and illustrate the booklet

### 8.1 Use one fictional person across several pages

A continuing example lets the reader see a concept become an action. Show the person first receiving a clear diagnosis, then drawing a tentative map, choosing one outcome, and planning the next booklet. Label the story “fictional example” and avoid making it a stereotyped trauma narrative.

### 8.2 Pair every abstract concept with an ordinary example

After defining attention, show what attention does in a shop. After defining prediction, show a prediction that includes both symptoms and social consequences. After defining a behavioural experiment, show what will be tested and how several outcomes could be informative.

### 8.3 Design diagrams as hypotheses

Use dotted lines, question marks, movable cards, and more than one exit. Avoid a polished chain that appears to prove a universal mechanism. The reader should be able to cross out a factor, add one, or write “unknown.”

### 8.4 Watch for four damaging shortcuts

Do not write “stress causes your seizures.” Do not imply that trauma must be found. Do not call symptoms a choice or behaviour problem. Do not make seizure cessation the only evidence of effort or recovery.

## 9. What the content creator should be able to explain before drafting

Before writing the reader-facing pages, the creator should be able to answer these questions in plain language:

1. Why can CBT be relevant to a real neurological disorder without making it imaginary?
2. What is the difference between a working feedback loop and the original cause of FND?
3. How can attention, prediction, action, environment, and support interact in one fictional episode?
4. Why is a behavioural experiment more than positive thinking?
5. How could a person improve meaningfully without becoming seizure-free?
6. What did the adult and paediatric studies actually establish, and what remains uncertain?

If the draft cannot answer these questions, it is not ready for reader testing.
