# Content-Creator Guide 5 — Support, Setbacks, and Maintaining Progress

This guide teaches the ideas behind [Booklet 5: Support, Setbacks, and Maintaining Progress](05-support-setbacks-and-maintaining-progress.md). It is designed to prevent two common harms: turning supporters into behaviour police, and describing persistent seizures as evidence that the patient failed CBT.

All examples are fictional composites. A real response plan needs the person's consent, individualized medical advice, and revision when the seizure pattern or circumstances change.

## 1. The purpose of a supporter plan

### 1.1 Predictability reduces avoidable confusion

During a seizure, one person may call an ambulance, another may shout instructions, another may film the event, and another may leave because they have been told not to “reinforce” it. A pre-agreed plan replaces improvised reactions with actions chosen for safety, dignity, and the person's familiar pattern.

The plan is not a technique for proving that other people's responses cause seizures. It is practical preparation for events that can be frightening and cognitively demanding.

### 1.2 The person remains the author of the plan

The person chooses who receives the plan, what cues or touch are acceptable, what privacy they want, and how help may change. Capacity and communication support may need consideration, but a diagnosis of functional seizures does not remove autonomy.

### 1.3 A supporter is not the therapist or diagnostician

The supporter can follow a familiar response plan, notice agreed signs, help with safety, and support activity. They should not decide that a new event is functional, analyse trauma, judge whether an episode is genuine, or be responsible for stopping it.

## 2. Teach four different moments

### 2.1 Immediate danger

The first question is whether the person is at risk from traffic, water, heat, falls, machinery, injury, breathing problems, or another individualized concern. Protect from injury and follow the medical plan. CBT exercises are secondary.

### 2.2 The familiar episode

Once immediate danger is addressed and the event matches the clinician-reviewed familiar pattern, use the agreed calm response. This might mean reducing the crowd, timing the event, protecting privacy, speaking one short orienting sentence, or simply staying nearby.

### 2.3 Early recovery

The person may have pain, fatigue, confusion, speech difficulty, sensory sensitivity, shame, or a wish to resume activity. Preferences vary. Food, drink, or medication must wait until the person is fully alert and safe according to their plan.

### 2.4 Later optional review

Problem-solving belongs later, when the person has recovered and wants to review. Not every event needs analysis. The supporter can ask what helped, what overwhelmed, and whether one plan detail should change.

Keeping these moments separate is itself a CBT-informed intervention: it connects the right action to the right cue and reduces chaotic responses that may add alarm or burden.

## 3. What calm, predictable support means

### 3.1 Calm is an action style, not a judgment

Calm support can include a steady voice, fewer people speaking, reduced noise, removal of hazards, and the same agreed wording each time. It communicates, “This is real, and we know what to do.” It does not communicate, “Nothing is happening.”

### 3.2 A dialogue example

Before the event, Amara has agreed that her partner should say one phrase and then remain quietly nearby. During a familiar episode the partner says, “You are safe on the floor. I am here and following our plan.” The partner moves a chair away, asks others to give space, and does not repeatedly demand eye contact or ask for a cause.

Later, when Amara has recovered, the partner asks, “Was the sentence useful, or would you prefer fewer words next time?” The plan changes only with Amara's input.

### 3.3 A cue is optional and practised

If the person has a warning and wants a supporter cue, it should retrieve a rehearsed step: “feet, room, next.” Repeated coaching, surprise commands, or a supporter testing whether the person can respond can increase cognitive load and undermine trust.

## 4. Behavioural principles must not become coercion

### 4.1 Functional seizures are involuntary

Consequences can influence future patterns without the symptom being intentional. A seizure may be followed by escape from a demand, emergency care, comfort, conflict, or isolation. Noticing what follows is not evidence that the person produced the event to obtain it.

### 4.2 Reject punishment and deliberate abandonment

The booklet must explicitly prohibit shame, threats, restraint outside emergency necessity, forced ignoring, filming without consent, removal of essential help, and reward charts tied to whether the person has a seizure. These responses can cause harm and rest on a false assumption of voluntary behaviour.

### 4.3 Reinforce ordinary life, not “being symptom-free”

If behavioural encouragement is used, it should be ordinary, collaborative support for a valued action: acknowledging that the person used the plan, returned to an activity, communicated a need, or practised a skill. Care and affection are not prizes that must be withheld after symptoms.

### 4.4 Change over-help gradually and consensually

Suppose a supporter completes every household task for the day after a seizure even when the person sometimes prefers to resume one light task after an hour. The pair might agree to ask rather than assume, or to leave one chosen task available. The aim is choice and participation. It is not to force activity to demonstrate wellness.

## 5. Personalize early recovery

### 5.1 Offer a menu instead of one rule

The person can indicate preferences about light, sound, touch, words, audience, positioning, orientation, rest, and when to discuss the event. “Ask me when able” must be an option because preference may vary.

### 5.2 Distinguish support from interrogation

Questions such as “What triggered it?”, “Did you use your skill?”, or “Why didn't you stop it?” can add shame and demand cognition that is not available. In early recovery, use only questions needed for safety and immediate comfort.

### 5.3 Plan the return to activity flexibly

Some people benefit from resuming the interrupted activity when safe; others need substantial recovery. The plan should avoid both automatic cancellation of the entire day and a blanket demand to carry on. The person's established pattern, symptoms, and goals guide the choice.

## 6. What a setback means

### 6.1 A setback is worsening after improvement or stability

It might involve more frequent events, stronger warnings, longer recovery, more avoidance, or reduced skill use. Illness, poor sleep, pain, routine disruption, treatment changes, a demanding period, or no identifiable factor may accompany it.

### 6.2 A symptom increase cannot measure learning or effort

Fictional example: after three months without emergency visits, Devon develops influenza and has several familiar episodes in one week. That increase does not tell us how well Devon understood CBT, how hard Devon tried, whether the diagnosis was accepted, or what happened in any other part of life.

The correct message is not “start CBT again from page one.” If Devon wants to review the plan, the purpose is to support safety or a personally chosen goal—not to prove effort or regain credit for earlier work.

### 6.3 Do not call FND a steadily progressing disease

Symptoms can fluctuate, recur, change, remit, or persist. A recurrence tells us that symptoms recurred; it cannot measure effort, learning, acceptance, treatment participation, or personal progress. The language should preserve uncertainty and prompt review when needed.

## 7. A setback and a new medical problem are not the same

### 7.1 Familiar form, changed amount

More of the person's clinician-reviewed usual events may fit a setback, though the individual plan may still call for clinical contact. The reader can review recent conditions and restore the plan without assuming one cause.

### 7.2 New or substantially different form

A different movement, new focal deficit, significant injury, breathing concern, unusual duration or recovery, event during pregnancy, or other individualized warning sign should not automatically be attributed to FND. The booklet cannot give universal emergency thresholds. It should help the clinician and patient pre-fill them.

### 7.3 Avoid both extremes

Calling emergency services for every familiar uncomplicated event can create harm and dependency on emergency care. Dismissing every future event as “just functional” can miss illness. A personalized plan is the bridge between those risks.

## 8. Review without demanding a hidden trigger

### 8.1 Use a brief conditions review

Look at illness, sleep, pain, medication changes, activity load, practice, environment, support, and major life events. Ask what is different and adjustable. “Nothing obvious” is a complete answer.

### 8.2 Protect against retrospective blame

If the reader identifies conflict before one episode, do not write that conflict caused the setback. If practice decreased, do not conclude that motivation failed. Several conditions can change together, and symptoms can worsen despite excellent self-management.

## 9. Refresh one part rather than restarting everything

### 9.1 Understanding

The reader may reread the concise explanation that symptoms are real and involuntary and that fluctuation does not equal fabrication.

### 9.2 Skill

They may rehearse one previously usable onset or recovery skill while well, instead of cycling through every technique.

### 9.3 Activity

They may protect the smallest meaningful version of one valued activity, such as joining the first ten minutes of a regular call.

### 9.4 Support

They may restore one agreed response that became inconsistent during a frightening week.

The worksheet should ask the reader to choose one starting point. Cognitive overload is a treatment barrier, not a sign that more homework is needed.

## 10. Keep outcomes separate when seizures continue

### 10.1 Define the outcomes separately

Symptom remission means an identified symptom is absent over a stated period. Symptom improvement can mean fewer, shorter, less severe, or less disruptive events. Functional improvement means doing more or needing different support. Personal recovery may mean confidence, identity, purpose, or living a meaningful life alongside symptoms.

These outcomes overlap but are not interchangeable.

### 10.2 Use a multidimensional example

Kai still has two seizures each week. Episodes now usually occur in a safer position, injuries have stopped, recovery has fallen from a day to two hours, and Kai attends a weekly class with a response plan. The seizure frequency outcome is unchanged. Safety, recovery, and participation outcomes have improved. The booklet should allow Kai to value these gains and still want fewer seizures.

### 10.3 More participation can complicate counts

A person who leaves home more often may encounter more demanding contexts and temporarily record more episodes. This does not automatically mean treatment caused harm or failed. Separate frequency, exposure to life, severity, and burden so reviewers can interpret change honestly.

## 11. Continued care when improvement is limited

### 11.1 Non-remission is not non-compliance

When symptoms persist, review the diagnosis and event types, formulation, treatment fit and dose, accessibility, competing health problems, adverse effects, practical barriers, and whether the chosen outcome mattered to the person. “The patient has not accepted the diagnosis” should not be the automatic explanation.

### 11.2 Care still has worthwhile jobs

These include preventing injury, treating pain, sleep, mood, or other coexisting conditions, improving access and communication, supporting daily activity, reducing unnecessary emergency intervention, providing appropriate aids, and maintaining clinical follow-up.

### 11.3 Adaptation is not surrender

A mobility aid, personal assistance, reduced schedule, or recovery space may improve safety and participation. If rehabilitation goals also exist, they can be pursued alongside adaptation. Never imply that support must be withheld to make recovery happen.

## 12. Supporter wellbeing and boundaries

### 12.1 Supporters may also be frightened and exhausted

They need accurate information, rest, emotional support, and backup. A sustainable plan names who else can help and what falls outside the supporter's role.

### 12.2 A boundary is not a covert punishment

“I can sit with you for twenty minutes and then Pat will take over” is a planned boundary. Silently leaving during an event to discourage seizures is abandonment. The content must make the difference clear.

### 12.3 Consider unsafe relationships

A joint workbook assumes enough safety for collaboration. Where there is coercion, abuse, stalking, or severe conflict, the person should make the plan with a clinician or advocate. Do not send private formulation material home with an unsafe supporter.

## 13. How to illustrate the concepts

### 13.1 Use a four-moment strip

Show the same supporter changing tasks across immediate safety, familiar episode, recovery, and later review. This makes timing visible without depicting dramatic seizure movements.

### 13.2 Use dialogue panels with few words

Contrast several simultaneous commands with one agreed sentence, then show the later consent question. Do not label the quieter response “less attention”; label it “less overload, same care.”

### 13.3 Show progress as separate paths or dials

Frequency, injury, recovery, emergency use, activity, autonomy, and quality of life should move independently. Avoid a single arrow from sick to cured.

## 14. Common authoring errors

1. Implying that family attention causes seizures.
2. Teaching relatives to ignore, punish, reward, test, or force the person.
3. Using one emergency threshold for every reader and jurisdiction.
4. Analysing triggers during or immediately after an event.
5. Describing recurrence as “the disease progressing.”
6. Describing persistent symptoms as poor effort or refusal to recover.
7. Counting only seizures and hiding changes in injury, recovery, participation, and quality of life.
8. Making a family member solely responsible for clinical care.

## 15. Readiness check for the content creator

Before drafting, the creator should be able to explain:

1. Why immediate safety, familiar-episode response, early recovery, and later review need different actions.
2. How calm support differs from disbelief or ignoring.
3. Why consequences can influence a pattern without making seizures intentional.
4. How a consensual reduction in over-help differs from punishment or abandonment.
5. How to distinguish a familiar setback from a changed event requiring medical guidance.
6. Why recurrence cannot measure effort, learning, acceptance, treatment participation, or personal progress.
7. How continued care remains valuable when seizure remission does not occur.
8. What supporters need in order to remain supporters rather than unpaid therapists.
