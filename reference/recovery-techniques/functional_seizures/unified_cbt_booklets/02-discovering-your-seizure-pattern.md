# Booklet 2 Outline — Discovering Your Seizure Pattern

> **Reader-facing promise:** “Build a useful picture of what happens before, during, and after your episodes—without being told that you must have a trigger.”

**For the person creating this booklet:** Read the linked [in-depth content-creator guide](02-content-creator-guide-discovering-your-seizure-pattern.md) before drafting. It explains CBT formulation, warnings, triggers, maintaining patterns, limited monitoring, and no-warning routes through worked functional-seizure examples.

## 1. Purpose and limits

### 1.1 Main purpose

This booklet helps the reader create a compassionate working map of one established episode pattern. The map should identify information that could guide treatment: an earlier warning, an opportunity to get safe, a response that may be tested, a recovery need, an avoidance pattern, or a supporter action.

### 1.2 Why the word “pattern” is used

The booklet should not become a hunt for one emotional trigger or original cause. Functional seizures may be affected by several changing factors, and some episodes remain unpredictable. *Pattern* is broader and less blaming than *trigger*. It allows the reader to record body sensations, attention, expectations, surroundings, activity, sleep, pain, emotions, relationships, memories, and consequences without requiring any category.

### 1.3 What the map means

A connection on the map is a possibility to test, not proof that one event caused a seizure. The final map is a working hypothesis that may change with new information.

## 2. Learning outcomes

After reading the booklet, the reader should be able to:

1. describe one familiar episode using a before–during–after sequence;
2. separate observed facts from memories, reports, and interpretations;
3. notice a possible warning or treatment opportunity without constantly scanning the body;
4. distinguish factors that may precede an episode from patterns that keep fear, avoidance, or disability going afterward;
5. accept “no clear warning” or “no pattern found” as useful information; and
6. choose one small hypothesis to test rather than treating the diary as a final explanation.

## 3. Proposed page sequence

### 3.1 Page 1 — Cover and purpose

State that this booklet looks for useful clues, not blame and not a perfect explanation. Explain that the reader does not have to discover trauma, stress, or a hidden emotion in order to complete it successfully.

An illustration could combine a notebook and changing weather symbols. Include the words, “A clue can show what to test; it does not prove cause.” Avoid interrogation, crime-scene, or “solve the mystery” imagery.

### 3.2 Page 2 — Select the episode pattern safely

Ask the reader to name the familiar pattern this map applies to using the wording agreed with a clinician. If several event types occur, each requires its own map and medical plan. A substantially new event should not be added to the existing functional-seizure map merely because the person already has FND.

Explain why: mixing several event types can create a false pattern and may cause an epileptic or other medical event to be managed as functional.

Use separate folder tabs for “pattern A,” “pattern B,” and “new or uncertain—check.”

### 3.3 Page 3 — Why observe an episode?

Teach that monitoring has a purpose only if it improves understanding, safety, treatment selection, or recovery. The goal is not to produce the longest possible diary.

Use examples. One reader may discover a ten-second warning that creates time to sit safely. Another may find no warning but learn that recovery is shorter when the room is quiet. A third may discover that continuous body checking makes fear worse and decide to stop detailed monitoring.

Illustrate a before–during–after strip with several possible findings, showing that an opportunity point can occur before, during, or after the event.

### 3.4 Pages 4–5 — Build the episode timeline

Teach the reader to record four kinds of information separately:

1. what they directly noticed;
2. what another person observed;
3. what they remember or were later told about the event; and
4. what they wonder might be connected.

Explain that memory gaps and altered awareness are common reasons for uncertainty. A supporter observation is useful information but is not automatically more accurate, and it does not diagnose the event.

Use a fictional example. Before an event, “Lee remembers feeling warm.” A supporter reports, “Lee stopped answering for five seconds.” Lee wonders, “Was the warmth a warning?” The first two are observations from different viewpoints; the last is a hypothesis.

Design a two-level timeline. The top level records observations. The bottom level records questions or interpretations, using dotted rather than solid arrows.

### 3.5 Page 6 — Warnings, supporter-noticed signs, and no warning

Explain that a warning may be a body sensation, altered awareness, attention shift, thought, urge, movement change, or sign noticed by someone else. Some people have no usable warning. Others may have one but not remember it after the event.

All three routes must appear equal:

1. “I notice a possible warning.”
2. “Someone else notices a sign and I consent to an agreed cue.”
3. “No usable warning has been found.”

The no-warning route should lead toward background practice, safety, recovery, graded activity, and supporter planning—not to a dead end.

### 3.6 Pages 7–8 — Factors worth considering

Introduce categories one at a time and explain that none is compulsory.

1. **Body and health:** illness, sleep, pain, fatigue, hunger, medication changes, movement, breathing changes, and other conditions.
2. **Attention and expectations:** intense focus on sensations, fear of an event, or expecting loss of control.
3. **Emotions and memories:** anxiety, anger, sadness, excitement, trauma reminders, or no recognized emotion.
4. **Activity and surroundings:** resting, crowds, medical settings, conflict, sensory load, work, or transitions.
5. **What happens afterward:** recovery conditions, emergency responses, missed activities, reassurance, shame, or increased avoidance.

Use movable “weather tiles” around an episode timeline. The visual should show conditions that may combine, not dominoes implying one fixed causal chain. Include blank tiles and an “unknown” tile.

### 3.7 Page 9 — Beginning, precipitating, and maintaining are different

Teach three different questions:

1. “What was happening when this problem first began?”
2. “What sometimes occurs near an episode now?”
3. “What may be keeping fear, avoidance, disruption, or recovery difficulty going now?”

Use an example. A person's first seizure followed an infection, but current episodes sometimes occur when a familiar internal sensation is interpreted as dangerous. Immediate escape reduces fear briefly but increases avoidance over time. CBT can work with the current prediction and avoidance even if the original cause remains unknown.

Illustrate three cards rather than one chain. Use the labels “beginning,” “current pattern,” and “what may continue the difficulty.”

### 3.8 Page 10 — One possible fear-and-avoidance loop

Explain the loop in ordinary language. A feared prediction may increase monitoring and alarm. Leaving or avoiding a situation can bring immediate relief, which teaches the person to leave again next time. Life may gradually become smaller and confidence may fall. This does not mean avoidance caused the condition or that every reader has this pattern.

Use a fictional shopping example and show several exits from the loop: accurate safety advice, an onset skill, a smaller activity experiment, a supporter plan, or “this is not my pattern.”

### 3.9 Page 11 — Observe without living under surveillance

Set firm monitoring limits. Suggest mapping one to three representative episodes or using a short agreed period. Explain that the reader should reduce or stop monitoring if it increases body checking, rumination, fear, dissociation, sleep disruption, or episodes.

Offer alternatives: a one-line voice note, a supporter recording one neutral fact with consent, mapping only recovery, or completing the exercise with a clinician.

Illustrate a camera moving between “zoom in briefly” and “return to life.”

### 3.10 Pages 12–13 — Build one working formulation

Use the plain-language structure:

1. “In this situation…”
2. “I notice…”
3. “I predict or fear…”
4. “I tend to…”
5. “What happens next is…”
6. “One small change worth testing is…”

Every field must allow “unclear,” “varies,” or “not part of my pattern.” Explain that arrows mean “may be connected and worth testing,” not “causes.”

Provide one fictional completed map and one blank map. The example should demonstrate uncertainty and more than one possible treatment point.

### 3.11 Page 14 — Choose an opportunity point

The first useful target may occur:

1. hours or minutes before an event;
2. at a warning;
3. in the immediate safety response;
4. during recovery;
5. in supporter behaviour; or
6. during return to an avoided activity.

Link warning and onset targets to Booklet 3, activity and avoidance targets to Booklet 4, and recovery or supporter targets to Booklet 5.

### 3.12 Page 15 — Decide what to share

Help the reader create a short clinical summary while keeping private formulation material private. Provide checkboxes for “for me,” “share with clinician,” “share with supporter,” and “share only in an emergency plan.”

Explain that identifying an emotional or trauma-related factor does not require sharing details with family, employers, schools, or emergency responders.

### 3.13 Page 16 — Summary and next step

Record the episode pattern, one working hypothesis, the monitoring stop rule, the selected opportunity point, and the next booklet or clinical conversation. Design the page so it can join a personal FND handbook.

## 4. Worksheets to develop

### 4.1 One-episode factual record

Separate time, direct observation, supporter observation, uncertainty, and later interpretation. Ask for only a small number of representative episodes. Do not prompt graphic trauma disclosure.

### 4.2 Pattern possibilities sheet

Use the heading “Factors worth discussing,” not “My triggers.” Include “none noticed,” “varies,” and “I prefer not to answer.”

### 4.3 Working formulation

End with one small test and one outcome to observe. The worksheet must visibly explain that it is a changeable hypothesis, not a final cause diagram.

## 5. Lower-demand and professionally supported versions

1. Map an ordinary episode rather than the worst episode.
2. Use icons, checkboxes, or a voice note instead of paragraphs.
3. Ask a trusted person for one neutral observation, with consent.
4. Focus on recovery or activity if warning-focused attention is unhelpful.
5. Complete the map with a clinician when memory gaps, trauma material, severe dissociation, mixed event types, or heightened monitoring make self-mapping unreliable or distressing.

## 6. Relationship to the online course

The course fully explains [causes and honest uncertainty](../../../../course/part-1-understanding-fnd/module-03-causes-mechanisms-and-honest-uncertainty/README.md) and [general personal symptom mapping](../../../../course/part-1-understanding-fnd/module-04-mapping-your-individual-condition/README.md).

This booklet repeats that no single cause is required, then adds a functional-seizure timeline, warning/no-warning routes, a monitoring stop rule, and a treatment opportunity point.

## 7. Evidence and safety requirements

1. Explain that formulation and diaries are included in seizure-focused CBT packages but have not generally been proven as isolated treatments.
2. Do not label body sensations as panic or emotions as hidden causes unless that understanding fits the individual and remains a hypothesis.
3. Do not treat supporter observations as diagnosis or permission for continuous supervision.
4. Keep trauma exploration optional and direct detailed trauma work to a suitably trained professional.
5. Do not use diary findings to change medication or emergency guidance without the appropriate clinician.

## 8. Questions for reviewers

1. Can a person with no warning complete the booklet without feeling that treatment is unavailable to them?
2. Does every visual distinguish observation, interpretation, and uncertainty?
3. Is the risk of hypervigilance explained next to the monitoring exercise?
4. Does discussion of what happens after an episode avoid suggesting that care or support rewards seizures?
5. Are health factors and coexisting conditions visible without inviting self-diagnosis?
