# Booklet 2 Outline — Discovering Your Seizure Pattern

> **Reader-facing promise:** “Build a useful picture of what happens before, during, and after your episodes—without being told that you must have a trigger.”

**Proposed length:** 14–16 pages. **Main job:** turn observation into a limited, compassionate personal formulation that points toward a safe place to intervene. “Pattern” is used instead of “trigger” because some episodes are unpredictable and many influences are not single causes.

## What the reader should be able to do afterward

- describe one established episode using a before–during–after map;
- notice possible warnings or opportunity points without constantly scanning the body;
- identify several categories that may affect episodes while accepting “not known” as a real result;
- distinguish a precipitating event from factors that keep fear, avoidance, or disability going; and
- choose one small hypothesis to test rather than treating a diary as proof of cause.

| 11. Attention without surveillance | Use short, scheduled observation periods. Stop the diary if it increases body checking, fear, rumination, or episodes. | Detailed monitoring can itself be burdensome or amplifying. | A kitchen timer icon and a “zoom in / zoom out” camera control. |

## Worksheets to create

### One-episode factual record

Use four columns: time; what I directly noticed; what another person observed; and what remains uncertain. Ask for only one to three representative episodes rather than indefinite daily monitoring. Do not ask for graphic trauma detail.

### Pattern possibilities

Use checkboxes titled “worth discussing,” not “my triggers.” Include health factors, sensations, attention, expectations, emotions, activities, places, social context, and after-effects. Provide “none noticed,” “varies,” and “I prefer not to answer.”

### My working map

The worksheet must show that an arrow means “may be linked and can be tested,” not “this caused my seizure.” It ends with one small experiment and one measurement.

## Lower-demand options

- Choose one ordinary episode rather than the worst episode.
- Ask a trusted observer for one neutral fact only, with consent.
- Use icons or voice notes instead of paragraphs.
- Map recovery or activity if warning-focused attention is unhelpful.
- Complete the worksheet with a clinician if memory gaps, trauma material, severe dissociation, or conflicting episode types make self-mapping distressing or unreliable.

## Evidence and safety notes for the author

- Pattern mapping and formulation are common parts of seizure-focused CBT packages, including CODES and *Taking Control*. This does not prove that diaries independently reduce seizures.
- Avoid stating that body sensations are panic, that emotions are hidden, or that a seizure provides relief unless the reader recognizes that pattern themselves.
- A supporter observation is information, not a diagnosis and not permission to supervise the person continuously.
- Trauma exploration is optional and usually belongs with a suitably trained professional; the booklet should never prompt detailed exposure or disclosure.
- Diary data should not be used to change medication or emergency rules without the relevant clinician.

## Reviewer questions

- Can someone with no warning complete the booklet without feeling excluded?
- Does every diagram distinguish observation, interpretation, and uncertainty?
- Could the diary increase hypervigilance, and is the stop rule prominent enough?
- Does “what happens afterward” avoid implying that care or support rewards seizures?
- Are coexisting epilepsy, pain, sleep, medication, and other health factors visible without encouraging self-diagnosis?
