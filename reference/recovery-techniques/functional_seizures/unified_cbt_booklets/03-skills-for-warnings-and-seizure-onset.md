# Booklet 3 Outline — Skills for Warnings and Seizure Onset

> **Reader-facing promise:** “Choose, practise, and test a small number of safe skills for your own established warning pattern. If you have no warning, this booklet still offers useful routes.”

**For the person creating this booklet:** Read the linked [in-depth content-creator guide](03-content-creator-guide-skills-for-warnings-and-seizure-onset.md) before drafting. It explains why practice, attention-shifting, grounding, competing responses, breathing, cue words, and behavioural experiments are used—and why none should be presented as a test of effort or a guaranteed way to stop a seizure.

## 1. Purpose and limits

### 1.1 Main purpose

This booklet turns the opportunity point from Booklet 2 into a carefully monitored skill experiment. It teaches the reader how to select a small number of strategies, practise them while well, try them only in suitable situations, and decide whether to continue, adapt, or omit them.

### 1.2 What the booklet cannot promise

There is no universal seizure-interruption technique. A skill may help one person notice more, remain safer, retain awareness, reduce severity, or recover more quickly without preventing every seizure. A skill that does not help is information about fit, timing, or delivery—not evidence that the person failed.

### 1.3 Safety priority

Physical safety and the individualized episode plan always come before a CBT skill. These strategies do not treat epilepsy, diagnose an event, or replace emergency action for a new or changed event.

## 2. Learning outcomes

After reading the booklet, the reader should be able to:

1. choose one or two skills that match their own opportunity point;
2. practise a skill while well before trying to use it during a warning;
3. explain the intended purpose and possible downside of the selected skill;
4. use a simple prediction–test–observe–decide process;
5. measure usability, safety, awareness, severity, recovery, and return to activity rather than only seizure prevention; and
6. follow a useful no-warning route.

## 3. Proposed page sequence

### 3.1 Page 1 — Cover and learning frame

State, “A skill is an experiment, not a test of willpower.” Explain that the reader is looking for a strategy that fits their pattern, not trying to collect or master every available technique.

Illustrate a toolbox with only two open spaces. This shows that selecting a small number of tools is the goal.

### 3.2 Page 2 — Safety comes before technique

Teach the order clearly:

1. move toward the safest available position if possible;
2. follow the individualized episode or emergency plan; and
3. use the optional CBT-informed skill only when it is appropriate and does not delay needed care.

Explain that a technique should not require unsafe standing, movement near hazards, forced breathing, painful stimulation, or a supporter physically controlling the person.

Use a three-step illustrated card with the safety action visibly first.

### 3.3 Page 3 — Choose the correct route

Offer four routes:

1. the person notices a usable warning;
2. a supporter notices an agreed sign and the person has consented to a cue;
3. there is no usable warning; or
4. attempts to focus on warning signs increase symptoms or distress.

The third and fourth routes should lead to background rehearsal, recovery planning, supporter response, and activity work. They must not appear inferior.

### 3.4 Page 4 — Learn and rehearse before using a skill at onset

Explain that a response is easier to use under pressure when its steps are already familiar. Begin in an ordinary moment, in a safe position, for a short period. Check whether the skill itself causes dizziness, distress, pain, breath discomfort, dissociation, or intense body monitoring.

Use three panels: “learn the steps,” “rehearse briefly while well,” and “test in the agreed situation.” Add a dimmer image showing that practice difficulty increases gradually.

### 3.5 Page 5 — Orientation to the present

Teach a short orienting sequence: name the place or activity, state a current fact, and identify several neutral details in the environment. If attention to the body is frightening, keep the anchors outside the body.

Explain the CBT purpose. The skill may widen attention when it has narrowed around a sensation, frightening prediction, or dissociative experience. It is not a command to “snap out of” an involuntary event.

Use a room illustration with neutral features such as a blue door, a square window, and the pressure of a chair. Include exact sample language the reader can adapt.

### 3.6 Page 6 — Focused attention task

Offer several tasks of different cognitive demand: reading visible words, describing a picture, sorting objects, naming items in a category, counting in a chosen pattern, or using a brief game. The task must be safe and demanding enough to hold attention without being impossible.

Explain that the purpose is not denial or “thinking positively.” It is a planned change in where attention is directed during a familiar warning.

Use individual tool cards arranged from lower to higher cognitive demand. Readers should select one, not perform the whole list.

### 3.7 Page 7 — Sensory grounding

Teach the reader to choose one safe, neutral sensation and describe it precisely: texture, ordinary coolness, colour, sound, or contact with a chair. Avoid extreme cold, pain, startling, direct ice on skin, or face immersion.

Explain that grounding may support present-moment orientation for some people. It appears in clinical and community resources, but direct evidence that this component independently prevents functional seizures is limited.

Illustrate a textured cloth, a cool container held safely, and a patterned object. Pair each image with a specific descriptive phrase and a safety icon.

### 3.8 Page 8 — Planned competing response

Explain a competing response as a small, preselected action that is different from the person's earliest functional pattern and supports continued voluntary action. It might involve keeping the eyes on a target, continuing a simple purposeful hand task, gently pressing the feet into the floor while seated, or another response chosen with a clinician.

Use a fictional example. At a familiar warning, Sam's gaze drops and both hands become still. Sam practises keeping the eyes on a chosen object while slowly moving one thumb across each fingertip. The point is not to overpower the seizure. It is to test whether a rehearsed voluntary pattern changes the next part of the event.

Make clear that the action must match the individual and may require professional guidance, especially when falls, weakness, pain, abnormal postures, or mixed event types occur.

### 3.9 Page 9 — Breathing or settling as an optional subgroup tool

If breathing changes are part of the person's assessed warning pattern, describe a gentle, comfortable rhythm or relaxed exhalation taught or reviewed by a clinician. Say explicitly that the reader should not take repeated deep breaths or force a slow rate.

Explain that breathing work may be relevant to some patterns but can increase dizziness, air hunger, panic, or symptoms. It is not the default tool for everyone.

Use a small feather moving on a comfortable exhale and the caption, “comfortable, not deep or forced.” Place the stop rule beside the illustration.

### 3.10 Page 10 — Cue words and imagery

Teach how one brief phrase or image can compress a multi-step plan. Examples include “safe—orient—continue,” imagining a path widening, or picturing the feet connecting with the floor.

Explain that the cue helps memory and directs the planned response. It is not magic, positive thinking, or proof that imagery independently treats seizures.

Avoid imagery of fighting the brain, forcing control, broken machinery, or suppressing emotion. Provide original cue-card examples and a blank card.

### 3.11 Page 11 — Build a plan with no more than two skills

Use an if–then form: “If I notice ___, I first get safe. I then try ___ for ___. If it is not usable, I try or do ___. I follow my medical plan when ___.”

Explain why the sequence is short: a long menu increases memory load and makes it impossible to know which component affected the result.

### 3.12 Pages 12–13 — Run a behavioural experiment

Teach four steps:

1. **Predict:** What does the person expect to happen, and what would count as a useful change?
2. **Test:** Use the selected skill in the agreed safe context.
3. **Observe:** Record what actually happened without grading effort.
4. **Decide:** Continue, adapt, seek help, or omit the skill.

Use a worked example. Priya predicts, “Once the warning begins, nothing I do can affect any part of the event.” During three safe trials, orientation does not prevent every episode, but twice she remains aware longer and recovers more quickly. The conclusion is not “the technique works” or “the seizure was voluntary.” The conclusion is that orientation may affect part of Priya's established pattern and is worth refining.

Measure ease of remembering, distress, warning length, awareness, injury, severity, recovery, and return to activity.

### 3.13 Page 14 — The no-warning route

Explain that no-warning treatment can focus on:

1. practising orientation during ordinary moments without continuous symptom searching;
2. improving environmental and injury safety;
3. developing a calm supporter response;
4. making recovery more predictable;
5. reducing avoidance and returning to valued activity; and
6. reviewing with a clinician whether a subtle opportunity point becomes apparent over time.

Use a wide timeline showing possible treatment points in the hours before an event, the event response, recovery, and return to activity.

### 3.14 Page 15 — Continue, adapt, or stop

Teach the reader to stop and review a skill if it increases dizziness, breath difficulty, pain, falls, panic, dissociation, body monitoring, or symptom severity. A skill may also be omitted simply because it is impractical or does not fit the person's goals.

Use three distinct signs labelled “continue,” “adapt/check,” and “stop/seek guidance.” Do not rely on colour alone.

### 3.15 Page 16 — Personal skill card

The final card should contain the familiar warning or opportunity point, first safety action, primary skill, optional alternative, stop rule, and individualized emergency threshold. The emergency threshold must be copied from an existing plan or added with a clinician—not invented by the booklet.

Provide both a low-ink printable card and instructions for creating an accessible phone version.

## 4. Worksheets to develop

### 4.1 Skill selector

For each option, ask: “What is this intended to change?”, “Does it fit my pattern?”, “Where can I practise safely?”, “What could make it unhelpful?”, “Who can help?”, and “try, adapt, or omit?” Do not create a total score.

### 4.2 Short skill experiment

Record practice while well separately from use at a warning. Limit the experiment to a manageable number of trials and specify the review point.

### 4.3 Pocket plan

Use no more than five short lines. Large type and simple icons should make it usable during cognitive difficulty.

## 5. Relationship to the online course

Repeat the minimum episode-safety information from [Module 6](../../../../course/part-2-safety-and-symptoms/module-06-functional-seizures-and-episodic-symptoms/README.md) and the general explanation of practice from [rehabilitation and neuroplastic change](../../../../course/part-4-treatment-and-rehabilitation/module-14-rehabilitation-and-neuroplastic-change/README.md).

This booklet adds seizure-specific choice routes, original skill instructions, a monitored behavioural experiment, and a complete no-warning route.

## 6. Evidence and safety requirements

1. State that grounding, attention-shifting, relaxation, imagery, and related techniques were often included in treatment packages but usually were not isolated in trials.
2. Use the CODES process evaluation to support choice and monitoring. It reported that different strategies suited different people and that breathing made some participants dizzy.
3. Keep ReACT's competing-response principle within its promising paediatric evidence boundary.
4. Exclude painful stimuli, restraint, forced breathing, extreme cold, and unsafe standing or movement.
5. Require advance consent for any supporter cue and prevent it from becoming surveillance, pressure, or repeated commands.

## 7. Questions for reviewers

1. Is safety visibly first on every route?
2. Does the design encourage one or two selected tools rather than an overwhelming list?
3. Can a reader with no warning find a complete and useful path?
4. Is the possible harm or stop rule beside each relevant skill?
5. Does every example avoid suggesting that a person can stop seizures if they simply try hard enough?
