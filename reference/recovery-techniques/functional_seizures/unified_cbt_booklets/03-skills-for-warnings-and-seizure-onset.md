# Booklet 3 Outline — Skills for Warnings and Seizure Onset

> **Reader-facing promise:** “Choose, practise, and test a small number of safe skills for your own established warning pattern. If you have no warning, this booklet still offers useful routes.”

**Proposed length:** 14–16 pages. **Main job:** turn possible treatment components into a cautious personal experiment. The booklet teaches a menu, not one universal seizure-stopping method.

## What the reader should be able to do afterward

- choose one or two skills that match the opportunity point found in Booklet 2;
- practise a skill while well before attempting it at a warning;
- use an orienting, attention, sensory, movement, or breathing option safely;
- record whether the skill was usable and what changed, without defining success only as stopping a seizure; and
- follow a no-warning route based on recovery, supporter response, and background practice.

## Page-by-page teaching plan

| Page or spread | What is taught in user-friendly language | Why it is included / why it may help | Imagery, illustration, or activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Cover | “A skill is an experiment, not a test of willpower.” | Sets a non-blaming learning frame. | A small toolbox with only two open slots, emphasizing selection rather than collecting every tool. |
| 2. Safety before technique | Get physically safe first and follow the personal episode plan. These tools are not for epilepsy, a first event, or a changed event, and must not delay emergency action. | Separates symptom-management practice from medical response. | A clear order card: “safe position → personal plan → optional skill.” |
| 3. Find your route | Choose: a usable warning; a sign noticed by a supporter with consent; no warning; or skills that increase symptoms. Each route leads somewhere. | Prevents the common error of presenting warning interruption as the only form of progress. | Four-path decision tree. The no-warning path leads to background rehearsal, recovery, and Booklets 4–5. |
| 4. Practise when well | Learn the steps in a calm or ordinary moment, in a safe position, for a short period. Then make the task slightly more realistic if appropriate. | Repetition can make a response easier to access under time pressure and reveals adverse effects before a warning. | Three comic panels: learn, rehearse, test. A dimmer shows gradual difficulty. |
| 5. Orient to now | Name where you are, the date or task, and three neutral facts you can see or feel. Keep the task external if body attention is alarming. | Orientation may interrupt narrowing attention or dissociation for some people. It is not a command to “snap out of it.” | A room scene with three numbered neutral anchors and sample words: “blue door, firm chair, window light.” |
| 6. Focused attention task | Count backward, read signs, sort objects, describe a picture, or use a brief word/game task that is demanding enough to hold attention but still safe. | Attention-shifting was included in seizure-focused treatment programmes and may create a competing focus during a warning. | Five tool cards graded from low to higher cognitive demand. No copyrighted game interface. |
| 7. Sensory grounding | Choose one safe, non-painful texture, temperature, sound, or visual detail; describe it precisely. Avoid extreme cold, pain, face immersion, or startling. | Sensory grounding is used in clinical and community resources and may support present-moment orientation; direct seizure-specific component evidence is limited. | Close-up drawings of a textured fabric, cool drink container held through cloth, and a patterned object, each with a safety icon. |
| 8. Planned competing action | With professional help where needed, choose an action incompatible with the person's early pattern: press feet gently into the floor, keep eyes on a target, continue a simple purposeful movement, or use another individualized response. | Habit-reversal and retraining approaches aim to strengthen an alternative response and sense of control. Suitability depends on the episode pattern and physical safety. | Split-screen fictional example: warning pattern on one side, planned safe action on the other. Avoid implying physical force can overpower a seizure. |
| 9. Breathing or settling—optional | If breathing changes are part of the person's assessed pattern, try a gentle, comfortable exhale-focused rhythm taught or reviewed by a clinician. Stop if dizzy, air-hungry, faint, or more distressed. | Breathing work may help a subgroup; it can also worsen symptoms and is not a universal calming instruction. | A feather moving slightly on an exhale, plus a prominent “comfortable—not deep or forced” caption. |
| 10. Helpful imagery and cue words | Rehearse one brief image or phrase linked to the chosen action: a path widening, feet connecting to the floor, or “notice—orient—continue.” Avoid imagery of fighting, suppressing, or broken machinery. | A simple cue can reduce the memory load of a multi-step skill. Imagery itself should be treated as an aid, not a proven mechanism. | Three original cue-card examples and a blank card. |
| 11. Combine no more than two tools | Write an if–then plan: “If I notice ___, first I get safe, then I try ___ for ___, and I follow my plan if ___.” | A short sequence is easier to remember and evaluate than a long list used at once. | Pocket-card layout with large type and symbols. |
| 12–13. Test and record | Record usability, warning length, awareness, severity, injury, recovery, and activity resumed. “No change” and “made things worse” are valid results. | Personal testing identifies fit and prevents post-hoc claims that every attempt worked. | A simple experiment card: predict, try, observe, decide. Use checkboxes rather than a dense diary. |
| 14. If there is no warning | Practise general orientation at neutral times; improve the supporter and recovery plan; work on avoidance and valued activity; review whether subtle warnings emerge without searching continuously. | Treatment opportunities exist before and after the immediate onset window. | A wider timeline showing “days/hours before,” “episode,” “recovery,” and “return to life,” with several non-onset entry points. |
| 15. When to adapt or stop | Stop a skill that increases symptoms, pain, dizziness, panic, falls, breath difficulty, or monitoring. Ask for professional help if episodes are mixed, high-risk, traumatic, or hard to distinguish. | Makes adverse effects and lack of fit expected data rather than failure. | Three signs: continue, adapt, stop/check. Each uses text and shape, not colour alone. |
| 16. Personal skill card and sources | Finalize one primary skill, one alternative, safety conditions, and who knows the plan. | Creates a portable result rather than a one-time reading exercise. | Low-ink cut-out card plus phone-lock-screen mock-up to recreate independently. |

## Worksheets to create

### Skill selector

Rows: orienting, attention task, sensory grounding, competing action, and optional breathing/settling. Columns: “fits my pattern?”, “safe place to practise,” “possible downside,” “who can help,” and “try / adapt / omit.” Do not include a total score.

### One-week skill experiment

Use a small number of trials. Record practice while well separately from use at a warning. Outcomes include ease of remembering, distress, warning/awareness, event and recovery changes, and return to activity.

### My pocket plan

Maximum five lines: familiar warning; first safety action; primary skill; alternative; personal emergency threshold. The emergency threshold should be copied from the person's plan, not invented by the booklet.

## Evidence and safety notes for the author

- Make clear that grounding, attention-shifting, relaxation, imagery, and other techniques were commonly included in treatment packages; most were not isolated in trials.
- The CODES process evaluation reported that different strategies suited different people; some participants experienced dizziness with breathing work. Use this to justify choice and monitoring, not to reject all breathing approaches.
- ReACT's competing-response model is promising in paediatric evidence. Do not use its result to promise adult seizure control.
- Avoid painful stimuli, ice directly on skin, forced breathing, restraint, commands, or instructions that require unsafe standing or movement.
- A supporter cue must be agreed in advance. It should not become surveillance or pressure during an episode.

## Reviewer questions

- Is physical safety visibly first on every onset route?
- Does the layout encourage choosing two tools rather than trying everything?
- Can readers with no warning find a useful, non-inferior path?
- Are potential adverse effects and stop rules next to the relevant skill?
- Does the wording distinguish “may create an opportunity” from “you can control seizures if you try”?
