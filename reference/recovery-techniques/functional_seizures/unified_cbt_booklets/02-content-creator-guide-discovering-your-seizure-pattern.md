# Content-Creator Guide 2 — Discovering Your Seizure Pattern

This guide teaches the ideas behind [Booklet 2: Discovering Your Seizure Pattern](02-discovering-your-seizure-pattern.md). Its purpose is to help an inexperienced content creator teach observation and formulation without turning the reader into a full-time symptom detective or inventing a psychological cause.

All examples are fictional composites. A real formulation belongs to the person and should be reviewed with an appropriately trained clinician.

## 1. What “discovering a pattern” means

### 1.1 A pattern is a repeated relationship, not necessarily a trigger

A reader may notice that episodes often follow pain, occur in bright crowded places, begin with derealization, or require long recovery. These are useful repeated relationships. They do not by themselves prove that pain, light, crowds, or dissociation caused the disorder.

The word “trigger” is often used as if one event presses a button and mechanically causes a seizure. Functional-seizure patterns are usually more complex. A crowded place might matter only when the person is also fatigued and already monitoring a warning sensation. On another day it may not matter at all. The booklet should prefer “factor,” “condition,” “warning,” “possible link,” or “what was happening around the episode” until enough evidence exists.

### 1.2 The aim is to find a reachable point, not a perfect explanation

The reader does not need to solve their entire history before trying a skill or improving daily life. A modest finding such as “I sometimes have thirty seconds of narrowing vision before a familiar episode” can justify safely testing an onset plan. “No warning that I can detect” is also useful because it redirects work toward safety, recovery, supporter response, and participation.

## 2. Begin with observations before interpretations

### 2.1 Teach the difference explicitly

An observation is something the person or another witness noticed: “My hearing sounded far away,” “I sat down,” “My partner saw my head turn,” or “I needed two hours before I could read.” An interpretation adds meaning: “My brain was shutting down,” “I panicked,” “I failed to stop it,” or “The noise triggered me.” Interpretations may be important, but mixing them with observations makes the map appear more certain than it is.

Use a fictional example:

1. Observation: “At 2:10 p.m. I noticed tingling around my mouth and the room seemed distant.”
2. Observation: “I thought, ‘This is starting.’” The thought itself is observable; whether it was accurate is a later question.
3. Observation: “I called my partner and lay down.”
4. Observation from partner: “Speech became quiet and the episode began about a minute later.”
5. Interpretation to test: “Calling for help made the seizure happen.”

The record supports a possible warning period. It does not yet support the final interpretation.

### 2.2 Organize one event into before, during, and after

“Before” should include only a limited window chosen in advance, such as the hour before and relevant background conditions that day. If the worksheet asks about the entire week, almost anything can be made to look causal.

“During” describes the person's awareness and any witness observations without demanding recall that may be unavailable. “After” includes physical symptoms, emotional effects, help received, activities cancelled, recovery time, injuries, and medical care. This reveals burden and possible treatment targets beyond episode count.

## 3. Warnings are opportunities, not proof of control

### 3.1 A warning can be bodily, attentional, emotional, cognitive, or observed by someone else

Examples include rising nausea, altered sound, visual narrowing, shaking, derealization, a sudden image, a fear surge, slowed speech, or a supporter noticing stillness. Avoid sorting these into “physical” and “psychological” as if one group is more real.

### 3.2 Some people have no usable warning

The booklet must make this an equal route, not a failed worksheet. The person may have no warning, may recognize one only after the fact, or may not be able to act safely during it. A no-warning route focuses on reducing injury, preparing supporters, improving post-episode recovery, reviewing activity restrictions, and measuring broader outcomes.

### 3.3 Do not train continuous internal surveillance

Checking the body every few seconds may increase fear and make sensations dominate attention. Use short, scheduled reviews of a small sample of episodes. Stop or simplify tracking when it increases distress, dissociation, compulsive checking, or symptom focus.

## 4. Teach the difference among beginning, precipitating, and maintaining

### 4.1 Beginning describes the broader onset story

The first episodes may have appeared during infection, injury, panic, bereavement, medication change, prolonged strain, or no identifiable event. The booklet should allow “unknown.” It should also avoid implying that whatever happened near onset necessarily explains every current episode.

### 4.2 A precipitating factor is close to a particular episode

For one familiar episode, relevant nearby conditions might include lack of sleep, pain, standing in heat, sensory overload, conflict, a threatening memory, or noticing a warning. The same factor may be present without a seizure, so it is more accurate to say it “may increase the chance” than “causes” the event.

### 4.3 A maintaining process helps a current problem continue

A maintaining process can occur after the disorder has begun. Fear of warnings may produce constant monitoring; avoiding all public places may reduce opportunities to learn; repeated emergency treatment for a familiar uncomplicated pattern may increase alarm; or an inaccessible workplace may keep participation impossible. These examples are not universal and do not imply intention.

### 4.4 A protective factor helps or creates options

Accurate diagnosis, a calm response plan, sleep treatment, an onset skill, a trusted clinician, accessible transport, predictable routines, and valued activity may all be protective. A formulation that contains only vulnerabilities and problems will feel like a list of personal defects. Always map strengths, resources, and exceptions.

## 5. How to explain a fear-and-avoidance loop without blame

### 5.1 Immediate relief is powerful learning

Suppose Lee has a seizure on a bus. The next week, seeing the bus produces fear and Lee takes a taxi. Fear falls immediately. The nervous system learns, “Escaping the bus brought relief,” even though the taxi is unaffordable and Lee now feels less able to travel.

In behavioural language this is sometimes called negative reinforcement: removing an unpleasant feeling makes the preceding action more likely. Do not use the term in the reader booklet unless it is carefully defined; many people hear “reinforcement” as reward or deliberate manipulation.

### 5.2 Avoidance is often understandable and may begin as safety

Lee may initially have been medically advised not to travel alone, may have been injured, or may lack a reliable response plan. The author must not decide that every avoided activity is excessive. The CBT question is: “What does this response protect against, what does it cost, and is there a safer or more flexible version?”

### 5.3 The loop does not explain every seizure

It may explain why a bus has become frightening and life has narrowed. It does not establish why Lee developed functional seizures. The visual should include environmental and safety exits, not only cognitive ones.

## 6. How to build a working formulation

### 6.1 Use tentative language

A good formulation sounds like:

> “When Noor is physically depleted and notices the familiar unreal feeling, the prediction of collapsing makes attention narrow and alarm rise. Noor leaves immediately and needs a family member to finish the task. Leaving brings short-term relief but may reduce confidence and practice. This does not explain every seizure. We could test an orientation skill during a low-risk warning and review the result.”

A poor formulation sounds like:

> “Noor's unresolved stress causes seizures, which are maintained by family attention.”

The first is specific, supported by observations, revisable, and connected to an experiment. The second asserts hidden causes, stigmatizes family care, and leaves no room for uncertainty.

### 6.2 Put facts, hypotheses, and unknowns in different places

The visual map should let the reader mark:

1. “I have observed this more than once.”
2. “This might be connected.”
3. “I do not know yet.”
4. “This does not fit me.”

This is more honest than a completed flowchart that appears clinician-approved before review.

### 6.3 Include exceptions

Ask, “When was this factor present without an episode?” and “When did an episode occur without it?” Exceptions prevent the map from becoming confirmation bias. They may identify another condition: perhaps noise matters only with fatigue, or the warning sometimes passes when the person is absorbed in conversation.

### 6.4 Formulate one pattern at a time

A person may have more than one event type. Mixing them can produce a meaningless average. The booklet must first ask which clinician-reviewed familiar event is being mapped and direct the reader to seek guidance for new or substantially different episodes.

## 7. Trauma and difficult experiences

### 7.1 Trauma is relevant for some people, not required for FND

Trauma, abuse, loss, and chronic threat can affect the nervous system and may be important in an individual's formulation. Many people with functional seizures do not identify trauma, and no reader should be pressured to search for or disclose it to make the diagnosis legitimate.

### 7.2 A general booklet should not perform trauma processing

The worksheet can offer “past or current threat that I choose to discuss with a professional” as an optional category. It should not ask for detailed memories, encourage exposure to traumatic material, or invite supporters to investigate the person's past. If observation evokes flashbacks, marked dissociation, self-harm risk, or overwhelming distress, the appropriate next step is professional support, not more detailed self-monitoring.

## 8. Turn the formulation into one fair experiment

### 8.1 Choose the smallest informative question

If the working map suggests that a familiar warning is followed by narrowed attention, the question might be: “When I notice the warning while already seated safely, can I orient to three external details for thirty seconds?” The outcome could include whether the skill was usable, whether awareness changed, what happened to the warning, and what happened next.

### 8.2 Do not define only “no seizure” as success

The skill may be remembered but too difficult, may extend the warning, may reduce distress without changing the episode, or may be unhelpful. Each result informs the map. If the person cannot use the skill during onset, that may justify a supporter cue, more rehearsal, a different skill, or the no-warning route.

### 8.3 Use an explicit learning cycle

Teach: predict, try safely, observe, compare, and decide. The decision can be continue, adapt, stop, or seek advice. This is the core bridge from Booklet 2 into Booklet 3.

## 9. How to design the pages and illustrations

### 9.1 Show a messy map becoming simpler

Begin with many possible factors on loose cards. Then show the reader choosing only repeated or plausible items and marking uncertainty. This teaches that formulation is selection and revision, not collecting every bad event in a life story.

### 9.2 Use a before–during–after strip with two viewpoints

Where useful, place “what I noticed” above and “what my supporter observed” below. Neither viewpoint outranks the other. Include “not known” when memory or observation is absent.

### 9.3 Build an off-ramp from tracking

Every monitoring page should tell the reader when to stop: enough information has been gathered; it is increasing distress; the event is new; or the task should be completed with a clinician. This prevents the booklet from silently prescribing hypervigilance.

## 10. Readiness check for the content creator

Before drafting, the creator should be able to explain:

1. Why a repeated pattern is not automatically a cause.
2. How an observation differs from an interpretation.
3. Why onset, precipitating conditions, maintaining loops, and protective factors are not interchangeable.
4. How immediate relief can maintain avoidance without making the person responsible for the seizure.
5. Why “no warning” is a useful clinical route rather than failure.
6. How one tentative formulation leads to one safe, measurable experiment.
7. When monitoring or trauma-related reflection should stop and move into professional care.
