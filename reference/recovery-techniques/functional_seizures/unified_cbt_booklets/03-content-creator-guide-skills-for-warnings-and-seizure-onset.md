# Content-Creator Guide 3 — Skills for Warnings and Seizure Onset

This guide teaches the ideas behind [Booklet 3: Skills for Warnings and Seizure Onset](03-skills-for-warnings-and-seizure-onset.md). It is for a content creator who needs to understand why an onset skill is used, how it differs from generic relaxation, and how to present practice without implying that a person should be able to stop every seizure.

All people and examples are fictional composites. Skills must be tested within an individualized safety and clinical plan.

## 1. The basic treatment idea

### 1.1 A warning may create a brief opportunity point

Some people notice a familiar change before loss of awareness or control: derealization, visual narrowing, altered sound, tingling, rising sensations, fear, shaking, or another repeatable sign. If the person can still act safely, a short practised response may alter attention, arousal, action, or the learned sequence.

This is an opportunity, not proof of voluntary control. A warning may be too short, awareness may already be impaired, or the skill may not change the episode. The author should never say, “You can stop the seizure if you catch it early enough.” A better message is, “A familiar warning may give some people a moment to test a planned response.”

### 1.2 The skill is an experiment rather than a command

The person chooses one or two skills that fit the working formulation, rehearses them when well, and tries them during a safe warning when possible. They observe what changed. “I remembered the first step” may be meaningful before any symptom change occurs.

## 2. Why rehearsal matters

### 2.1 Knowing a technique is not the same as retrieving it under load

During a warning, attention may narrow, language may become difficult, and the body may feel urgent or unreal. A paragraph of instructions learned yesterday may be inaccessible. Rehearsal turns the technique into a short sequence and helps identify whether it is tolerable before the critical moment.

Use an ordinary analogy carefully: practising where the emergency exit is does not cause the emergency or guarantee perfect action; it makes the next step easier to retrieve when cognitive load is high.

### 2.2 Rehearse in layers

First, learn the skill while calm and seated safely. Next, practise it during a mild ordinary sensation or distraction if appropriate. Then use it during a familiar warning only within the agreed plan. Do not deliberately provoke a seizure, hyperventilate, expose trauma, or recreate hazardous conditions through a general self-help booklet.

### 2.3 Keep instructions short

A skill card might say: “Feet supported. Name three blue things. Read the date and place aloud. Continue for thirty seconds.” It should not require the person to recall a theory lesson during onset.

## 3. Safety comes before technique

### 3.1 The first action may be physical protection

If a person is standing near traffic, heat, water, machinery, stairs, or another hazard, moving to the individualized safe position takes priority. The skill begins only if awareness and circumstances allow. A failed attempt to use a technique must never delay emergency action required by the person's plan.

### 3.2 New or substantially different events leave the workbook pathway

The skills are for a clinician-reviewed familiar pattern. A changed event, injury, unusual severity, breathing concern, pregnancy-related concern, or another individualized warning sign follows medical advice. The booklet must not train the reader or supporter to reinterpret every event as functional.

## 4. Attention-shifting: what it is and is not

### 4.1 The conceptual reason

In some formulations, attention becomes captured by internal sensations, threat, or a narrowing sense of disconnection. A focused external task may compete for attention and strengthen orientation to the current environment. This is not pretending the warning is absent; it is deliberately changing what the brain is doing during the available window.

### 4.2 A worked example

Priya's familiar warning is that sound becomes distant and the room looks tunnelled for about forty seconds. Once seated safely, Priya practises finding five rectangular objects and naming their colours. The task is specific enough to hold attention but simple enough to retrieve. After five trials, Priya reports that it was usable three times, once seemed to widen awareness, once did not change the episode, and once was remembered too late.

The correct lesson is not “rectangles stop seizures.” The lesson is that this kind of external selection may be feasible for Priya and deserves refinement. Another person may find visual searching overstimulating.

### 4.3 Distinguish it from avoidance or distraction

“Distract yourself” can sound dismissive and vague. Focused attention is a planned task selected for a hypothesized attentional process. It remains connected to observation: what did attention do, was the task possible, and what followed?

## 5. Orientation and sensory grounding

### 5.1 Orientation tells the brain where and when the person is

An orientation sequence may name the person's name, location, date, visible objects, and next intended action. It can be useful when the warning includes unreality, time confusion, or narrowing awareness. It should use current neutral information, not detailed questioning.

Example: “I am Alex. I am in the clinic waiting room. It is Tuesday morning. My feet are on the floor. The next thing I am doing is walking to the reception desk with Jo.”

### 5.2 Sensory grounding uses present, tolerable sensation

The person may press feet into the floor, notice the support of a chair, hold a textured object, or name external sounds. More sensation is not always better. Strong smells, cold, touch, or body-focused instructions can provoke pain, trauma responses, sensory overload, or greater dissociation.

The content creator should offer choice and an immediate stop rule: “If this increases disconnection, distress, pain, breathlessness, or the warning, stop and use your safety plan.”

### 5.3 Do not make grounding a universal solution

Grounding is widely discussed but has not been independently shown to stop functional seizures in every pattern. Phrase it as a tool to test when disconnection or narrowed attention appears relevant.

## 6. Planned competing responses

### 6.1 What “competing” means

A competing response is a simple voluntary action that is difficult to perform at the same time as part of the familiar emerging pattern. It may also anchor attention and create a different learned sequence. The action must be individually chosen, physically safe, and compatible with the person's mobility and symptoms.

For example, if a familiar onset includes both hands tightening while the person remains aware, a therapist might help identify a gentle, neutral hand posture or another movement to practise. This is not suitable when forcing movement could cause pain or injury, when awareness is already impaired, or when the movement has not been assessed.

### 6.2 Avoid the implication of suppression by force

The instruction is not “fight the seizure,” “hold yourself still,” or “prove you control it.” Force and struggle may increase alarm or injury. The person is testing a brief alternative action, not overpowering the nervous system.

### 6.3 Content needs professional review

Because seizure forms and coexisting movement, pain, and mobility problems vary, the final booklet should give the concept and safe generic prompts but direct readers to a trained clinician for a personalized motor response.

## 7. Breathing or settling skills are optional subgroup tools

### 7.1 Why breathing may help some people

If a person's map includes rapid breathing, breath-holding, escalating panic, or autonomic alarm, a gentle paced exhalation or settling routine may reduce that component. The target is the identified breathing or alarm pattern, not FND as a whole.

### 7.2 Why breathing may be the wrong tool

Focusing on breath can increase dizziness, air hunger, panic, internal monitoring, or trauma-related discomfort. Instructions such as repeated deep breaths can worsen over-breathing. Some cardiopulmonary conditions also require individualized advice.

The booklet should say: practise only a gentle clinician-approved method; never strain, hold the breath, or take repeated large breaths; stop if symptoms worsen. It should offer an external-attention alternative on the same page.

## 8. Cue words and imagery

### 8.1 A cue compresses a rehearsed plan

A phrase such as “feet, room, next step” can retrieve a longer sequence. The phrase itself is not magic. It works, if useful, because it has been linked through rehearsal to specific actions.

### 8.2 Imagery should rehearse process, not promise an outcome

A useful image might be a radio tuner widening from one alarming signal to the whole room, or a path marker that points to the next action. Avoid imagery of defeating, trapping, or forcing the seizure away. Also avoid asking readers to visualize places or memories that may be unsafe or trauma-linked.

### 8.3 Illustrate diverse forms of skill use

Show someone using a visual search, someone feeling chair support, and someone reading a three-word cue. Do not depict all people lying down or assume everyone can speak, see, hear, or move in the same way.

## 9. How to choose no more than two skills

### 9.1 Match the tool to the tentative mechanism

If the warning includes disconnection, orientation may be a reasonable first test. If it includes internal threat-monitoring, external focused attention may fit. If it includes an assessed emerging motor sequence while awareness remains, a competing response may fit. If rapid breathing is a repeated part, a gentle breathing skill may fit.

This is a hypothesis-to-tool match, not a symptom checklist that automatically prescribes treatment.

### 9.2 Too many tools create a retrieval problem

Giving eight techniques can make a person search frantically for the correct one during onset. Begin with one primary skill and one backup. The card should state when each is used and when to stop.

### 9.3 Include accessibility

A visually impaired person may use sound or touch; a person with limited speech may use a gesture or card; someone with pain may avoid forceful grounding; a person with cognitive difficulty may need a supporter cue. The goal is the underlying function, not identical performance.

## 10. Teach the behavioural experiment in full

### 10.1 Predict

“When the unreal feeling begins, I expect I will be unable to use the orientation card at all.” A prediction should be specific and can be rated from not at all to completely expected.

### 10.2 Plan

Identify the familiar warning, immediate safety position, skill, supporter role, duration, stop rule, and what will be observed. Do not deliberately bring on an episode.

### 10.3 Try

Use the skill when the familiar warning occurs naturally and circumstances are safe. If it is not safe, follow the safety plan and record that the skill was not attempted.

### 10.4 Observe

Record whether the warning was recognized, whether the card was retrieved, what happened to awareness or distress, whether an episode followed, what recovery was like, and any unwanted effect. Keep recording brief.

### 10.5 Decide

Continue if the skill is usable or promising. Adapt if it is too long, inaccessible, or needs a supporter cue. Stop if it worsens symptoms or is repeatedly unhelpful. Seek clinical advice when safety, event type, or adverse effects are uncertain.

## 11. The no-warning route

### 11.1 Name it early and respectfully

Readers with no warning should not spend sixteen pages being told to catch something they cannot detect. Direct them to a parallel route: prevent injury, prepare a calm supporter response, personalize early recovery, and build participation within appropriate restrictions.

### 11.2 A supporter-noticed sign needs consent

A supporter may notice slowed speech or stillness before the person does. An agreed short cue might create an opportunity, but constant surveillance or repeated correction can damage autonomy and increase tension. The person chooses whether the cue is used and can revoke it.

## 12. Common authoring errors

### 12.1 Making the instruction sound like willpower

Avoid “take control,” “stop the episode,” and “do not let it progress.” Prefer “try the practised response,” “notice whether anything changes,” and “safety first if the window is too short.”

### 12.2 Calling every tool grounding

External attention, orientation, sensory grounding, competing motor response, and gentle breathing have different rationales. Naming the target makes the booklet teachable and allows the reader to choose intelligently.

### 12.3 Hiding adverse effects

Every technique needs a stop rule and an alternative. A picture-heavy guide can still show this with a clear “if this makes X worse, stop and use Y” branch.

### 12.4 Treating one success or failure as proof

Natural symptoms vary. One no-seizure trial does not prove the skill worked; one seizure does not prove it cannot help. Review a small planned set of attempts without requiring exhaustive tracking.

## 13. Readiness check for the content creator

Before drafting, the creator should be able to explain:

1. Why a warning creates a possible opportunity without proving voluntary control.
2. Why rehearsal is part of the intervention rather than filler.
3. How orientation, external attention, sensory grounding, competing response, and breathing target different processes.
4. Why breathing instructions require caution.
5. How to run a behavioural experiment in which a seizure can occur without the person failing.
6. What an equal no-warning pathway contains.
7. When safety or medical reassessment replaces skill practice.
