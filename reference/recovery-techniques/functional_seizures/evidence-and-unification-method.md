# How the Unified Booklets Will Weigh Evidence

> **Decision framework for reviewers:** This page explains how material from different CBT-informed programmes will be selected, changed, or left out. “Better outcomes” receive more weight only after the study and the outcome have been examined carefully.

## The main rule

We will prefer an approach when the best available research shows meaningful benefit for people similar to the intended readers, using a fair comparison and outcomes that matter. A dramatic percentage from a small, short study will not automatically outweigh a larger, longer, or more rigorous study. An adult result will not automatically be applied to children, and a paediatric result will not automatically be applied to adults.

No programme receives an all-or-nothing endorsement. A programme may offer a strong teaching structure even when its seizure-frequency result is uncertain. It may also contain a useful-looking technique that has never been tested on its own.

## What “better outcomes” means

The review will record all of the following rather than rank treatments by seizure count alone:

1. **Safety and harms:** injury, adverse effects, emergency treatment, treatment-related distress, and withdrawal.
2. **Function and participation:** return to school, work, relationships, mobility, community life, and valued activities.
3. **Seizure outcomes:** seizure freedom, frequency, severity, duration, warnings, retained awareness, and recovery time.
4. **Quality of life and confidence:** independence, self-efficacy, fear, avoidance, and ability to use a plan.
5. **Mental and physical health:** anxiety, depression, trauma symptoms, pain, sleep, fatigue, and coexisting conditions when these were actual treatment targets.
6. **Healthcare use and treatment burden:** emergency visits, admissions, time, cost, accessibility, and acceptability.
7. **Durability:** whether improvement remains after treatment rather than only at the end of a session series.

The reader's own priority also matters. Fewer seizures may be the main goal for one person; safer recovery or returning to school may be more important for another.

## Evidence questions asked before adopting an element

| Question | Why it changes the decision |
|---|---|
| Who was studied: adults, children, mixed ages, or a specialist subgroup? | The same strategy may not have the same effect or practical meaning in another population. |
| How many people took part, and how many completed follow-up? | Small or highly selected samples give less stable estimates and may hide acceptability problems. |
| Was there random assignment and a credible comparison? | Improvement over time does not show that the treatment caused it. Education, medical follow-up, regression to the mean, and natural change can also matter. |
| What was the prespecified primary outcome? | Positive secondary findings should not be substituted for an unsuccessful primary outcome without saying so. |
| How large and personally meaningful was the difference? | Statistical significance alone does not tell a reader whether life changed. |
| How long was follow-up? | Seven-day seizure freedom and one-year functioning answer different questions. |
| Was the complete programme tested, or the individual technique? | A component included in a successful package is not automatically independently proven. |
| Were harms, drop-out, access, and burden reported? | A strategy that few people can use is not universally “better.” |
| Do other studies and current guidelines agree? | Replication and synthesis receive more weight than one isolated result. |

## Evidence labels used while drafting

- **Supported by controlled adult evidence:** a relevant comparative adult study supports the outcome being discussed, with limitations stated.
- **Promising paediatric evidence:** a child or adolescent pilot or controlled study supports further use and research; adult applicability is not assumed.
- **Included in a studied programme:** the element was part of a treatment package, but its independent effect is unknown.
- **Reported helpful by participants:** qualitative or lived-experience material describes usefulness or acceptability; this is not an effectiveness estimate.
- **Clinical recommendation; direct trial evidence unavailable:** a guideline or specialist consensus recommends the practice, usually for safety, communication, or individualized care.

These labels are more precise than calling every CBT exercise “evidence-based.”

## How the main programmes influence the unified plan

### Adult foundation: CODES, with an explicit mixed result

CODES is the largest pragmatic adult trial in this set and therefore receives substantial weight for adult treatment structure, individualized formulation, seizure-management work, reduction of avoidance, and relapse planning. Its primary 12-month monthly-seizure-frequency outcome was not significantly better than standardized medical care. Several secondary outcomes were better. The booklets may therefore borrow the *questions and practice domains* tested in CODES, but must not promise seizure cessation or say that every CODES component caused the secondary improvements. [CODES trial protocol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12883-015-0350-0) · [CODES trial report](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(20)30128-0) · [CODES process evaluation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK571616/)

### Paediatric signal: ReACT, kept within its population and time frame

The ReACT pilot randomized trial reported a large short-term reduction in seizures in a small paediatric sample. This justifies clearly labelling its core ideas—retraining an alternative response, reducing catastrophic expectations, building a sense of control, a planned family response, and prompt return to school—as promising paediatric evidence. It does not justify presenting the result as established for adults or reproducing older behaviour-management wording that could be experienced as punitive. [ReACT pilot trial](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/acn3.51138)

### Clinical depth: the paediatric Mind-Body Manual

The open-access manual provides a rich, flexible clinical model, family and systems guidance, and body-regulation and rehabilitation options. Its ideas were developed through clinical practice and prospective cohorts rather than one definitive randomized test of every element. The booklets should use it as a source of questions and options, not as proof that one “stress-system” explanation fits every young person. [Open-access manual](https://www.ausapress.com/p/treatment-of-functional-seizures-in-children-and-adolescents/)

### Structured adult practice: Taking Control

The *Taking Control* workbook and therapist guide demonstrate how education, monitoring, goals, thought work, communication, and relapse planning can become a teachable sequence. The unified booklets will create original, shorter activities and will avoid importing any assumption that emotional conflict or a particular trigger must be present. [Workbook record](https://academic.oup.com/book/1357)

### Current clinical floor: the AAN guideline

The current guideline is the safety and evidence floor: evaluate functional seizures appropriately, consider coexisting epilepsy and other conditions, communicate respectfully, involve supporters with consent, avoid antiseizure medication for functional seizures alone, and offer suitable psychological intervention while acknowledging that the evidence is “possibly effective.” [AAN functional-seizures guideline](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214466)

## Unification decisions already made

- The booklets will not require trauma, stress, anxiety, or a psychiatric diagnosis as the explanation.
- “Trigger mapping” will be renamed **pattern mapping**. It can include body sensations, attention, expectations, surroundings, activity, sleep, pain, relationships, memories, and consequences—and it can honestly conclude that no clear pattern has been found.
- Breathing or relaxation will be optional tools, not universal first-line instructions. They will be stopped or adapted if they increase dizziness, breath discomfort, panic, or symptoms.
- Supporter guidance will aim for calm, predictable, autonomy-preserving help. It will not use punishment, deliberate ignoring, blame, or withdrawal of appropriate care.
- Return to activity will use graded, safety-aware experiments. It will distinguish medically necessary restrictions from fear-driven avoidance and will defer driving, water, heights, machinery, and similar decisions to individual/local guidance.
- Progress will include participation and quality of life even when seizures continue.
- A technique that does not help is information about fit, timing, or delivery—not evidence that the person failed.

## Decision record required for every exercise

Before an exercise enters the final PDF, its authoring note must state:

- the reader and situation it is for;
- the intended outcome;
- the source programme or rationale;
- the evidence label above;
- known limits and possible harms;
- how it can be made lower-demand or accessible;
- what result means “continue,” “adapt,” or “stop”; and
- whether professional guidance is needed.

## Reviewer questions

- Does the wording distinguish programme-level evidence from component-level evidence?
- Are adult and paediatric findings kept separate?
- Is a non-significant primary result as visible as positive secondary findings?
- Does “why this may help” describe a plausible treatment target without declaring one cause of functional seizures?
- Are safety, function, and continued care visible even when seizure control is limited?
- Could any reward, supporter, exposure, thought-challenging, breathing, or monitoring instruction be experienced as coercive, blaming, or unsafe?
