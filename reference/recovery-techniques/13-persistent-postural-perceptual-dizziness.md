# Recovery Techniques for Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness

> **Working draft:** This page was automatically generated and is [looking for contributors and reviewers](https://fnd-education-project.github.io/FND-Education/).

**Also described as:** PPPD, 3PD and persistent perceptual dizziness.

PPPD treatment usually combines an individualized explanation with gradual return to upright posture, head/body motion, visual motion and everyday activity. The dose matters: too little exposure can preserve avoidance, while too much can cause a setback and loss of confidence.

**Evidence labels:** **R** research-supported · **C** clinical consensus · **E** emerging.

## For the person with symptoms

### Techniques used in treatment

1. **Individualized vestibular rehabilitation — R/C.** A vestibular physiotherapist identifies the person’s motion, balance, visual and gait pattern and selects a small set of exercises. A generic internet program may target the wrong system or progress too quickly.
2. **Habituation to head and body motion — R/C.** Repeat a mild, relevant movement in a controlled dose until the response becomes more manageable, then change speed, range, repetitions or position gradually. The goal is adaptation and restored activity, not provoking the strongest possible symptoms.
3. **Graded upright exposure — R/C.** Build time sitting, standing and walking in small increments. Use stable support when needed and progress duration before adding a busy environment or another challenge.
4. **Graded visual-motion exposure — R/C.** Start with a tolerable visual task and add movement or complexity slowly: a quiet room, brief screen task, calm shop aisle, then busier environments. Autoplay patterns and rapid scrolling are poor unsupervised starting points.
5. **Gaze-stability exercise when indicated — C.** Keep a target clear while moving the head in a prescribed direction and speed. This is useful only when assessment identifies a relevant gaze problem; it is not mandatory for every person with PPPD.
6. **Balance and gait practice — C.** Progress stance, walking surface, head movement and reduced visual dependence according to fall risk. Use an aid when needed for safety and participation.
7. **Graded community activity — C.** Practise real destinations such as a sidewalk, transit stop, café or shop, starting at a manageable time and duration. Repeat before adding crowd, noise, visual motion or fatigue.
8. **Psychologically informed vestibular rehabilitation — E.** A therapist may integrate attention, prediction, fear and avoidance into vestibular practice. CBT or ACT can help change the response to symptoms and support return to activity; evidence specific to PPPD is still developing.
9. **Treat the precipitant and comorbidity — C.** Vestibular migraine, benign positional vertigo, Ménière disease, panic, neuropathy and other conditions can coexist. Treating them may be necessary even when the persistent PPPD pattern also needs rehabilitation.
10. **Optokinetic or virtual-reality practice — E.** Supervised visual-motion technology may help grade complex environments. It can be strongly provocative and requires a static alternative, a clear stop plan and clinical selection.
11. **Specialist neuromodulation or galvanic vestibular stimulation — E.** A 2026 network meta-analysis found promising balance and anxiety results for neuromodulation, but most included studies had high risk of bias and the network was sparse. rTMS, non-invasive vagus-nerve stimulation and galvanic vestibular stimulation belong in specialist or research settings, not home experimentation.
12. **SSRI or SNRI discussion — C/uncertain.** These medicines are used in clinical practice for some people, especially with anxiety or migraine overlap, but a 2023 Cochrane review found no eligible placebo-controlled PPPD medication trials. Discuss possible benefits, harms and uncertainty with a prescriber; do not stop suddenly.
13. **Relapse plan — C.** Record the baseline dose that remains possible during a flare, helpful visual or motion settings, medication/condition triggers, fall precautions and when to seek reassessment.

### A low-risk starting practice

Choose one already-safe activity—such as sitting upright or a short walk on a clear, level route—and repeat a dose that produces only manageable, settling symptoms. Change one variable at a time and allow recovery. Stop for fainting, falls, new neurological symptoms, chest pain, severe headache, prolonged vomiting or a large sustained deterioration.

### Ask for urgent help or reassessment when

Dizziness is sudden or distinctly different, or occurs with new weakness, double vision, speech trouble, inability to walk, severe headache, fainting, chest pain, hearing loss, head injury or persistent vomiting. Even established PPPD requires reassessment when falls, new ear/neurological signs or progressive functional loss occur.

## For family, friends and supporters

- Do not deliberately expose the person to scrolling, supermarkets or rapid head movement to test the diagnosis.
- Help repeat the agreed dose and leave before symptoms become unmanageable.
- Offer steady support without pulling, and respect prescribed aids.
- Encourage return to meaningful places while accepting planned recovery time.
- Treat a new dizziness pattern as new information, not automatically as PPPD.

## For clinicians

Confirm the Bárány criteria and evaluate the precipitating and coexisting vestibular, migraine, neurological, cardiovascular and psychological conditions. Profile visual dependence, motion sensitivity, gaze, balance, gait, avoidance and fall risk. Explain the model without implying that symptoms are imaginary.

Set a baseline dose and progress one dimension at a time. Integrate CBT-informed principles when threat prediction or avoidance impedes vestibular adaptation. Measure participation and visually complex real-world function, not only symptom provocation. Discuss medication evidence honestly and coordinate migraine or psychiatric treatment as appropriate.

## Research and evidence

A 2025 meta-analysis found improvement with vestibular rehabilitation across eight studies, but heterogeneity and limited trial quality mean further randomized research is needed (**FND-CIT-0038**). Strict 2023 Cochrane reviews found no eligible placebo-controlled pharmacological trial (**FND-CIT-0039**) and only one very small eligible non-pharmacological study, leaving substantial uncertainty (**FND-CIT-0040**). A newer network meta-analysis of 11 trials found promising balance and anxiety estimates for neuromodulation, but 81.8% of included studies were judged at high risk of bias and the overall evidence was low to moderate quality (**FND-CIT-0041**). These findings can coexist because reviews use different inclusion criteria and dates.

No community-only technique was added. Repeated user reports about graded walking, visual exposure and pacing were already represented in vestibular practice; improvised spinning, flashing or forceful exposure did not pass the safety screen.
