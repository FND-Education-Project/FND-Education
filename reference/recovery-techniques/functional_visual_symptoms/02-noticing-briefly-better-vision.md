## For the Person With FND

### What this approach is for

An ordinary moment may provide useful information: a large word was readable, an object was easier to find or a familiar face was clearer. Notice the circumstances if this is helpful, then return to what you were doing. You do not need to recreate the moment until it becomes permanent.

The purpose is to find a possible starting point for care. This is different from keeping score of whether your symptoms are “good enough.” If observation makes you monitor your vision constantly, simplify or stop it.

### Terms in everyday language

**Variability** means that an ability changes between occasions or conditions. Font size, contrast, visual clutter, fatigue and the task itself can change performance. Variability alone does not diagnose FND or explain the cause of every change.

### Practical discussion and flare adaptations

For example, you might tell the therapist: “I could read one large word on a plain label while seated.” That is enough to explore whether a similar layout makes an important task accessible. There is no need for a detailed hourly diary.

Ask before a supporter records a moment. A note should describe the event, not interpret your willingness or recovery prospects. If no better moments are noticeable, the team can work with your current limitations. During a flare, observation can wait; safety and assistance come first.

These examples require selection for your assessed presentation. The clinical rationale and its limits are described in the sources below. (*Sources:* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

Use neutral observations only with permission. “That label was readable in this layout” is more useful than “You can see when you stop thinking about it.” Do not secretly film, test or arrange obstacles to catch a response.

Help preserve the conditions that made the activity manageable. Avoid demanding the same performance later, especially when fatigue, headache or the setting has changed.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Selection and assessment

Clarify the exact ability observed, its context and whether a coexisting optical, ocular or neurological explanation contributes. Spontaneous daily variability can guide treatment hypotheses but cannot replace measured assessment. Ensure observations are clinically relevant rather than intrusive surveillance.

### Explicit procedure

1. Agree whether noticing variation is acceptable and useful to the person.
2. Choose a low-burden way to report an occasional observation, or omit recording altogether.
3. Describe the task, size, distance, lighting and assistance without assigning a psychological cause.
4. Select one comparable safe task for a later session, with current supports retained.
5. Check whether the task is actually useful and tolerable rather than requiring the earlier result.
6. Stop the observation plan if it promotes repeated testing, conflict or symptom escalation.

### Review, progression and stopping

Judge usefulness by improved understanding or task access, not the number of favourable moments collected. A quieter week with no clear examples is not evidence of deterioration or low effort. Discuss any new, persistent or otherwise different visual change on its own merits.

This is an educational implementation example, not a validated standalone treatment protocol. (*Sources:* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))
