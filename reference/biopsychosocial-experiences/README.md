Some parts of living with FND do not fit neatly into a symptom list.

A person may become overwhelmed more easily, have less patience when they are exhausted or in pain, feel unusually alert or easily startled, or find that noise, light, thinking, emotions and social demands all become harder to manage at the same time.

These experiences can matter a great deal. They are still not automatically FND symptoms, and they do not tell us what caused a person's FND.

> **Biopsychosocial:** A way of looking at health that includes the body, thoughts and emotions, relationships, surroundings and life circumstances. It does **not** mean “psychological cause.”

**“Biopsychosocial Experiences” is a project navigation label.** It is not a formal diagnosis, a recognized FND subtype or an established medical classification. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))

> **Associated experience:** Something that may happen alongside FND or while living with FND, without necessarily being caused by FND.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A new problem should not automatically be blamed on FND. Pain, migraine, poor sleep, medication or substance effects, another medical or neurological condition, anxiety or depression, environmental strain and many other things can affect how a person feels and functions.

## For the Person With FND

### Why have a section like this?

A symptom list can tell you that FND may affect movement, sensation, speech, vision, awareness or thinking. It does not always explain what daily life can feel like when several demands pile up at once.

You might notice that a difficult conversation is much harder when you are already in pain. You might cope with noise for a while and then suddenly feel that one more sound is too much. You may find it harder to stay patient when you are exhausted, or need much longer than other people expect to recover after an upsetting event.

Those experiences are worth understanding even when we cannot say, “FND caused this.”

> **Diagnostic sign:** A finding a clinician uses as positive evidence when diagnosing a condition. The experiences in this collection are **not** being presented as diagnostic signs of FND.

That distinction matters. You should be able to talk about an important problem without having every part of your life turned into proof of why you developed FND.

### Start with what is happening now

It can be more useful to ask:

“What is making this harder right now?”

than:

“Which one thing caused this?”

For one person, pain and poor sleep may be the biggest part of the picture. For another, noise and too many conversations may use up what they can manage. For someone else, medication changes, grief, anxiety, migraine or an unrelated health problem may matter more.

More than one thing can be true at the same time.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

An associated experience may help explain why something became harder without explaining everything about the person.

Try describing what you can actually see. “You look like you have less room for another conversation right now” is usually more useful than “This is your FND,” “You are too stressed,” or “You are overreacting.”

Support might mean lowering the noise, asking one question at a time, postponing a decision, following an agreed safety plan or giving the person time to recover.

It can also mean respecting a boundary.

> **Boundary:** A clear limit about what a person is willing or able to do, and what they will do if that limit is reached. A boundary is about your own actions; it is not a way to control another person.

For example: “I want to talk about this, but I cannot do it while we are both shouting. If the shouting continues, I am going to pause and we can try again later.”

That is different from “You are not allowed to be angry” or “You need to calm down.” Those statements try to control the other person's feelings.

Both the person with FND and the supporter are allowed to have boundaries. Illness can explain why someone has less capacity without making threats, intimidation or violence acceptable.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

Keep the **positive neurological basis of the FND diagnosis** separate from a biopsychosocial formulation.

Associated experiences may be useful treatment targets or contributors to disability, but they should not be used retrospectively as proof of FND causation. Clarify whether a factor is being considered as a comorbidity, maintaining influence, treatment target, differential diagnosis, environmental demand or ordinary consequence of chronic illness. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))

Use plain descriptions before technical labels when possible. “Noise and several people speaking make it harder for you to think and stay regulated” may be more useful to a patient than introducing multiple mechanism terms at once.

Where a cross-cutting experience is severe or new, review relevant neurological, medical, sleep, medication, substance and psychiatric contributors proportionately. A useful formulation can contain uncertainty and more than one explanation.
