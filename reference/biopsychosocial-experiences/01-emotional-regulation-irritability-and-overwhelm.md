**This page covers:** Becoming overwhelmed more easily, losing patience faster than usual, strong emotions that rise quickly, crying more easily, taking longer to settle after an argument or upsetting event, or sometimes feeling shut down and disconnected instead.

**Scope boundary:** These experiences may happen in some people with FND, but **anger and irritability are not diagnostic signs of FND**. Research has looked at emotional processing in FND, but it does not show one emotional pattern that applies to everyone or prove that emotional difficulty caused an individual's FND. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))

## For the Person With FND

### When your emotions feel harder to control

You may notice that you have less patience than you used to. A small frustration may suddenly feel enormous. You might go from “I am managing this” to angry, tearful or completely overwhelmed very quickly.

Some people notice the opposite and shut down instead. They may go quiet, feel numb, lose words or feel disconnected from what is happening around them.

> **Emotional regulation:** The ability to notice a feeling, adjust to it enough to think, and choose what to do next. It does not mean never getting angry, sad, frightened or frustrated.

Having trouble with emotional regulation does not mean your feelings are wrong. The problem may be that the feeling becomes **too big, too fast, or too hard to come down from**.

> **Overwhelm:** The point where the demands on you feel greater than what you can manage at that moment. The demands might be physical, sensory, thinking, emotional or social.

For example, you may be able to handle a difficult conversation on a fairly good day. The same conversation may become much harder when you also have a migraine, slept badly, are in pain and have several people talking around you.

### This does not mean “FND made me angry”

Researchers have studied how people with FND notice, feel and respond to emotions. Some studies have found differences when groups of people with FND are compared with other groups. That tells us emotion is worth studying. It does **not** tell us that everyone with FND has trouble managing emotions, that anger is an FND symptom, or that emotion caused a particular person's FND. (*citation* [1](#citation-1))

Researchers also study how attention, body signals, emotions and the feeling of being in control of an action may interact. These ideas are still being worked out. They are not tests that can tell a person why their FND began. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))

There is another direction to remember too: **FND itself can give you plenty to be emotional about.**

A seizure, fall, loss of movement, trouble speaking, pain flare or loss of independence can be frightening and frustrating. Being disbelieved, needing help, losing work or having plans repeatedly interrupted can affect anyone's mood and relationships.

Emotion can affect symptoms, symptoms can affect emotion, and sometimes both are being affected by something else.

### Why might you have less room before you are overwhelmed?

Think about how much you are already carrying before the difficult moment begins.

Pain, migraine, poor sleep, fatigue, hunger, illness, noise, bright light, medication changes, anxiety, depression, trauma-related symptoms, conflict and ordinary life strain can all make a hard moment harder.

> **Available capacity:** What you can realistically manage right now, including physical, thinking, sensory, emotional and social demands. It can change during the day.

If much of your available capacity is already being used by pain, symptoms or sensory input, you may have less left for patience, careful listening, decision-making or stopping yourself before saying something you regret.

That can help explain a lower threshold. It still does not tell us which one thing caused it.

If irritability or emotional intensity changed after starting, reducing or stopping a medication or substance, tell the prescriber or appropriate clinician. Do not assume the change is simply FND.

### Notice the earlier signs, not just the explosion

The easiest time to change course is often **before** you are fully overwhelmed.

Your early signs may be physical: a tight jaw, heat, shaking, faster breathing or pacing.

They may be thinking changes: losing words, not being able to follow what someone is saying, repeating yourself, or feeling that you must solve the disagreement immediately.

They may be emotional: suddenly feeling trapped, attacked, desperate to leave or far angrier than the situation seemed to deserve a few minutes earlier.

You do not need to monitor yourself every minute. The aim is simply to learn one or two signs that tell you, “I am getting close to my limit.”

### What is a boundary?

Many people hear “set boundaries” without anyone explaining what that actually means.

> **Boundary:** A clear statement about what you are willing or able to do, and what **you will do** if that limit is reached. A boundary is about your actions. It is not a rule that forces another person to feel, think or behave the way you want.

A boundary can protect your health, time, privacy, safety or relationship.

For example:

“I want to finish this conversation, but I cannot do it while two people are talking over each other. If that keeps happening, I am going to pause and we can try again later.”

“I am having trouble speaking. I can listen for five minutes, but I am not making this decision right now. I will answer tomorrow.”

“I am willing to talk about what happened. If I am called names, I will end the conversation and come back when we can speak respectfully.”

“I need quiet for the next half hour. After that, please check in again.”

Notice what these have in common. They do not say, “You are not allowed to be angry,” “You must calm down,” or “You must agree with me.” Those are attempts to control the other person.

A boundary also does not have to sound harsh. **Polite and firm can exist together.**

### What does it mean to hold a boundary?

Saying a boundary once is only the first part.

> **Holding a boundary:** Calmly following through on what you said you would do when the limit is reached.

Suppose your boundary is: “If we start shouting, I am going to pause the conversation.”

Holding it may look like this:

First, say it before things become severe if you can: “I want to keep talking. I am getting overloaded. If we start shouting, I am going to pause.”

If shouting starts, repeat the boundary briefly: “We are shouting now. I am going to pause.”

Then do what you said: stop the conversation, leave the room if that is safe, or reduce contact for the agreed period.

If the issue still matters, return when you said you would: “I am ready to try again now.”

You do not have to keep arguing about whether you are “allowed” to have the boundary. The other person may dislike it. They do not have to agree with it for you to follow through on your own action.

At the same time, boundaries should not be used as punishment. Refusing to speak to someone for days to make them suffer is different from saying, “I need an hour before I can safely continue this conversation.”

If a boundary repeatedly does not work, talk about it later when things are calmer and make it more realistic.

### A pause is not abandonment

For some people, “I need to stop this conversation” can sound like rejection. It may help to include a return plan.

Instead of only saying, “I am done,” you might say:

“I need to stop for twenty minutes. I am not abandoning this. I will come back at 7:30.”

Or:

“I cannot promise a time because I am having symptoms. I will check in when I can speak again.”

A return plan is helpful when it is safe and realistic. If there are threats, violence or immediate danger, safety comes first and you do not have to stay in the situation to prove you are being fair.

### Both people get boundaries

Having FND does not mean only the person with FND gets limits.

A supporter might say:

“I want to help, but I cannot keep talking while I am being shouted at. I am going to step away and come back in half an hour.”

“I can drive you to the appointment, but I cannot also make three extra stops today.”

“I can listen, but I cannot decide whether this symptom is FND or something medical.”

Those are boundaries about what the supporter can do.

Likewise, a person with FND might need boundaries around noise, touch, symptom discussions, being spoken for, fast decisions or being pushed to continue after their capacity has dropped.

### Responsibility and explanation can both be true

Pain, fatigue, FND symptoms or overload may make emotional control harder. That can help explain why something happened.

It does not make threats, intimidation, violence or deliberately cruel behaviour acceptable.

If you hurt someone during an overwhelmed moment, the next step is not necessarily to blame yourself or to deny what happened. It can be to acknowledge it, apologize where appropriate, repair what you can, and change the plan for next time.

A useful plan protects everyone involved.

### A simple plan for the next emotional surge

You do not need a complicated worksheet.

Try remembering four steps:

**Notice.** What is one early sign that you are getting close to your limit?

**Reduce.** Can you remove one demand—noise, another conversation, a decision, standing, bright light or something else?

**Pause.** Is there a boundary you can state before the situation becomes worse?

**Return.** If the issue still matters, when and how will you come back to it?

If this kind of planning makes you constantly watch yourself or increases distress, simplify it.

### When to ask for reassessment

Ask for clinical review when irritability, emotional intensity or behaviour is **new, severe, rapidly worsening or very different from your usual pattern**.

That is especially important when the change comes with major changes in sleep, confusion, poor judgement, medication or substance changes, new neurological symptoms or safety concerns.

Immediate help may be needed when there is danger to you or someone else.

### Questions

#### What is one early sign that tells you, “I am getting close to my limit”?

#### What usually uses up your available capacity before a difficult interaction even begins?

#### What boundary could you say in one or two sentences?

#### If you pause a difficult conversation, what would make it easier to return to it later?

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

A fast emotional response can be difficult for everyone involved.

If the person has an early-warning plan, help use it before the conversation becomes harder. That may mean one person speaking at a time, reducing noise, postponing a non-urgent decision or accepting a pause.

Do not diagnose the person's emotion for them. “This is your FND” or “this is just anxiety” may be wrong and can turn a disagreement into an argument about illness.

### You can set boundaries too

A supporter does not have to absorb unlimited shouting, threats, demands or caregiving because the other person has FND.

A useful boundary is short and specific:

“I want to listen, but I cannot do that while I am being shouted at. I am going to step away for twenty minutes.”

“I can help with the shower or the meal tonight, but I cannot do both. Which one matters more?”

“I will help you get somewhere safe during an episode. I will not argue about what caused it while it is happening.”

Holding the boundary means following through calmly. It does **not** mean lecturing, proving a point or punishing the person.

Where possible, separate a capacity problem from a character judgement. “You were already in pain, the television was loud, and then we both started talking” gives useful information. “You always overreact” does not.

Both people should be able to say, “Not now,” “I need space,” “I need help,” or “I can do this much but not more.”

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Keep diagnosis and formulation separate

Do not use irritability, crying, emotional reactivity, trauma history or difficulty regulating emotion as positive diagnostic evidence for FND.

The FND diagnosis should rest on the appropriate positive neurological features. Emotional processing can then be considered separately as part of formulation, disability, treatment planning or comorbidity when it is relevant to the individual. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [4](#citation-4))

### Ask what the person means before applying a label

“Emotion dysregulation” can hide several different problems. Clarify whether the person means rapid emotional intensity, irritability, impulsive behaviour, sensory overload, loss of speech during conflict, prolonged recovery, dissociation, difficulty identifying emotions or something else.

Also clarify:

- baseline personality and emotional regulation before the change;
- relationship to pain, migraine, sleep, fatigue and sensory load;
- medication, substance or withdrawal effects;
- anxiety, depression, PTSD, dissociation, ADHD or other relevant psychiatric or neurodevelopmental factors;
- seizure, cognitive or autonomic symptoms around episodes; and
- interpersonal and environmental demands.

A severe new change in behaviour should prompt proportionate medical and psychiatric differential assessment rather than automatic attribution to FND.

### Match treatment to the actual target

Psychological treatment may be useful when the person wants help with emotion regulation, trauma, anxiety, depression, symptom fear, coping or interpersonal consequences. That does not mean psychotherapy is required to validate the FND diagnosis, nor does improvement with therapy prove a psychological cause. (*citation* [4](#citation-4))

Occupational, rehabilitation, neurological and primary-care work may also matter when pain, sleep, medication effects, sensory load or activity demands are major contributors.

The aim is not compulsory calmness. A better target may be earlier recognition, safer communication, fewer escalations, quicker recovery, more participation or reduced distress.

Boundary-setting and pause-and-return examples on this page are general communication and safety tools. They should not be presented as a tested FND treatment.

## Research and Sources

### What the evidence can and cannot support

Pick and colleagues reviewed research on emotional processing in FND and proposed a biopsychosocial research model. The literature included multiple emotional domains and varied methods. It supports studying emotion as one potentially relevant system, not treating anger, irritability or emotional dysregulation as universal FND symptoms or proven causes. (*citation* [1](#citation-1))

Broader FND mechanism reviews discuss interactions among emotion, salience, attention, interoception, agency and motor control. These are research frameworks rather than individual diagnostic biomarkers. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))

Psychological-management guidance supports individualized treatment targets and attention to comorbidity without requiring a psychological stressor to establish FND. (*citation* [4](#citation-4))

The practical boundary-setting examples on this page are general relationship and safety guidance. They have not been presented here as an FND-specific evidence-based treatment.
