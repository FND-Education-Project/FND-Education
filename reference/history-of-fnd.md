Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) has been described under many names and explained in very different ways. Learning a little of that history makes today's information easier to understand—and helps explain why two papers about seemingly similar patients can reach very different conclusions.

**When reading older research, pay particular attention to papers published before 2013.** That year, the DSM-5 diagnostic manual removed the requirement to identify an associated psychological cause and placed more emphasis on clinical evidence supporting the diagnosis. Earlier studies may use labels such as *hysteria* or *conversion disorder*, select patients differently, or assume psychological explanations that are no longer required. **2013 is a useful landmark, not an expiry date:** some older studies remain valuable, and newer papers also need to be judged on their methods and claims. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [13](#citation-13), [17](#citation-17))

A few highlights show the journey: **1908**, a bedside sign for functional leg weakness; **2013**, a major change in diagnostic criteria; **the 2010s onward**, growing research using brain imaging; and **2024**, an emerging study of brain chemistry in young people with FND. Imaging and chemistry results are research findings in groups, not diagnostic scans or blood tests for individuals. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [4](#citation-4), [14](#citation-14), [22](#citation-22), [23](#citation-23))

This selective timeline follows how clinicians have **recognized, diagnosed, explained and treated** functional neurological symptoms. It is not a complete history of every theory or researcher.

> [!NOTE]
> Historical terms such as *hysteria*, *psychogenic*, *conversion disorder* and *non-organic* are included because they were used in the literature. Their appearance here does not mean that this project recommends those terms today.

## Timeline at a glance
{: #timeline-at-a-glance .shareable }

| Period | What changed | Why it mattered | Representative source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Late 1800s** | Neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot and others carefully described paralysis, movement problems and other symptoms then grouped under the label “hysteria.” | This brought the symptoms serious attention within neurology. But the old label covered many different conditions, so not every historical case would be called FND today. | [Stone, 2016 historical review](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-801772-2.00003-5). (*citations* [13](#citation-13)) |
| **1890s** | Breuer, Freud and other writers developed influential explanations in which physical symptoms could express emotional conflict outside a person's awareness. | This helped make psychological explanations dominant for much of the next century. Today, someone does not need a known trauma or psychological conflict to receive an FND diagnosis. | [Stone, 2016 historical review](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-801772-2.00003-5). (*citations* [13](#citation-13)) |
| **1908** | Charles Franklin Hoover described a bedside examination for leg weakness: a leg that seems weak during one requested movement may produce more force automatically during another. This is now called **Hoover's sign**. | It was an early example of looking for a **positive sign**—a recognizable change in movement that can support a diagnosis. The finding is not evidence that someone is faking or choosing their weakness. | [Hoover's original 1908 report](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1908.25410090028001h). See the project's [Hoover's Sign](diagnostic-signs/functional_limb_weakness/01-hoovers-sign.md) page and later prospective validation by [McWhirter et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2011.09.003). (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [14](#citation-14)) |
| **1914–1945** | Doctors reported soldiers with paralysis, tremor, difficulty walking, seizure-like events and sensory changes during and after the two world wars. They often used the name “shell shock” for some of these problems. | These accounts influenced how medicine linked symptoms to injury, trauma and stress, and how neurology and psychiatry approached patients. Historical wartime labels do not neatly match today's FND categories. | [Stone, 2016 historical review](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-801772-2.00003-5). (*citations* [13](#citation-13)) |
| **1965** | Psychiatrist Eliot Slater warned that some patients diagnosed with “hysteria” later turned out to have another neurological or medical illness. His paper became widely discussed. | It raised an important question: how can clinicians avoid overlooking another illness? Later, better-designed follow-up research found lower rates of diagnostic error. Both the patients studied and the way diagnoses were made differed across eras. | [Slater, 1965](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.1.5447.1395); later reassessment in [Stone et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38628.466898.55). (*citations* [15](#citation-15), [16](#citation-16)) |
| **1980s–1990s** | Psychiatric diagnostic manuals formalized the label “conversion disorder,” and the search for an underlying psychological explanation remained prominent. | This affected research and clinical referrals for decades. Many people were sent from neurology to psychiatry, even when their symptoms needed neurological assessment and rehabilitation too. | The later problems with these criteria are reviewed by [Stone et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2011.07.005). (*citations* [17](#citation-17)) |
| **2000s** | Researchers revisited how often the diagnosis was mistaken and studied distinctive patterns on neurological examination. Attention increasingly shifted from simply ruling everything else out to identifying features that support FND. | A normal scan alone cannot establish FND. Clear findings from an examination can provide positive evidence, while doctors still check for other conditions when needed. | [Stone et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38628.466898.55). (*citations* [16](#citation-16)) |
| **2012** | Edwards and colleagues proposed a **predictive-processing** model. In simple terms, it explores how the brain's automatic expectations and its attention to incoming information might influence movement and bodily experience. | This offered testable ideas about brain function without assuming one hidden psychological cause. It remains a scientific model, not a proven explanation for every symptom or person. | [Edwards et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/aws129). (*citations* [18](#citation-18)) |
| **2013** | The DSM-5 manual updated its criteria for **functional neurological symptom disorder**. Clinicians no longer had to identify a psychological stressor, and clinical findings that support the diagnosis became more central. | This was a major change in how the condition was officially described. It also helps explain why older articles may give a different impression of who can have FND. | Discussed in [Espay et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaneurol.2018.1264). (*citations* [1](#citation-1)) |
| **2015** | Physiotherapists published international recommendations for helping people with functional movement symptoms. The approach included retraining movement and making use of more automatic ways of moving. | It gave rehabilitation teams shared principles for treatment rather than assuming that an explanation alone was enough. The plan still needs to fit each person's abilities, goals and safety needs. | [Nielsen et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2014-309255). (*citations* [5](#citation-5)) |
| **2018** | A major review in *JAMA Neurology* brought together developments in positive diagnosis, explanations of FND and multidisciplinary treatment. | It helped present FND as a condition neurologists can recognize and discuss, with different treatments available for different symptoms and needs. | [Espay et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaneurol.2018.1264). (*citations* [1](#citation-1)) |
| **2010s–2020s (brain imaging)** | Researchers increasingly used **functional MRI (fMRI)** to study changes in blood-oxygen signals linked to brain activity and how brain regions work together. Other MRI studies looked for differences in brain structure between groups. | These studies gave researchers new ways to test ideas about brain networks. They do **not** provide an fMRI scan that diagnoses FND in an individual, or prove that any reported difference caused symptoms. | [Hallett et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(21)00422-1); [Bègue et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nicl.2019.101798). (*citations* [4](#citation-4), [22](#citation-22)) |
| **2020** | Occupational therapists published FND-specific recommendations for everyday activities. The large **CODES** trial tested a particular form of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for functional seizures. | The OT paper helped guide practical support. In CODES, the main measure—seizure frequency—did not show a statistically clear difference between groups, although several other measures favoured CBT. Results depend on what the study actually measured. | [Nicholson et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2019-322281); [Goldstein et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(20)30128-0). (*citations* [6](#citation-6), [19](#citation-19)) |
| **2021** | Speech and language specialists published shared recommendations for functional difficulties with communication, swallowing, coughing and related symptoms. | This made work already being done by these clinicians easier to explain and organize. It did not mean these treatments had only just been invented or proven equally effective for every symptom. | [Baker et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2021-326767). (*citations* [7](#citation-7)) |
| **2022** | A major review looked across functional movement symptoms, seizures, cognition and other presentations, discussing possible shared mechanisms and differences between them. | Researchers were increasingly studying interactions among brain networks rather than looking for one damaged spot or one psychological explanation that fits everyone. | [Hallett et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(21)00422-1). (*citations* [4](#citation-4)) |
| **2024** | The large **Physio4FMD** trial compared specialist FND physiotherapy with community neurological physiotherapy. Separate guidance summarized tests of nerve and muscle signals used in some functional movement disorders. | The specialist programme did not clearly outperform the comparison treatment on its main physical-function measure at 12 months, though some other outcomes favoured it. The nerve-and-muscle tests can support selected diagnoses; they are not general FND tests. | [Nielsen et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(24)00135-2); [Edwards et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cnp.2023.12.006). (*citations* [20](#citation-20), [21](#citation-21)) |
| **2024 (brain chemistry)** | In a small study involving children and adolescents with FND, researchers used **magnetic resonance spectroscopy**—an MRI-related technique that estimates levels of certain brain chemicals—and reported differences between groups. | This opened another line of research into how FND may relate to brain biology. The findings do not establish a cause, apply automatically to adults or provide an individual diagnostic test. | [Charney et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nicl.2023.103557). (*citation* [23](#citation-23)) |
| **2025** | New multidisciplinary recommendations emphasized care matched to the person's needs. Other research asked patients, caregivers and clinicians which changes matter most in daily life. | This moved the discussion beyond “Which one treatment fixes FND?” toward practical questions about coordination, function, quality of life and personally meaningful progress. | [Lehn et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjno-2024-000970); [Rutten et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-025-12912-9). (*citations* [8](#citation-8), [9](#citation-9)) |
| **Late 2025–2026** | The American Academy of Neurology published guidance for treating functional seizures. Researchers also studied sensory examination signs, electrical testing for functional tremor and how clinicians explain a diagnosis. | The guideline and studies answer different questions. For example, evidence about electrical tremor testing cannot be treated as a diagnostic test for every kind of FND; even familiar bedside signs need proper testing. | [Tolchin et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214466); [Nielsen et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/braincomms/fcag031); [Rujirussawarawong et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.70707); [Silva & Silva, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pec.2026.109826). (*citations* [3](#citation-3), [10](#citation-10), [11](#citation-11), [12](#citation-12)) |

**A small piece of FND history:** Hoover's sign was described in **1908**, decades before doctors had MRI scanners. Careful observation at the bedside remains important even as imaging research advances. (*citation* [14](#citation-14))

## What changed most?

### From exclusion toward positive diagnosis

Older practice often treated functional symptoms as what remained after tests failed to find another explanation. Modern diagnosis instead looks for **positive clinical features** that support a functional diagnosis while still investigating other conditions when appropriate. Hoover's sign, distractibility, entrainment and phenotype-specific seizure assessment are examples of this broader change. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))

### From one psychological explanation toward multiple testable mechanisms

Psychological factors can matter greatly for some people, but current FND models also investigate attention, prediction, agency, learned motor patterns, sensory processing, autonomic state, prior experience and interactions between brain networks. No one mechanism has been shown to explain every FND symptom in every person. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4), [18](#citation-18))

### From one profession toward coordinated care

Neurology remains important for diagnosis and reassessment, but rehabilitation, occupational therapy, speech-language therapy, psychology, psychiatry, primary care, nursing and other specialties may each address different needs. Modern guidance increasingly describes FND care as **need-based and multidisciplinary**, not as a hand-off from one specialty to another. (*citations* [5](#citation-5), [6](#citation-6), [7](#citation-7), [8](#citation-8))

### From symptom counts toward meaningful outcomes

Recent studies increasingly measure daily function, participation, quality of life, communication, treatment burden and patient priorities rather than relying on symptom frequency alone. That does not make symptom reduction unimportant; it broadens what improvement can mean. (*citations* [9](#citation-9), [19](#citation-19), [20](#citation-20))

## Reading the recent findings carefully

**CODES (2020):** 368 adults were assigned to seizure-specific CBT plus standardized medical care or standardized medical care alone. At 12 months, the primary monthly seizure-frequency comparison was not statistically significant (incidence rate ratio 0.78; 95% confidence interval 0.56–1.09). Several secondary measures, including psychosocial function and seizure bothersomeness, favoured CBT. These were multiple comparisons without statistical correction, so the results need balanced interpretation. This was evidence about a treatment package in selected adults, not proof of what caused their seizures. (*citations* [19](#citation-19))

**Physio4FMD (2024):** 355 adults were randomized to specialist physiotherapy or community neurological physiotherapy. The main analysis included 241 after pandemic-related exclusions and other missing follow-up. The primary physical-function score at 12 months did not differ significantly (adjusted difference 3.5 points; 95% confidence interval −2.3 to 9.3). Some secondary outcomes favoured specialist care. This comparison does not establish that physiotherapy is ineffective; both groups received an active care pathway. (*citations* [20](#citation-20))

**Sensory signs (2026):** a study compared 102 people with motor FND with 75 people with recent stroke. Midline splitting of light touch did not reliably separate the groups. This is a useful reminder that familiar signs need validation. Results in motor FND cannot automatically be extended to every isolated sensory presentation. (*citations* [12](#citation-12))

**Other 2026 developments:** the tremor meta-analysis concerns the accuracy of electrophysiological tests for tremor, not a general FND biomarker. The AAN guideline addresses functional seizures, including coexisting epilepsy and continuity of care. A new diagnostic-communication review is included as a research milestone; its full methods were not available in this check, so no pooled treatment-effect claim is made here. (*citations* [3](#citation-3), [10](#citation-10), [11](#citation-11))

## Where the field is now
{: #where-the-field-is-now .shareable }

As of this selective source check on **October 1, 2026**, FND is understood as a genuine disorder of nervous-system functioning that can often be diagnosed using positive clinical features. Structural disease can coexist with FND, and new or substantially changed symptoms still require appropriate assessment. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4))

Research is now increasingly divided by symptom phenotype and clinical question. That is useful progress: evidence for functional tremor does not automatically establish the diagnosis or treatment of functional seizures, functional cognitive disorder or functional visual symptoms.

The history also shows why certainty should be used carefully. FND has repeatedly been reshaped by better observation, improved diagnostic methods, changing theories and stronger treatment studies. The current model is more evidence-based than many earlier models, but it is still developing.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This timeline is selective. It should not be read as saying that each newer theory simply replaced an incorrect older one, or that historical diagnostic labels are interchangeable with modern FND.
