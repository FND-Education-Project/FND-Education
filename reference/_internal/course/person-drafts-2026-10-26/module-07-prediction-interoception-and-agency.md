# Lesson 18 — The Predictive Brain

## For the Person With FND

### The last step that wasn't there

You're walking up a familiar staircase. Your foot lifts for one more step—and finds empty air because you're already at the top.

For an instant, your body prepared for a step that didn't exist. You may lurch forward or catch yourself awkwardly. You didn't decide to make that movement.

This familiar example from our first module shows something remarkable: the brain doesn't wait until every detail of the world is confirmed. It prepares for what is *likely* to happen next.

### Your brain is always making educated guesses

When someone throws you a ball, you move your hands toward where you expect it to arrive. You don't need to stop and calculate the exact path.

Your brain combines **incoming information**—what your eyes and body are telling it now—with **predictions** based on past experience. Usually, these work together well. If the ball changes direction, you correct your movement.

Researchers call one family of explanations for this process **predictive processing**. It helps explain ordinary perception and action. It is not a claim that your brain simply invents reality.

And a prediction isn't necessarily a conscious thought. You cannot prevent your brain from making predictions just by deciding to think differently.

### What might prediction have to do with FND?

Some researchers propose that the balance between expectations and incoming sensory information may be altered in certain FND symptoms. Scientists study this through behaviour, examination findings and brain-network research.

That is a **model under investigation**, not a test that can identify the cause of your own symptoms. There is no good basis for saying, “You expected to be sick, so you made yourself sick.” That would confuse an automatic process with a deliberate choice.

### Learning to use the idea

Consider a task that feels unpredictable. Maybe a crowded doorway makes it hard to judge where to move next. Instead of trying to change what your brain predicts, you might reduce uncertainty by choosing a familiar route, making the path clearer or arranging enough time.

This adaptation may help because it changes the demands of the activity. You don't have to prove the mechanism.

**Something to think about:** What does your nervous system normally anticipate successfully—catching an object, finishing a familiar phrase, navigating a familiar room?

**One small step:** Notice one helpful prediction in ordinary life. You could simply think, “I knew where the door handle would be without studying it.” No need to deliberately trigger symptoms to explore the concept.

Next we will meet the body's many sources of information—and the difference between a signal and what it means.

# Lesson 19 — Interoception, Proprioception, and Outside Senses

## For the Person With FND

### How did you know you were hungry?

You might notice a hollow feeling, a change in energy, a thought about food, or even irritability before you recognise hunger. Those clues don't all feel the same.

Your nervous system gathers information from inside your body as well as from the world around you. Different systems help you understand different things.

**Interoception** involves sensing and interpreting internal conditions, such as hunger, breathing, heartbeat or bladder fullness.

**Proprioception** helps you know where your limbs are and how they are moving, even with your eyes closed.

**Exteroception** concerns information from the outside world, including sight, sound and touch.

These systems cooperate constantly. When you reach for a cup, you see where it is, sense your arm's position and may notice whether you're tired or uncomfortable.

### A feeling isn't a full explanation

Suppose your heart feels as though it's racing. That experience matters. But the feeling alone can't tell you why it's happening. Physical activity, medication effects, dehydration, an abnormal heart rhythm or other conditions may need consideration.

The same is true of dizziness, numbness or a feeling of being disconnected from a limb. Knowing the *kind* of information involved helps you describe the symptom. It doesn't establish a diagnosis.

And no one can consciously monitor every signal. Sometimes you only notice fatigue when you stop a demanding activity. Sometimes a sensation keeps drawing your attention. These experiences vary widely, both with and without FND.

### Why this belongs in our course

Scientists are studying whether differences in body-signal processing and interpretation contribute to some FND experiences. These are active areas of research, not proof that everyone with FND misreads their body.

Understanding interoception might still help you ask a useful question: “Is this a sensation I'm noticing, a measurement someone has taken, a difficulty controlling movement, or several things together?”

That distinction can make medical conversations clearer.

### Respecting the body you have

If you find it difficult to notice early fatigue, planning rest may be useful. If certain sensory input is overwhelming, a quiet space or adjusted lighting may make an activity more accessible. Neither adaptation requires you to label the underlying cause.

You should not practise holding your breath, ignoring breathing trouble or pushing through a concerning symptom in order to test body awareness.

**Something to think about:** When you say “I feel strange,” what sort of information are you trying to describe: inside-the-body sensation, body position, awareness, or something outside you?

**One small step:** Try replacing one vague description with one precise, ordinary phrase. “My right foot feels distant,” for example, says more than “My leg is weird.” You don't have to decide why it happens.

# Lesson 20 — Salience, Arousal, and Feedback Loops

## For the Person With FND

### Why do some things demand attention?

Imagine being in a room full of conversation. Someone across the room says your name. Suddenly that voice stands out.

The sound didn't necessarily get louder. Your brain recognised it as important.

**Salience** is the importance the brain gives to a piece of information. A pain, a sudden movement or a sound you weren't expecting can become important quickly.

**Arousal** describes a person's level of physiological and mental readiness. It can rise during exercise, excitement, illness, pain, surprise or fear. Arousal is not the same as anxiety, and it does not automatically mean something is wrong.

### The two-way street

Suppose you're exhausted after a long appointment. Ordinary noises seem louder. That makes it harder to follow a conversation, and the effort of trying to listen leaves you even more tired.

Here, several things affect each other. We might call this a **feedback loop**.

Some loops help us. Drinking water when we're thirsty helps restore balance. Other loops can make an unpleasant experience harder to manage for a while.

FND researchers study whether interactions among attention, bodily arousal, prediction and movement or awareness systems help sustain certain symptoms. These ideas are still being tested and may not apply to everyone's symptoms.

A feedback loop is **not** evidence that someone thought themselves into illness. It also isn't a reason to dismiss a fever, fainting, breathlessness or another medical concern.

### More than one way to ease the load

Imagine the busy appointment again. A quieter waiting place, written instructions or a shorter visit might reduce the demand on your attention. A rest period afterward might make the day more manageable. These are practical choices even when we don't know what caused the symptoms.

We can ask, “What keeps making this situation harder?” without assuming that changing one part of it will cure a neurological disorder.

**Something to think about:** What makes a room, conversation or activity feel demanding—noise, lighting, uncertainty, physical effort, pain, something else?

**One small step:** Choose one source of unnecessary strain you can safely reduce. If you have a medical condition affecting breathing, heart rate or blood pressure, discuss changes and measurements with your clinician instead of attributing them to FND.

# Lesson 21 — Agency, Learning, and Your Own Adaptations

## For the Person With FND

### “That didn't feel like me.”

You move your hand to pick up a glass. Usually, you experience the movement as something you intended and carried out.

Now imagine your arm jerking without warning. Even though it's your arm, the movement doesn't feel chosen. People with FND may describe movements, sounds or other experiences that feel outside their usual control.

Scientists call the sense that **“I caused this action”** a *sense of agency*. It is different from whether the movement really happened, whether you own your body, or whether you are responsible for an action.

Agency is a subject of active FND research. An altered sense of control is real as an experience; it cannot establish a mechanism or diagnosis by itself.

### What about learning?

Think of learning a route through a new building. At first, you read every sign. After several visits, the route becomes familiar. Learning has changed how much work the journey takes.

The nervous system can learn patterns of movement, attention and expectation. Researchers investigate how learning may influence functional symptoms, although they cannot assume the same pattern developed in everyone.

Learning is not the same as pretending. It doesn't mean you chose a symptom or that you can simply unlearn it on command.

### Bringing the ideas together

We have explored attention, automatic actions, predictions, body signals, salience, arousal and agency. That's quite a cast of characters.

The point isn't to work out which single character is “to blame.” It is to develop better questions.

Here's an example. Imagine that answering questions is hard during an appointment. You might wonder whether unexpected questions, fatigue, several people talking or word-finding difficulties make the task more demanding. You don't have to choose one cause.

You could ask for written questions, extra time or a quieter room. These changes are useful possibilities because they make communication more accessible—not because they prove a particular brain theory.

### A small way to reason through adaptations

**Understand:** What ordinary brain or body function have I learned about?

**Observe:** What actually happens in my life, without exaggeration or self-testing?

**Consider:** What are some possibilities? What remains unknown? Does something need medical assessment?

**Adapt:** What safe, reversible adjustment might make the task more possible?

**Review:** Did it help safety, effort, independence or enjoyment? If not, can I leave it behind?

**Something to think about:** Is there an adaptation you've already found that makes an important part of life easier? You may have learned something useful long before you knew the scientific words.

**One small step:** Take one existing accommodation and finish the sentence: “This helps me because it makes _____ easier.” That answer matters, even if you cannot explain the precise biology.
