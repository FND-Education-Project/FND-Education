# Lesson 28 — What Uses Your Available Capacity?

## For the Person With FND

### You haven't moved much. So why are you exhausted?

Imagine spending an hour filling out forms for an appointment. You haven't lifted a box or climbed a flight of stairs. Yet when you're done, you feel worn out.

That can be confusing—especially if someone asks what you did all morning.

Physical activity is only one kind of demand. Thinking, listening, reading, dealing with noise, managing pain, making decisions and being around people can require effort too.

Earlier in the course, we introduced **available capacity** as a way to picture how much you can manage before you need to change tasks or recover. It's a useful planning idea, not a laboratory measure or an exact number of energy units.

### One day isn't every day

The same activity may feel manageable on Tuesday and overwhelming on Wednesday.

Maybe Tuesday came after better sleep. Maybe Wednesday began with a migraine or an unexpectedly busy phone call. You may not know why the difference happened.

Variable ability does not make yesterday's success false or today's difficulty less real.

Capacity also depends on *how* you do something. Ordering groceries on a quiet phone may cost less effort than navigating a crowded store. Using a chair to prepare food might leave room for a conversation afterward. An adaptation can protect participation rather than merely reduce activity.

### Activities have different costs—and different values

A shower, a medical appointment and an enjoyable visit may all take effort. But they aren't interchangeable in what they mean to you.

A plan that only protects chores but leaves no room for pleasure isn't necessarily a good plan. Nor should every enjoyable activity come with guilt.

Sometimes a support or accessible tool makes the same activity less demanding. Sometimes another health condition—such as a sleep disorder, migraine or orthostatic problem—requires treatment in its own right.

### You don't need to measure everything

Constant counting can become a job of its own. If a pattern doesn't help you make a decision, you don't have to keep tracking it.

**Something to think about:** What costs you energy that other people may not notice? What activities are worth protecting because they give something back to your life?

**One small step:** Pick one ordinary demand that could be made easier. You might break a form into sections, lower background noise or use a sitting position for a task. The goal is not to prove that your capacity increased. The goal is to make a day more livable.

# Lesson 29 — Flexible Pacing Without Boom-and-Bust

## For the Person With FND

### The good day trap

You wake up feeling a little better. Finally! There are dishes to wash, laundry to finish, friends to call and things you haven't touched in weeks.

You tackle as much as you can. At the time it may feel wonderful. Later—or the next day—you feel much worse.

Many people recognise a **boom-and-bust pattern**: an unusually high-activity period followed by a period of increased symptoms or reduced function.

A community member put it simply:

> “I feel good, I’ll do it all—only to crash out later and feel awful.”

*Community quotation candidate — one person's experience of activity and worsening.* [Public source](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1qlwz51/exercise_and_fnd/). Original wording/context must be rechecked.

### What pacing really means

**Pacing** is planning activity and recovery around what is realistic for you. It isn't an instruction to do as little as possible. Nor is it a rule that each day must contain more activity than the day before.

A plan might leave room for higher-capacity and lower-capacity days. It might keep a little time available for food, rest and something enjoyable rather than using every good moment to catch up on chores.

The plan can be flexible: “On a difficult day, this is enough. On a better day, I may do more if it is safe and sustainable.”

### Pay attention to delayed worsening

Sometimes symptoms increase during an activity. Sometimes they worsen hours or days later. The delay matters.

**Post-exertional malaise**, seen in conditions such as ME/CFS, is more than ordinary tiredness; it may involve delayed, disproportionate and prolonged worsening after physical, cognitive or other exertion. If that pattern fits your experience, ask for appropriate assessment and condition-specific guidance rather than assuming a graded increase in activity is safe.

Not all activity-related worsening is post-exertional malaise, and not all fatigue comes from FND. There is no single pacing schedule that fits every person.

### Pacing doesn't have to become surveillance

You might find one rough guideline helpful: “After a demanding appointment, don't schedule another demanding task right away.” You don't need an app full of numbers unless it genuinely helps.

**Something to think about:** Does a better day sometimes lead to a harder day afterward? Are there activities you wish you could save room for?

**One small step:** Choose one part of a day where you could leave a bit of breathing room. If you already have a sensible routine, keep it. This lesson isn't asking you to rebuild your schedule.

# Lesson 30 — Balancing Practice, Rest, and Everyday Life

## For the Person With FND

### There's only one day to spend

Imagine your care team recommends some practice for a movement difficulty. You also need to eat, attend an appointment, spend time with someone you love, and perhaps recover from yesterday.

The practice could be useful. It could also take up some of the capacity you need for the rest of life.

That is why rehabilitation and pacing belong next to one another in this course. **A useful exercise still needs to fit the person who is doing it.**

### Different kinds of progress

Sometimes progress means an activity becomes easier. Sometimes it means learning to stop before an avoidable injury or prolonged worsening. Sometimes it means finding an accommodation that lets you participate now.

None of these outcomes requires a steady upward line on a chart.

If you are in rehabilitation, a helpful review may ask: Is the activity useful? What happens afterward? Do I need a different dose, schedule, method, aid or treatment? Is another medical condition affecting how I respond?

If an activity repeatedly makes you much worse or causes harm, tell the treating clinician. Pushing harder isn't automatically the answer.

### Plans are allowed to change

An earlier version of you may have had different abilities. You might also have a day when the plan works and another when it doesn't.

A plan is a tool, not a promise. You can adjust it as life changes.

One member of the community described trying to protect a balance:

> “It’s good to test out boundaries but listen to your body!!”

*Community quotation candidate — personal opinion about limits; not an exercise prescription.* [Public source](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1ipf4gt/is_this_really_the_only_way/). Verify original context before publication.

For some people, experimentation needs professional supervision, and for others with delayed worsening even seemingly modest increases may be too much. The quote is a reminder that **the person's response matters**, not an instruction to push.

### Making room for living

You may want to use some capacity to practise a skill, and some to enjoy the results of having that skill—or simply to do something that matters today.

That's not a failure of commitment. Rehabilitation exists to serve your life, not to replace it.

**Something to think about:** Does your current routine leave room for something you care about besides appointments, chores and symptom management?

**One small step:** Name one activity you'd like to protect. It might be talking with a friend, watching a show comfortably, time outside or something entirely different. Then ask what would make space for it. A support, rest period or change of priorities may be as important as another exercise.
