# Lesson 22 — Finding Words for What Happens

## For the Person With FND

### “It did that thing again.”

Have you ever tried to explain an episode and discovered that the useful words have disappeared?

Maybe your arm felt heavy. Maybe your legs wouldn't cooperate. Maybe you heard someone speaking but couldn't answer. Perhaps you remember the beginning of an event and nothing else until later.

You know something happened. Describing it can be much harder.

The good news is that **you don't need medical terminology to describe your experience well**.

### Begin with what actually happened

Imagine two people who say, “My hand stopped working.”

One means, “My grip became weak, and I dropped the cup.”

The other means, “My fingers curled tight, and I couldn't straighten them.”

Neither account is wrong. But the details invite different questions.

It can help to describe movement, strength, feeling, awareness, time and recovery separately. Was something shaking, jerking or stiffening? Did a limb feel weak or stuck? Could you hear people? Could you answer them? Did you remember what happened later? Was the symptom brief or still present hours afterward?

You don't need to fill in every blank.

Someone watching may report that you didn't answer. You may remember hearing their voice. **Responsiveness** is what other people could observe; **awareness** is what you experienced. Later memory is another piece of the story.

One community member described another difficulty:

> “I can’t really tell the difference between whether something happened two weeks ago or four months ago.”

*Community quotation candidate — a lived experience of memory difficulties, not a diagnosis.* [Public source](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1mnw4fd/dae_have_functional_cognitive_disorder/). Wording and surrounding context require original-source verification.

If your memory is patchy, it's fine to say, “I'm not sure.” An agreed supporter note or previous symptom map can help without becoming a daily examination.

### Describing isn't diagnosing

Two people can describe the same sensation and have different medical conditions. Even a very detailed account can't replace clinical assessment. New, severe or clearly changed symptoms still need the medical judgment discussed in Module 5.

**Something to think about:** Is there a symptom that you usually describe only as “weird,” “shaky” or “not working”?

**One small step:** Write one ordinary sentence beginning, “What I notice is ...” If your Module 4 map already says it well, leave it alone. You're not starting over.

# Lesson 23 — Similar Experiences, Different Features

## For the Person With FND

### “Dizzy” can mean several things

Imagine three people telling a doctor they feel dizzy.

One says the room seems to spin. Another feels close to fainting after standing. The third feels unsteady among bright shelves in a supermarket.

They have all used an honest word. But the experiences are not identical, and the appropriate medical questions may be different.

That's the skill we'll practise: **notice differences in symptoms without choosing their cause**.

### Compare what happens, not what you think it is

A shaking hand might move rhythmically, suddenly jerk or make repeated brief movements. People use words such as *tremor*, *jerk* and *tic* to describe different patterns. These words are useful starting points; the full history and examination still matter.

“I couldn't move” can also mean several things. Perhaps a leg had almost no strength. Perhaps it felt fixed in one posture. Maybe a movement wouldn't begin, or the inability to move happened during an episode of altered awareness.

Or consider a fall. Functional seizures, epileptic seizures, fainting and other events can sometimes look similar. The beginning, responsiveness, breathing, observed movements, time course and recovery may all matter. No single feature makes a safe diagnosis, and a home recording needs expert context.

This is why our Reference Library includes **scope boundaries**. An article about a functional symptom tells you what *that article* covers. It does not decide what happened to you.

### How the previous modules help

We now know words such as *attention*, *automatic movement* and *interoception*. We can use them to describe how a task feels, but they cannot tell us whether a collapse was a functional seizure, faint or something else.

If a symptom is new, dangerous or substantially different, do not delay urgent assessment to search for its matching description.

**Something to think about:** When you say weak, dizzy, absent or foggy, what exactly are you trying to describe?

**One small step:** Add one concrete detail to one familiar symptom. For instance, “The room seems to turn when I move my head,” or “I feel faint after I stand.” That is more useful than attempting a diagnosis.

# Lesson 24 — Finding Your Way Through the Reference Library

## For the Person With FND

### The library is not a diagnostic quiz

Imagine asking a librarian for “the book about shaking.” They would probably ask a few questions before choosing a shelf.

Our Reference Library works much the same way. You can start with your own words and explore more than one relevant page.

The goal isn't to keep clicking until a diagnosis appears. The goal is to **understand possible descriptions and arrive at better questions**.

### Three places to begin

The [Diagnostic Signs Library](https://github.com/FND-Education-Project/FND-Education/tree/main/reference/diagnostic-signs) describes how clinicians evaluate particular functional presentations, including other explanations that may need consideration.

The [Recovery Techniques Library](https://github.com/FND-Education-Project/FND-Education/tree/main/reference/recovery-techniques) describes possible care and rehabilitation approaches, their limits and relevant safety information.

The [Co-occurring Conditions Library](https://github.com/FND-Education-Project/FND-Education/tree/main/reference/co-occurring-conditions) describes conditions that may accompany FND or share some symptoms with it.

Each symptom page has separate reading paths for the **Person**, **Supporters**, and **Clinicians**. You can read the part that helps you. The technical section is there when you need it, not as a test you must pass.

### Look for what the page does—and doesn't—cover

Suppose you have episodes where your legs shake and you lose your balance. You might explore pages about tremor, gait problems or falls. You may also need assessment for a different medical condition.

First read **Refers to**: what sorts of experiences belong on the page? Then look for a **scope boundary**: what does the page leave for another topic?

A recovery method being described isn't proof it works. Someone's story can show what mattered to them without predicting your outcome.

### When several pages fit

Sometimes a few descriptions sound familiar. That's not a failure to pick correctly.

You might ask your care team, “I noticed these experiences overlap. Which features are important in my assessment, and what is still uncertain?”

**Something to think about:** Would you rather begin from a symptom word, an everyday description or a problem in daily life?

**One small step:** Choose one previously assessed symptom and visit one relevant page. Read the opening and scope boundary. You can stop there. One useful question is worth more than an exhausting afternoon of searching.

Next comes rehabilitation. We'll explore how learning and practice may help, without assuming every symptom needs the same approach.
