# Lesson 28 — What Uses Your Available Capacity?

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### “Tired” is a description, not a diagnosis

Available capacity is useful planning language, not a measured pool of neuronal energy. Begin by asking whether the person means sleepiness, muscle fatigability, cognitive strain, pain-limited performance, sensory overload, orthostatic symptoms, post-event recovery, delayed symptom exacerbation or several phenomena together. The same visible task can impose very different demands depending on setting, medical comorbidity, cognitive load and accessibility.

**OT** can use activity analysis to identify standing demands, executive load, sensory input, communication barriers and transport or caregiver reliance. **Physiotherapy** can identify safe movement and endurance goals while accounting for fall risk, coexisting motor disability and whether exertion is tolerated. **Psychology/neuropsychology** can address adjustment, attentional load and wellbeing without treating all fatigue as anxiety or avoidance. **Sleep, pain, migraine, autonomic and primary-care specialists** should evaluate other relevant conditions in their own right.

### Consider symptom and condition interactions without collapsing diagnoses

A person may have functional gait disorder, migraine, insomnia and POTS. An appointment may cost mobility effort, sensory tolerance, concentration and upright time. Improvement in one domain can make the appointment more manageable without proving it was the mechanism of every other domain.

Similarly, **functional seizures** may have prolonged recovery or surrounding flares that differ from the visible episode; a person with **FCD** may spend substantial effort processing instructions despite an acceptable performance on a short test. **Functional visual symptoms** or **PPPD** may make lighting and visually busy travel settings particularly burdensome. **Functional speech symptoms** can add a high communication cost when a visit uses repeated questioning.

Use the [co-occurring conditions collection](../../../co-occurring-conditions/README.md) to review fatigue, migraine, persistent pain, fibromyalgia, sleep, tinnitus, IBS and autonomic complaints without claiming they are all FND symptoms. New or changing symptoms require separate clinical assessment.

### A practical plan for the care team

Ask the person to identify one activity and one constraint. Arrange the most important communication at a time and in a format they can use. Define whether the goal is reduced fatigue, increased reliability, access to an essential task or preserving capacity for something pleasurable. Avoid mandatory minute-by-minute diaries or unsupported “battery” scores.

**Evidence boundary:** FND occupational-therapy recommendations concern individualized function and rehabilitation planning [R15](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r15). ME/CFS energy-management guidance [R17](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r17) is condition-specific and cannot be presented as a validated FND theory of fatigue.

# Lesson 29 — Flexible Pacing When Capacity Changes

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Establish the time course before prescribing progression

A boom-and-bust description may identify alternating periods of activity and worsening, but it does not establish the cause of either phase. Ask what activity involved—physical, cognitive, emotional, sensory or upright demands—whether worsening was immediate or delayed, its duration, whether the person returned to baseline and how often the pattern occurs. Consider sleep, pain, migraine, medications, anemia, endocrine conditions, dysautonomia, depression and relevant other illness rather than assuming deconditioning alone.

**Physiotherapy and occupational therapy** should coordinate activity plans so therapy sessions, transfers, household tasks and travel do not unknowingly compete for the same practical tolerance. **Psychology** may address guilt, uncertainty, fear and adaptation in a consent-based way, rather than explain prolonged physical worsening as avoidance or lack of motivation. **Primary care or relevant specialists** assess possible ME/CFS or another contributor where delayed and disproportionate post-exertional symptom exacerbation suggests it.

### Pacing is not one standard exercise dose

The goal is to match activity to medical safety, tolerability and the person's priorities. Options include changing the environment, distributing effort, choosing supports, shortening the task, using planned rest or selecting an alternative method. Progression—if appropriate at all—is an individualized clinical decision.

When **post-exertional malaise (PEM)** is reported, especially if ME/CFS is suspected or established, assess the delayed, disproportionate and potentially prolonged nature of worsening and respect condition-specific energy-management guidance. Do not prescribe automatic fixed increments, use a successful single clinic session as clearance for increased activity or require deliberate provocation of a crash. NICE guidance on ME/CFS does not, by itself, invalidate individualized rehabilitation in FND without PEM; the two clinical questions must be kept separate.

### A case for a joint team plan

Someone with assessed functional weakness also reports exertion-related headache, light sensitivity and next-day exhaustion. PT could tailor movement to a safe function; OT could reduce task and sensory demands; the clinician responsible for migraine and fatigue could reassess other treatable causes. The patient chooses which activities deserve priority. The team watches whether the plan makes ordinary life more sustainable—not merely whether therapy attendance increased.

**Evidence boundary:** Pacing for FND relies heavily on individualized multidisciplinary and OT consensus, not a validated flare-prevention dose [R15](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r15). NICE ME/CFS recommendations apply specifically to that disease [R17](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r17). Research citations and exact current guideline language must be verified before publication.

# Lesson 30 — Balancing Rehabilitation, Rest, and Change

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Treat the person's week, not the therapy session alone

A treatment session may appear tolerable when considered in isolation but become unsustainable when the person also travels, cooks, manages medicines, cares for others or recovers from prior episodes. Ask about the whole day and delayed effects. Participation and quality of life are independent goals; a technically improved performance that makes an ordinary week unmanageable is not a complete rehabilitation success.

**OT** may map the sequence of daily activities and available supports. **PT** reviews the dose, movement strategy, safety and functional transfer. **SLT** adjusts session communication or swallowing/voice task demands as relevant. **Psychology/neuropsychology** can help people and families make realistic decisions without making every setback a behavioural failure. **Neurology/primary care** provide ongoing medical reassessment when symptoms change.

### Work through a review with the person

A concise shared review has five questions:

1. **What matters now?** The person may value independent meals, visiting a friend, safer transport or shorter recovery from a familiar episode more than one specific test score.
2. **What did the plan actually change?** Compare function, symptom impact, equipment need, falls/injuries, confidence and support burden, not only exercise repetitions.
3. **What did it cost?** Discuss immediate and next-day pain, fatigue, cognitive fog, migraine or post-event problems. Keep PEM and other medical conditions on the assessment pathway when relevant.
4. **What needs adjusting?** Consider timing, task simplification, environment, accessible transport, less frequent sessions, clinical referral or stopping an ineffective component.
5. **Who will review it?** Specify the owner and a meaningful interval or change trigger, including urgent-care boundaries from Module 5.

### Recovery does not invalidate continuing disability support

A person can make meaningful gains and still need a wheelchair, written reminders, sensory protection, communication alternatives or assistance with some activities. The ethical risk of withdrawing access before an alternative is reliable belongs in treatment planning. A lack of improvement should prompt reassessment of goals, fit, complications, evidence and resources—not blame or automatic discharge.

Consider a person who manages a supported therapy task but needs several days to recover. The team should not infer everyday independence from the isolated task or automatically intensify practice. It should establish what the session adds to or subtracts from the person's life, while preserving appropriate assessment of any new medical changes.

### Close the module with a shared skill

Model one conversation in which the patient and clinicians jointly choose **keep, modify, replace or discontinue** a component and document why. A plan that is respectful, safe, clinically defensible and usable is a valuable outcome even when the person continues to have substantial symptoms.

**Evidence boundary:** Patient-centred task and environmental adaptation are grounded in OT consensus [R15](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r15). Physio4FMD shows the importance of interpreting clinical and functional outcomes separately [R13](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r13). PEM-related safeguards require condition-specific guidance [R17](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r17); none establishes a universal rest-to-practice ratio.
