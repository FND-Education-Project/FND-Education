# Lesson 22 — Finding Words for What Happens

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Start with description before attribution

A person may use “paralyzed,” “shaking,” “dizzy,” “absent” or “can't speak” to describe several different phenomena. Rather than translate these immediately into a single diagnostic label, establish the patient's meaning and the witnessed pattern: onset, sequence, context, course, duration, awareness, responsiveness, memory, breathing, recovery, injury and effect on daily function. Record which parts the patient remembers, which were observed, and which are unknown.

For episodic events, a useful question is “What happened first?” followed by “What changed next?” and “When did the person return to their usual state?” If a video exists and was obtained safely with consent, understand whether it shows the onset, main event or recovery. A witness's report of unresponsiveness does not establish lack of awareness, and subsequent amnesia does not establish that no responses occurred.

### Listen to the language of the affected activity

**Neurology** should document any relevant positive signs and meaningful differential diagnosis—not merely rule out disease with normal tests. **OT** can translate symptom descriptions into safety, reliability, exertion and support needs across real tasks. **Physiotherapy** can document the observed motor pattern and function under appropriately safe conditions. **SLT** can distinguish intelligibility, word retrieval, phonation, swallowing and communication access. **Psychology/neuropsychology** can help clarify cognitive load, dissociation, distress and context without turning any of these into the presumed cause.

For example, “couldn't lift my arm” may involve strength, initiation, pain, posture or altered awareness. “Couldn't answer” may involve speech production, comprehension, motor response, attention, seizure-like activity or post-event state. Do not infer the complete clinical picture from one shared word.

### When a shorter record is better

Record two or three decision-relevant details and the patient's priorities, not a requirement for constant monitoring. Existing notes, the Module 4 symptom map, and a chosen supporter may be sufficient. A shorter description with explicit uncertainty can be more useful than an overly precise chronology reconstructed from absent memory.

**Teaching example (fictional):** “I heard my partner ask a question but could not reply; I remember a few minutes and then a gap. My partner noticed that I did not respond and had trouble moving. Recovery took much longer than the visible episode.” The next task is to identify the separately reported features and assess risks, not to choose between a functional or epileptic explanation by appearance.

**Evidence boundary:** Positive FND diagnosis relies on relevant positive signs, context and appropriate investigation, while event diagnosis is specific to the episode type. [Bennett clinical review, R10](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r10) · [AAN functional-seizure guidance, R11](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r11). Use the [diagnostic index](../../../diagnostic-signs/README.md) for technique-specific limits.

# Lesson 23 — Similar Experiences, Different Features

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Differential diagnosis is a disciplined comparison—not an exclusion ritual

The patient should learn to distinguish observable *features*, while clinicians retain responsibility for assessing *causes*. Functional signs and other medical conditions may coexist. An examination sign is meaningful only where it has sufficient specificity and reliability for the presentation under investigation.

Use a short case with incomplete information and ask three questions: **What is observed? What remains unknown? What would change assessment or urgent management?** Avoid giving a correct diagnosis based on a story alone.

### Three teachable cases

**Case A — A movement called “shaking.”** A person reports repeated hand movements, sudden jerks and occasional limb stiffening. Neurology may need to distinguish tremor, jerks, tic-like phenomena, dystonia or another movement disorder, based on phenomenology, medications, examination and appropriate tests. Movement-disorders clinicians can interpret positive findings in context; PT/OT then use the confirmed presentation and functional goals to select corresponding recovery techniques. A rhythmic task response alone does not classify all movements.

**Case B — An episode called “passing out.”** The person has falls, apparent unresponsiveness and uncertain recall. Evaluate syncope (including potential cardiac causes), epileptic events, functional seizures, drop attacks and other relevant medical explanations using the whole event and targeted tests. A reported warning, silence or remembered speech cannot by itself confirm any one category. An event in water, abnormal breathing, injury, new focal deficit or medical instability changes immediate safety decisions; individualized episode instructions do not replace acute medical care.

**Case C — “Dizzy, foggy, and can't see properly.”** Describe rotation versus faintness versus disequilibrium, upright/motion/visual triggers, headache, light sensitivity, visual phenomena and cognitive complaints. Possible contributing disorders include vestibular conditions, PPPD, migraine, autonomic/cardiac illness, visual disease, FCD or several together. Vestibular PT, OT and ophthalmology/neurology may be needed; psychotherapeutic support cannot substitute for omitted ocular or cardiovascular assessment.

### Make teaching the differential safer and more useful

After each case, describe why a *particular kind of information* is clinically useful. For example, knowing whether a fall followed standing is useful for a blood-pressure/rhythm work-up; it does not mean every standing fall is syncope. Knowing a tremor changes with rhythm is an examination observation; it does not establish the person's control over it.

**Psychologists** can help with the burden of uncertainty and repeated medical experiences. They should not be tasked with deciding neurological etiology based on a person's stress history. **OT and PT** can provide access and injury reduction while the diagnostic process continues.

**Evidence boundary:** The specific 17 presentation pages in [Diagnostic Signs](../../../diagnostic-signs/README.md) and current source-guided neurological assessment govern interpretation; the fictional cases are pedagogical, not validated triage algorithms. # Lesson 24 — Navigate the Reference Library and Ask Better Questions

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Translate a positive diagnosis into a usable referral and technique choice

An FND label without a clearly identified presentation does little to guide rehabilitation. After explaining the observed evidence, define the care question: which symptom, what task is affected, which coexisting conditions matter, what can be tried safely, what needs specialist input, and what outcome the person values?

**Step 1 — Diagnostic fit.** Start with the [Diagnostic Signs overview](../../../diagnostic-signs/README.md). Review “Refers to,” scope boundaries, positive signs and appropriate differentials. Some pages describe descriptive observations or investigation for alternative disease, not independent diagnostic tests. Do not simply count positive-sign entries.

**Step 2 — Technique fit.** Choose an entry from the corresponding [Recovery Techniques index](../../../recovery-techniques/technique-index.md), noting its evidence label (trial, guideline, consensus, emerging, or lived-experience report as indicated), prerequisites and contraindications. A technique demonstrated for functional tremor should not silently become the default treatment for seizures, paralysis, swallowing or functional cognition.

**Step 3 — Profession and care setting.** PT may lead movement or gait retraining when appropriate; OT may lead task and accessibility adaptation; SLT may lead speech, voice, swallowing or upper-airway intervention; psychology may offer evidence-informed seizure-focused or other agreed treatment, where indicated. Neurology, primary care, emergency care, vestibular/eye specialists and others address diagnosis, comorbidities and risk. These are examples of roles, not rigid ownership or substitutes for the patient's preferences.

**Step 4 — Measure what matters.** Document baseline function, safety, symptom burden, confidence, access and quality of life. Describe which clinician is responsible for review and when changed symptoms require reassessment. A response to any chosen technique does not prove or disprove the initial diagnosis.

### Worked example — from diagnosis to rehabilitation discussion

A person with an assessed functional tremor wants to use kitchen utensils. The team could review the tremor diagnostic overview, then discuss a task-oriented or external-focus recovery page with PT/OT. They might change grip or environment, agree on a manageable goal and review actual utensil use and post-task effects. If the person instead describes repeated falls with altered awareness, the team should **change reference pathways and safety assessment**, not recycle tremor retraining.

### Accessible shared decision-making

Offer one or two selected links and their reasons, rather than a catalogue of dozens. For patients with memory difficulties, provide a brief written summary, including what is confirmed, what is uncertain and who will act next. For supporters, obtain consent before sharing. Recognize when the reference contains consensus rather than comparative efficacy evidence, or when a technique depends on equipment, trained personnel or services that the person cannot access.

**Evidence boundary:** The Reference Library is a clinically oriented working collection, not a validated point-of-care decision support system. Its human and specialist reviews remain ongoing. Positive diagnosis and safe ongoing assessment are supported by the [clinical review](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r10) and [phenotype-specific guideline](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r11); intervention selection needs its own supporting studies or consensus.
