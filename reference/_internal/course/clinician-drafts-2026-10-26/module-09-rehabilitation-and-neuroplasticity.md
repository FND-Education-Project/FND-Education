# Lesson 25 — How Rehabilitation Uses Learning

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### From a mechanism to a rehabilitation principle

Neuroplasticity denotes experience-dependent changes in neural processing and skill; it is not a specific intervention, a guaranteed recovery trajectory or retrospective proof that a symptom arose through learning. Successful treatment does not validate a particular proposed causal model. The practical clinical question is narrower: **what process, function or participation goal can be addressed, with what evidence and safeguards?**

Motor-FND rehabilitation consensus describes explanation, redirected attention, automatic movement and meaningful functional practice. Occupational-therapy consensus describes activity analysis, adaptations and participation. Speech-language guidance addresses communication, swallowing and upper-airway phenotypes. These recommendations differ in evidence level and relevant populations; a shared label of “retraining” must not blur them.

**Physiotherapy:** Where positive motor signs and assessment support a functional movement presentation, consider goal-directed movement in appropriately protected tasks. Measure actual transfers, walking reliability or hand use over time; a smoother movement in clinic is only one observation.

**Occupational therapy:** Identify how sensory input, sequencing, cognition, pain, equipment and the environment alter success with a valued activity. Access aids and task modifications can be outcomes themselves, not rewards withheld until motor improvement.

**Speech-language therapy:** Separate communication, phonation, swallowing and airway needs. Use appropriate assessment before adapting speech or swallowing practice; retain accessible communication and nutrition protection even when active retraining is chosen.

**Psychology/psychiatry:** Psychological interventions may help certain presentations, distress, adaptation and coexisting conditions, based on the individual's goals and appropriate evidence. Psychological treatment does not establish a psychological cause.

**Neurology/primary care:** Provide a clear positive diagnosis with its certainty and continuing review of coexisting disease; coordinate referrals and medication indications rather than discharge the patient to a single service.

### Demonstrate the principle across different symptom goals

For assessed functional limb weakness or gait disorder, ask whether an externally cued, supported functional task better meets the person's mobility goal than repeating isolated strength tests. For diagnosed speech/voice symptoms, ask whether a chosen communication goal and SLP-guided alternative makes an interaction usable. For FCD, a reliable written prompt or activity sequence may reduce errors now, whether or not cognition changes with rehabilitation. For functional seizures, individual safety and possible seizure-focused psychological programmes have a different evidence base and cannot be inferred from motor learning studies.

Each example illustrates clinical reasoning; technique eligibility and actual procedures remain in the appropriate [Recovery Techniques collection](../../../recovery-techniques/README.md).

### Communicate uncertainty constructively

Avoid both “the brain can change, so everyone can recover” and “the best evidence is incomplete, so there's nothing to offer.” Instead explain the type of evidence, the goal, the access requirements and the agreed evaluation period. Document baseline symptoms, functional impact, participation, adverse effects and patient preference.

**Evidence basis:** General motor plasticity review [R12](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r12), motor consensus [R01](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r01), OT consensus [R15](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r15), SLT consensus [R14](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r14). # Lesson 26 — Choosing and Adjusting Meaningful Practice

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Design around a goal, not the diagnosis alone

Two people with a similar movement label may need quite different approaches because one wants safe transfers while the other wants to cook independently. Clarify the requested activity and current constraints before choosing an exercise.

A workable plan states: **task, starting conditions, cue or adaptation, assistance, safety threshold, intended outcome and review point**. Ask what can be maintained on a difficult day, not just during a successful session.

### Cross-disciplinary worked example — preparing lunch

A person with an assessed functional upper-limb movement disorder wants to make lunch. The **OT** identifies seating, reach, knife safety, grip, sequence, sensory load and opportunities for assistance. The **PT** assesses any relevant balance or motor task demands and selects professionally guided movement options. A **psychologist**, if invited and clinically indicated, can work with understandable fear after previous injuries, not demand that the patient think positively to regain movement. The team agrees whether success means safe participation, reduced effort, fewer dropped objects or less post-task worsening.

Changing a handle, avoiding a dangerous task or using prepared food are legitimate results. An externally focused cue may suit one assessed symptom, while another condition requires different specialist care.

### Choose a phenotype-appropriate path

For tremor, movement retraining might use a meaningful target or selected rhythmic cue. For painful fixed dystonia, supported positioning and complication prevention may precede any task progression. For sudden jerks or drop attacks without warning, protection against injury may matter more than a warning-based strategy. For visual or PPPD-related difficulty, adaptation and specialist vestibular/visual care must be matched to actual assessment, not given as a generic sensory exposure. For swallowing or upper-airway symptoms, trained SLT and medical airway/nutrition assessment precede any practice. For functional paralysis, seating, transfer assistance and equipment may be necessary regardless of short-lived movement observed elsewhere.

These are **examples of technique-selection logic, not protocols**. The corresponding Reference pages specify contraindications and what is consensus rather than comparative evidence.

### Know when to change the plan

Ask about immediate and delayed effects. Stopping, making a task smaller, altering environmental demands, choosing a different target or referring for medical reassessment are all valid adaptations. Repeated prolonged worsening, falls, choking, respiratory difficulty or new neurological findings cannot be treated as proof that more intensity is required.

**Evidence boundary:** Motor and OT consensus endorse functional and collaborative rehabilitation but do not validate a universal dose, equipment ban or exercise progression. [R01](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r01) · [R15](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r15).

# Lesson 27 — Evidence, Expectations, and Treatment Claims

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Explain studies without erasing either benefit or limits

A mechanism model, a consensus statement and a randomized efficacy trial answer different questions. When presenting a programme, specify the enrolled population, comparator, primary endpoint, follow-up time, secondary findings, relevant adverse effects and access burden. A compelling therapist demonstration may inform feasibility but does not establish average efficacy or identify an active ingredient.

**Functional motor rehabilitation — Physio4FMD:** The 2024 pragmatic randomized study did not show superiority of specialist over usual neurological physiotherapy on its primary 12-month physical-function endpoint, although several secondary outcomes favoured specialist treatment. Both arms received active care. Neither “physiotherapy cures FND” nor “physiotherapy is ineffective” follows from these results. Consider selection and missing-data limitations when counselling patients. [R13](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r13).

**Functional seizures — CODES and subsequent guidance:** The CODES trial did not find a statistically significant benefit of seizure-specific CBT added to standardized medical care for the primary monthly-seizure-frequency measure at 12 months; some relevant secondary outcomes favoured CBT. The broader 2026 AAN guideline reviews additional studies and describes psychological interventions as possibly effective. Neither finding guarantees a change in seizure frequency for a particular person; continue follow-up and attend to non-frequency outcomes. [R16](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r16) · [R11](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r11).

For other presentations—functional tics, visual loss, cognition, swallowing, facial symptoms, drop attacks and more—intervention-specific comparative data may be thinner. Separate professional consensus, emerging research and broad rehabilitation analogies. Do not cite a motor trial as direct evidence for a swallowing, cognitive or vocal technique.

### Respond to commercial “rewiring” claims responsibly

Ask the patient what they were promised, what it costs, and whether outcomes are reported transparently. Explain the difference between learning-related plasticity and programme-level efficacy without shaming someone who found a private service helpful. Document patient-defined goals, burden, access inequity and reasons to pause or withdraw an unsuitable treatment.

No treatment should require acceptance of an unsupported trauma narrative, insist symptoms are deliberate or describe ongoing disability as noncompliance. Separate treatment responsiveness from diagnosis and credibility.

### Agree how benefit is judged

Follow symptom burden alongside function, recovery time, injuries, confidence, autonomy, participation and quality of life. Choose the smallest set of useful measures; an outcome assessed in clinic may not capture the cost of daily activity or caregiver assistance. If a measure is not validated for the relevant presentation, state that limitation.

**Clinical challenge:** What would you tell a patient whose measured tremor improves but whose falls and exhaustion are unchanged? Acknowledge the improvement, reassess the plan and retain safety/access care rather than calling the treatment complete.

**Evidence basis:** [R13](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r13), [R16](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r16), [R11](../new-lessons-research-2026-10-26/sources.md#r11). Full-text verification, final citation tables and conflict-of-interest appraisal remain Work tasks.
