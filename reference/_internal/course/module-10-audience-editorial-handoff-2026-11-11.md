## Shared editorial promise

A learner should leave each section knowing **more than they knew when they began**. Write to change understanding, not merely to reassure, repeat earlier lessons or catalogue possible problems. Make the reasoning visible: what was observed; why it might matter; what alternative explanations, limitations or uncertainties remain; and what the observation means in ordinary life.

- **Familiar before new.** Assume a learner has encountered earlier modules. Name and briefly remind readers of a previously taught concept only when it supports a new idea. Do not rebuild its original definition, diagnosis discussion, professional-role directory or safety explanation.
- **Show, then explain.** An ordinary, specific event often teaches more than a string of abstract sentences. After the reader sees what happened, add enough explanation to connect the event to the concept. A story cannot replace scientific evidence, and evidence should not drown the story.
- **Reasoning with proportionate certainty.** Distinguish observed experience, clinical findings, group research, hypotheses and invented teaching cases. Neither hopeful recovery nor continuing disability is a promised outcome. Avoid both overconfidence and so many warnings that they obscure the lesson.
- **Depth without length for its own sake.** Develop the genuinely new concept until the reader can recognize it in an unfamiliar situation. Then stop. Depth is a better explanation, not a longer restatement.
- **Natural paragraph rhythm.** Prefer connected paragraphs of roughly two to five sentences when the thoughts belong together; this is a guide, not a rule. Reserve isolated one-sentence paragraphs for rare emphasis or an important turn in the story. Dialogue turns can stand separately. Avoid successive short narrative lines that make the lesson feel artificially slow.
- **An adult's dignity.** Be warm, occasionally funny and clear without talking down to anyone. Readers may have pain, memory difficulty, fatigue, sensory sensitivity and limited reading endurance. Make the structure navigable and the language genuinely useful, not oversimplified.
- **Real lives are varied.** Avoid treating one symptom, level of support, mechanism, family situation or response to care as the universal FND experience. Where several examples help, choose different circumstances rather than three versions of the same one.
- **Appropriate safety is integrated.** Keep actual clinically important boundaries. Do not insert generic disclaimers or danger lists each time the reader meets a familiar idea; avoid technique prescriptions that need individual assessment.

**Editorial test:** If a paragraph repeats what a learner already knows, asks a question the next paragraph immediately answers without adding insight, or explains a scene that already teaches itself, delete or combine it. Conversely, restore detail when cutting it would remove the reasoning.

## Approved Module 10 teaching design — application, not repetition

The learner has already studied **available capacity and Spoon Theory in Lesson 4**, symptom variability and recovery, diagnostic uncertainty, the personal symptom map and safety plans, attention and automatic control, external focus and dual-task demands, prediction, the three sensory systems, salience, arousal and feedback loops, cognition and retrieval, emotional regulation, agency, adaptation, and Module 9's neuroplasticity, transfer, treatment evidence, rehabilitation burden and participation outcomes. **Treat these as learned foundations.** Never restate Lesson 4 with another list of physical, cognitive, emotional, social and sensory costs, and never rewrite Module 9 under a pacing heading.

**New instructional progression:**
- **Lesson 30 — What Uses Your Available Capacity?** Examine *interactions, combinations, task-switching, interruptions, order and context*: why the same tasks can impose different practical demands when done together or in another sequence. Distinguish changes in task demands, access and efficiency from an unmeasured increase in neurological energy.
- **Lesson 31 — Flexible Pacing When Capacity Changes:** Reason through choices **under uncertainty**: variable ability, foreseeable and unforeseeable worsening, reasonable alternatives, outcomes that do not prove prior failure, and clinically meaningful delayed deterioration. Do not teach that all flares were preventable or equate FND pacing with ME/CFS-specific energy management.
- **Lesson 32 — Balancing Practice, Rest, and Everyday Life:** Examine **trade-offs** among rehabilitation benefits, travel/attendance/recovery demands, everyday needs, support availability and the person's chosen priorities. Separate whether treatment helps, what it requires and whether the whole plan is sustainable; do not set an invented optimal rest-to-practice ratio.

### The structure of each audience section

1. Briefly invite the reader to **follow and interpret** a story, not sit through another review of concepts.
2. **Story/case 1:** A developed, believable event with a mix of clear and deliberately less obvious but fair clues. Prompt the reader to look for the relevant processes, capacity demands or helpful changes, and to identify *what cannot be determined*.
3. **Answer key 1, immediately after its story:** Reveal **what the story establishes**, **what could reasonably be contributing**, and **what cannot yet be concluded**. Explain connections to the earlier lessons, rather than repeating those lessons; avoid a false single correct interpretation.
4. **Story/case 2 and answer key 2:** A genuinely different, usually more complex situation requiring the reader to connect multiple earlier processes. It must not replay the first plot with substituted characters. **Before the second story**, invite readers to consider a question or observation that they can test against the upcoming case.
5. **One concluding paragraph** bringing the cases together and naming the new transferable insight. No series of moral summaries, compulsory quiz score or extra mini-lesson.

**Within each Person story, pause at two meaningful decision points** before revealing what the character does or information that changes the decision. Ask readers what they would choose **with the knowledge, circumstances, symptoms and feelings available at that moment**; invite them to apply their own symptoms while recognizing that the choice may be easier or harder for someone else. Clues should be planted briefly *before* the moment they matter, rather than revealed only in the answer key. Avoid spoiling the outcome by narrating its explanation in advance. Keep paragraphs naturally joined and answers free of enumerated lists where narrative headings are clearer. The first Lesson 31 story's two pauses and Lesson 30's retrofitted pauses are approved examples.

Use **two substantial stories per audience section per lesson** as the approved authoring design. Person and Supporter cases are lived-experience teaching stories; Clinician cases present **two clinical reasoning problems** with appropriate evidence and uncertainty. The audience sections must teach distinct insights, not present the same scenes in three voices. The answer key should invite discovery, never shame readers for missing something. An absence of sufficient evidence can itself be the key finding.

**Previously taught terminology:** When a learned technical term becomes important, **bring it in with its short established definition in a blockquote**, then apply it to the new case. Prefer a handful of relevant definitions in or just before the answer key rather than a glossary dump interrupting the narrative. Link the established glossary/source where practical. New terms get clear plain-language definitions. Person and Supporter prose targets an accessible approximately 14-year-old reading level **without reducing conceptual complexity**; clinicians retain professional vocabulary and only use a blockquote when a distinction particularly needs clarification.

**What an answer key must not do:** Treat fictional details as findings about a diagnosis or brain mechanism; infer a private thought, a trigger, or a health condition not supported by the scene; equate successful performance with sustainable capacity; label an unpredictable flare a pacing failure; claim adaptation increased a measurable biological reserve; or prescribe unsupervised rehabilitation. A character may have made a reasonable decision followed by a bad outcome.

**Story continuity and variety:** Draw accurately from established characters when helpful: Natalie is severely disabled, lives alone, has ongoing functional seizures, continuous migraine, sensory, cognitive and movement difficulties, and sees Leah only occasionally; Jamie and Alex have previously navigated competing questions, cognitive demands and relationship misunderstandings; Chris and Maya have faced incomplete memory and different recollections; Daniel wants to access his garden despite functional walking difficulty; Amira manages involuntary arm movements while considering adaptations and rehabilitation; Priya has missed valued social events. **Do not merge same-named individuals from separate illustrations without explicit evidence they are the same character.** Include people with milder, different or independent experiences, and don't repeat earlier plots merely because they are familiar. Fictional scenes, attributed community accounts and verified scientific findings must remain distinct.

## Audience 1 — For the Person With FND

### Voice and language

Write directly to the person, approximately at a **14-year-old reading level**, but with the respect, curiosity and emotional intelligence owed to an adult. Prefer everyday words and natural speech. Explain an unfamiliar term in plain language **when it first becomes useful**, often in a short blockquote definition. A brief contextual reminder is enough for a term taught in earlier modules; do not create a second glossary entry for the same basic concept.

Use **“you”** naturally, without assuming the reader experiences the fictional person's symptoms. Prefer invitations to recognize and consider over instructions to feel, think or behave a prescribed way. Do not suggest that understandable frustration, anxiety or a difficult day proves any mechanism of FND.

### Pace and shape

Begin with a **brief invitation to find the clues** in two distinct substantial stories. Let the scene unfold uninterrupted; make the interpretation visible in the **answer key after each story**. Include obvious and less obvious details that can be recognized from earlier learning; reserve uncertainty and alternative explanations for the key. Keep natural paragraphs and realistic dialogue, without making every change of thought a dramatic one-line pause.

Do not rush: a person with cognitive difficulties may need an extra example or a clear transition to recognize why one idea follows another. But **slow teaching is not slow prose**. Repeated setup, dialogue that produces no new insight and three separate statements of the same conclusion increase reading effort without improving understanding.

### Explanatory depth and evidence

Take the main concept far enough that readers can apply it beyond the story. Explain the **why**, not simply the reassuring conclusion. When a mechanism is a model, present it as a model; when research measured an outcome, say what changed and what did not. Give carefully chosen study numbers only when they make an outcome understandable. State the relevant limitation close to the finding, without a repetitive cautionary paragraph after each study.

Show a range of manifestations or circumstances when this adds understanding. Differentiating the experiences of two people may be more informative than listing six symptom names. First-person community experience can add texture and hope, but never stand in for treatment evidence. A person's lived experience should not be used to establish another person's likely outcome.

### Ending and reader experience

For this authoring round, end after **one summary paragraph**. **Defer** the usual **Something to think about / How might that feel? / One small step** prompts until the full module's story exercises have been reviewed. At that point, the project lead will decide whether reflections and activities add something new, and will explicitly reconcile the decision with the course's publication requirements. Do not prescribe an unsupervised clinical exercise, mandatory tracking or a test of improvement.

**Self-check:** Could someone with FND explain the new idea in their own words and recognize it in another situation? Is there enough reasoning to be useful, but little needless repetition? Would the reader feel addressed rather than managed?

## Audience 2 — For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### Voice and perspective

Write as if giving a thoughtful explanation **over coffee**: conversational, observant, compassionate and occasionally humorous. Supporters are people in relationships, not substitute therapists or professional caregivers. Include their legitimate uncertainties, disappointment, responsibilities, hopes and limits without centring the lesson on their burden or blaming them for an ordinary misunderstanding.

Support need not mean a spouse or an available daily caregiver. A person may live alone, have an occasional friend, receive intermittent practical help or have no reliable support. Keep the person with FND's autonomy, preferences and unseen work visible.

### Two stories, two distinct supporter discoveries

Use **two developed supporter-facing stories per lesson**, each followed immediately by a concise answer key. Rather than repeating the Person section's plots, reveal what a caring relative, friend or intermittent supporter might reasonably observe, miss, ask, or change with consent. One story can show a plausible misunderstanding; the other should pose a different relationship, availability or planning challenge. **The supporter is not a detective diagnosing someone else's nervous system or an activity police officer.** Clues may involve the person's unseen demands, the supporter's own capacity, helpful collaboration, and information that remains inaccessible without asking the person.

The answer key names the observed facts, plausible contributions from familiar neurological processes, different perspectives and unknowable points. Use previously learned definitions in short blockquotes when the term genuinely helps understanding. Let the supporter recognize a constructive next conversation without prescribing another person's choices. This two-story structure supersedes the earlier optional two-pair pattern **for Module 10**.

### Pace, dialogue and depth

Favor believable conversation, ordinary detail and occasional gentle humour. Characters may misunderstand, hesitate and later adjust. Preserve their dignity and agency on both sides. Dialogue should do work—reveal a difference of perspective, invite new understanding or change what happens next.

Avoid a run of isolated one-line narrative paragraphs. Combine connected observations and reactions. **Cut repeated conversations and repeated moral summaries aggressively.** One compact closing reflection often does more than several paragraphs that tell readers what every character has learned.

Make practical implications visible through choices and relationship dynamics rather than checklists telling supporters what to supervise. Respect consent without revisiting the entire autonomy lesson in each section. Supporters should not be asked to diagnose symptoms, judge treatment efficacy, conduct therapy or continuously monitor a person's performance.

For the interim Module 10 draft, **do not append** the usual supporter discussion/action prompts; close with one synthesis paragraph. The project lead will decide on module-wide reflection and activity design after all stories and keys are reviewed.

**Self-check:** Could a caring supporter recognize themselves in the story without feeling accused? What new understanding would they take into their next ordinary conversation? Would the section still work if the person had only occasional help? Could about a third be cut without losing an insight? If yes, tighten it.

## Audience 3 — For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Voice and readership

Write for knowledgeable professionals, **without speaking with unwarranted authority**. The tone is collegial, precise, evidence-led and curious: a clinician exploring a meaningful inference with the reader, not a guideline issuing commands. Keep the established public heading **For Clinicians and the Care Team**.

Use language relevant to the professional question. A physiotherapist, occupational therapist, speech-language therapist, rehabilitation practitioner or **family doctor** may be central as appropriate; do not manufacture a paragraph for every profession. Prefer **family doctor** in the public narrative where that role is needed. Retain broader team relevance through realistic information-sharing and shared outcomes, not exhaustive lists of job titles.

Assume previous modules have already taught familiar clinical concepts, including the biopsychosocial framework, diagnostic evidence, attention/automatic movement, adaptations, participation, and distinctions between a proposed mechanism and evidence of benefit. **Do not define ordinary clinician vocabulary** such as retention or transfer in a blockquote. A short blockquote reminder is appropriate only when a specific distinction is central and potentially easy to conflate.

### Two case-based clinical reasoning challenges

Use **two realistic, self-contained clinical cases per lesson**, each followed immediately by its analysis/answer key. They should address a distinct but connected decision and test the clinician's ability to use earlier knowledge, rather than explain what fatigue, FND or pacing means again. Give enough relevant positives, competing findings and missing information to support **several defensible considerations** but not ungrounded speculation. Ask the clinician what they would need to establish, distinguish, discuss or review before recommending a change.

Organize the answer key using the established **What / Why / How** reasoning:
- **What:** Clinical observations and clearly stated unknowns, including timing, symptom specificity, functional effect and medical context.
- **Why:** Differential or coexisting possibilities and their relevance; distinguish established FND evidence, professional consensus, other-condition guidance and hypotheses. Make explicit what the case **does not prove**.
- **How:** A safe, individualized shared decision, information to gather, relevant interprofessional coordination, and an outcome or review trigger that could change care. This is a teaching case, **not** a universal clinical pathway.

Cases may involve activity analysis, immediate versus delayed worsening, exercise tolerance, migraine and sleep, functional seizures with post-event recovery, communication or cognitive load, treatment burden, lack of services, and protection of disability support while rehabilitation continues. Do not assume every patient has PEM; do not ignore possible PEM or impose fixed exercise escalation when relevant. Avoid repeated professional-role catalogues. Keep the case narratives and answer keys readable as two substantial pieces rather than a checklist masquerading as prose.

**Research dependency — brief delivered and used for Lessons 30–31 Clinician:** The dedicated Work investigation produced the [Module 10 Clinician Case Evidence & Design Brief](module-10-clinician-case-evidence-and-design-brief-2026-11-11.md), with an evidence map, six candidate cases (30A/B, 31A/B, 32A/B), What/Why/How answer outlines and source verification boundaries. Review and select cases in Chat with the project lead **before** authoring Clinician prose. Its findings inform options; they do not approve claims, final cases or clinical decisions, or replace later source audits and independent clinical review.

### Depth, sources and pace

Prefer enough detail to make the decision credible: which population was studied, what the intervention or comparison was, which outcome was measured and what the finding can support. Make the distinction between the evidence and the hypothetical case explicit. Cite existing suitable original research or authoritative guidance where it bears on the inference. Keep the full evidence apparatus in Research and Sources; don't repeat the Person section's entire review of studies or all the numbers for every presentation.

A precise clinical qualification is more useful than an apologetic cascade of caveats. Address coexisting health conditions and differential concerns when they materially alter the case, without repeatedly teaching the entire biopsychosocial framework. Avoid language that implies an individual response proves a neurological mechanism, reverses a diagnosis, guarantees independence or negates support needs.

**Aim for two substantial, differentiated cases with reasoned answers**, allowing enough length for competing interpretations and useful clinical decisions; do not impose the old single-case 800–1,200-word target. Use natural multi-sentence paragraphs, a restrained number of headings, and one clear clinical insight at the end. Don't use isolated one-liners to mimic drama.

**Self-check:** What new clinical inference did this section teach? Could a professional explain which evidence supports it and which information would change their view? Is the patient meaningful rather than just an illustration of terminology? What part repeats a previous lesson and can go?

## Across all three audiences

| Dimension | Person | Supporter | Clinician |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Understand and recognize the concept in lived experience | Understand another person's experience and relate more thoughtfully | Interpret an observation and make a better-informed clinical judgment |
| Primary vehicle | Two applied stories and two discovery keys | Two relationship stories and two discovery keys | Two clinical cases and two What/Why/How keys |
| Language | Conversational, plain and respectful; define genuinely new terms | “Advice over coffee”; human, not instructional or clinical | Professional but accessible; no false authority |
| Pace | Unhurried teaching, flowing paragraphs, no repeated explanations | Efficient scenes, no duplicated plot or moral | Concise case context, real depth in reasoning |
| Evidence | A few explained findings linked to the reader's question | A small amount at the point it illuminates the story | Claim-level relevant evidence with population, outcomes and limits |
| Ending | One summary paragraph; Thought/Heart/activity deferred | One summary paragraph; discussion/activity deferred | One clinically grounded synthesis paragraph |

**Avoid audience duplication:** The Person section should not need a supporter to explain the person's experience; the Supporter section should not rewrite the Person's definitions or medical study tour; the Clinician section should not retell both stories or begin with an elementary lesson on familiar terminology. Each should stand alone as a coherent educational section, but build from knowledge already taught in earlier modules.

**Continuity:** Reuse established fictional characters where it adds depth and respects their circumstances. Do not force the same characters or one type of symptom into every lesson. Keep invented scenes clearly separate from verified community accounts and research. A case may illustrate an inference, not establish prevalence, cause or treatment effectiveness.

**Health and evidence boundaries:** Maintain meaningful safety, diagnostic uncertainty and coexistence; never substitute a fictional recovery for trial evidence. Treat serious concerns proportionately and route technique-specific guidance to appropriate professional or existing Reference Library material when relevant. Clinical and source verification remain part of the later Work audit, not an assumption of this style handoff.

1. **Before each draft:** Read the current syllabus and approved previous lessons. State the section's *new teaching purpose* in one sentence and note the concepts already known; prepare **two different stories/cases and paired answer keys**, with fair clues and a distinct synthesis. Do not re-explain old concepts or generate an unrelated research outline.
2. **Draft one audience section at a time in Chat**, beginning with Lesson 30 Person. Cite sources only for real factual claims. **Before clinician authoring**, collect the focused Work research briefing below. Do not commit a section or infer its approval from general encouragement.
3. **Project-lead revision and approval:** Record specific accepted wording, pacing and structural decisions. Approval of one audience section does not approve the other two. Record exceptions to these style defaults rather than forcing the story to fit a template.
4. **Commit the approved prose** to the designated lesson branch/PR; preserve unaffected sections and their source tables. Maintain exact authoring and audit-status metadata. “Human-authored/approved” never means independently clinically reviewed.

Neither audit overwrites approved editorial choices without a documented project-lead decision.
