## Project lead's central direction — preserve this meaning

> **“Slow is smoothe. Smoothe is fast.”** — The project lead's own wording in discussion; spelling may be normalized to “Slow is smooth. Smooth is fast.” in public prose if approved.

The point is **not** an empirically demonstrated rule that slower treatment always leads to faster recovery. It is a lived metaphor for allowing understanding, readiness, recovery and realistic decisions to develop without the pressure to rush every step. Do not turn the phrase into a prescribed seizure-recovery interval, graded activity protocol, or guaranteed outcome.

The project lead described the early path through FND as a fog: hospitalization, diagnosis, unanswered questions, and an understandable urge to discover how to get better. By the end of Module 10, the learner may *still not have access to rehabilitation*, but should already have language to describe symptoms and candidate processes, recognize limits of those explanations, and participate in a more useful conversation. **That understanding is an achievement in its own right, not merely preparation for treatment.**

The project lead's explicit new Module 11 preference:

> “acknowledgement followed by how to work with doctors and overcoming barriers to healthcare. That's 3 lessons.”

**Teaching promise:** Recognize the value already gained from understanding FND; help readers work constructively with clinicians in unequal or uncertain healthcare relationships; help them make realistic, proportionate choices when access is constrained or the system does not meet their needs. The aim is *care that addresses actual needs without consuming more of life than necessary*, not assembling the largest possible team.

**Suggested working module title:** *Finding Care and Working With Healthcare* (editorial proposal; **not yet a project-lead-approved public title**). Existing canonical title *Building an Individual Treatment Team* remains until the lead approves a change and authorizes reconciliation of overview, syllabus, nav, progress metadata and site references.

## What the lead taught us from lived experience — **de-identified authoring material, not medical evidence**

These experiences were shared to deepen the teaching. They are **not** permission to publish identifying personal details, make causal diagnoses, portray a named clinician as negligent, or copy an individual's life into a recognizable fictional account. Ask before turning a particular detail into a public quote.

### A. The accomplishment before treatment

- Learning terms such as attention, predictive processing, sensory signals, cognition and variable access to skills can reduce the need to report **every unusual observation** to a loved one. The person may recognize a *possible* explanation, set aside a familiar pattern, or describe a meaningful change more clearly. This is **increased understanding and communication**, not proof of a particular mechanism or that a symptom requires no assessment.
- Better language can make conversations with a **spouse, parent, friend, family doctor or specialist** more productive; the person may be better understood even without improved symptom frequency or physical independence.
- Some readers will be young or dependent on a **parent/guardian** to arrange and accompany healthcare. Others live alone, lack a supporter, rely on a family doctor, or receive intermittent assistance. Do not write only to autonomous, well-resourced adults.
- Readers can sometimes distinguish what resembles familiar FND patterns from a problem that needs a different assessment; **they do not have to self-diagnose** or prove to a doctor that something is or is not FND.
- The project lead's experience of **waiting additional time after a functional seizure once movement returned**, after previously attempting activities immediately and reporting further episodes, illustrates the value of *individualized patient knowledge, negotiated recovery time and choice*. It does **not** establish an appropriate waiting period for other people or demonstrate that early activity caused additional seizures. Do not recommend self-testing paralysis, repeatedly attempting movement, or fixed seizure recovery routines.

### B. Unequal authority, referral pressure, and diagnostic overshadowing

- An ill, exhausted or cognitively impaired person may know a particular appointment does not answer the reason they sought care, yet feel compelled to accept because **the doctor has expertise and gatekeeping authority**. Respect for a family doctor and disagreement about a referral can coexist.
- One lived example concerned seeking care for **persistent migraine** while an FND or older “conversion disorder” explanation became dominant. A psychological referral generated another opinion that influenced later access to neurology. This is relevant to **diagnostic overshadowing** and **referral anchoring**, not proof that the whole psychological assessment was medically unsound. Migraine remains a legitimate condition to assess and treat in a person who also has FND.
- A referral can be accepted **without endorsing every theory offered by the referrer**, and a person can ask: “What question will this referral answer? What change in care might follow? Who will keep helping with my other problem?” Refusing, accepting or deferring a nonurgent appointment can each be reasonable; decisions depend on purpose, risk, access and patient preference. Good wording does not guarantee a gatekeeper will agree.
- Avoid an oversimple doctor-as-villain story. Real clinicians may be well-intentioned, uncertain, practicing under referral constraints, under-informed about contemporary FND, or disagreeing reasonably; harm or missed care is still possible. Represent differences in expertise and authority without implying all psychiatrists/psychologists are poor fits or that psychological care is intrinsically invalid.
- Over many years, numerous medications, appointments, supplements and recommended practitioners can involve **cost, adverse effects, uncertainty, disappointment, scarce energy and strain on a supporter**. Do not use the person's treatment history to endorse or condemn individual drugs, infer a pharmacological cause, or claim all were improperly prescribed. Teach *treatment burden* and evaluating the expected value of another referral.

### C. A required disability assessment is not the same as care

The project lead and assistant privately reviewed a **2022 WorkSafeBC psychological/neuropsychological impairment assessment** from a personal full-disclosure document. **Do not put that file, exact identifying details, allegations, or intimate history in GitHub; the private PDF is not a source for public medical claims.** The broad lessons supported by the report and user testimony are:

- The assessor's report itself recorded that **before the appointment**, the person warned about the length of testing, severe sensory triggers, fatigue, language disruption and possible functional seizures. Accommodations included dim lighting, sunglasses, an agreed place to lie down, and a supporter available to assist.
- The assessment record describes **several seizure-related interruptions and rest breaks**, after which testing resumed; the person also sometimes agreed or offered to continue. It acknowledges that testing and its interpretation were affected by seizures and limited tolerance. Do **not** claim the report independently proves the precise “five to eight hours” recalled for each session; a separate remote interview was recorded as 2.5 hours.
- **Having accommodations is not identical to judging whether the total duration, task burden, stopping points and recovery demands were appropriate.** The presence of a signed form or observed willingness to resume does not settle how free a person felt to stop when a required assessment affected benefits or an institutional decision.
- This assessment was designed for **impairment, work-capacity, causation and prognosis opinions**, not to build a rehabilitation service. Extensive testing can produce no new treatment pathway; asking what an appointment is for matters.
- The report documented relatively strong performance on some cognitive tests *and* explicitly distinguished that performance from sustained functional access in ordinary life. Do not interpret good test results as absence of disabling cognitive symptoms or as proof of safe work independence.
- The project lead remembers trusting the assessor partly because he claimed experience with a Canadian FND expert whose name the lead recalled imprecisely. **The inspected report did not identify or verify such a consultation**, so do not name or assert the expert or imply specialist participation as verified.
- No independent determination about professional misconduct, informed consent, the optimal testing duration, or assessment validity has been made. Treat this as an example of questions around access, purpose, accommodations, authority and follow-up, not a public case verdict.

### D. Scarcity is a real part of the care question

- In a Canadian setting, the **family doctor often coordinates referrals and provides longitudinal medical care**. Specialty waits, travel, coverage, expenses and availability can make a suggested care pathway impractical; a named referral is not the same as accessible treatment.
- The project lead has not built an extensive FND team over years and questions whether another search is worthwhile. The clinical point is not to tell him—or every reader—to keep seeking specialists indefinitely. Existing family medicine and pain/migraine care may be meaningful; identify *one specific unresolved need* for which another opinion or skill might make a difference, rather than promising an ideal team is attainable.
- No unsupported national prevalence statistics for FND-specialist scarcity, claims about Canadian practitioners being broadly outdated, or absolute claims that no expert exists may enter public prose without suitable evidence. Use local access as a realistic circumstance, not a researched nationwide fact.
- Parents and other supporters can help communicate, remember, arrange transport and advocate if invited, but they need support and limits too. Do not make them a compulsory unpaid care coordinator.

## Proposed three-lesson outline (Lessons 33–35)

**Progression:** **recognize what you have gained → work with clinicians → choose a way forward when healthcare is hard to access.** Each lesson has a *distinct question and new intellectual payoff*; avoid three retellings of “speak up for yourself.”

### Lesson 33 — Recognizing How Far You've Come

**Reader's question:** “I may still have FND and may not have started rehabilitation. What have I actually gained?”

**Teach new:**
- Recognize the difficult beginning (fear, hospitals, diagnosis and uncertain recovery) without dwelling on crisis or promising a turning point.
- Invite the reader to see what the course has already given them: a shared vocabulary; ability to describe a symptom with relevant context; some possible processes rather than one definite cause; ways to understand what is familiar, what differs, what matters to daily life, and what they still do not know.
- Show a real, small **communication difference**: the person no longer needs to relay every strange observation in distress, or can tell a parent/partner what was noticed, what is suspected, and what question remains. A person may also choose **not** to explain that day.
- Make clear that **understanding, participation, confidence and reduced uncertainty** can matter for quality of life independently of symptom reduction. This is a humane outcome, not a guarantee that education alone reduces symptoms.
- Introduce the “slow is smooth” sentiment as permission to approach the next stage thoughtfully, not a rule about exertion or an instruction to delay urgent care.

**Narrative possibility for Chat discussion (NOT approved final prose):** Natalie and occasional friend Leah have a new conversation that is simpler and more precise because Natalie recognizes and names her experience. Or introduce a young person who explains a pattern and an unresolved question to a parent who helps arrange appointments. The latter broadens the audience. Avoid another full story of Natalie searching the Reference Library.

**Person outcome:** “I already know more and can communicate more clearly, even if I haven't improved physically.”  
**Supporter distinct contribution:** Notice that calm recognition or a choice not to discuss a familiar symptom may be progress; listen without requiring explanations or maintaining surveillance. Include parent/guardian access where relevant.  
**Clinician distinct decision:** Recognize health-literacy gains and meaningful patient understanding, use the person's observations to guide an individualized conversation without mistaking articulate symptom language for a definitive diagnosis, improved functioning, or reassurance that no further assessment is needed.

**Do not repeat:** original FND introduction (Modules 1–3), positive diagnostic signs (Module 2), symptom map (Module 4), red-flag checklist (Module 5), the mechanics of prediction/attention/cognition (Modules 6–7), reference-library navigation (Module 8), neuroplasticity (Module 9), or pacing instructions (Module 10). The value of *acquired language at this point in the course* is the new idea.

### Lesson 34 — Working With Your Healthcare Professionals

**Reader's question:** “What can I do when I need care, but a professional and I see the problem differently?”

**Teach new:**
- The asymmetry of authority, medical vocabulary and control over referrals. This can be harder when there are cognitive symptoms, communication interruptions, youth/dependence, a trusted family doctor, or fear of appearing “noncompliant.”
- Distinguish **reason for seeking help**, **what a referral is intended to assess/treat**, **the explanation offered**, and **what remains unresolved**. Agreement to an appointment is not proof of agreement with a theory.
- Clarify how an appointment might be useful while leaving another medical concern unattended. Teach respectful but specific questions to identify *which issue has an owner* and *what happens if the plan does not help*.
- A person may reasonably request an explanation, notes, a supporter or parent, accessible communication, reconsideration of new evidence, or another opinion. They may decide how far to continue a discussion. Avoid a magical scripted conversation that always persuades the doctor.
- Include a specific example of **FND plus a separately assessable migraine or other condition**. Acceptance of FND and a request for migraine care are not contradictory. Explain diagnostic overshadowing when it genuinely illuminates the case; avoid suggesting the reader independently diagnoses neurological disease.
- Explain why psychological treatment offered for an appropriate purpose is not in itself invalid or proof that FND was caused by trauma; detailed psychological-treatment choice and consent evidence **belongs in Module 12**, not here.

**Narrative possibility (not yet approved):** A person with FND asks for help with a persistent migraine; a doctor proposes a psychological referral, the person accepts, but returns with the migraine question still unresolved. The pivot is *not an argument won*: the next appointment separates problems, clarifies the referral's purpose, and names follow-up while acknowledging uncertainty or continued disagreement. Consider a variant where a parent must help a teenager articulate a concern.

**Person outcome:** “I can take part in a difficult conversation without proving that I know more than a doctor.”  
**Supporter distinct contribution:** Being an invited witness, helping keep one question visible when the patient loses words, negotiating when to speak, and not taking over or disparaging clinicians automatically.  
**Clinician distinct decision:** Prevent diagnostic overshadowing; respond productively when a patient has researched FND; distinguish expertise, uncertainty, referral purpose, comorbid conditions, patient goals and possible harm from another low-purpose referral; preserve an ongoing therapeutic relationship and a route to reassessment.

**Do not repeat:** Module 8's lesson about whether an individual symptom fits FND, Module 5's generic medical-safety protocol, Module 9's appraisal of treatment advertisements, or Module 15's future full appointment-preparation/records/self-advocacy toolkit. Here the new matter is **the interpersonal, institutional and clinical decision when authority and interpretation differ**.

### Lesson 35 — Finding Your Way Through Healthcare Barriers

**Reader's question:** “What is a realistic next step when specialists are far away, expensive, scarce, inaccessible, or when I'm exhausted by trying?”

**Teach new:**
- A care pathway's *actual burden*: time to referral, travel, light/sound intolerance, cognitive load, money/coverage, appointment stamina, possible symptom worsening, parent/supporter time, and the emotional cost of another unhelpful encounter. This is not an energy accounting lesson; it is about choosing high-value healthcare.
- Not all assessments offer treatment, and not all appointments have equal purpose. A required insurance/benefits assessment, an expert opinion, rehabilitation, and care for a coexisting condition serve **different goals**. Their conclusions may affect each other but they are not interchangeable.
- Ask what is realistically available **now**: ongoing family-doctor care, a pain or migraine clinician, appropriately informed local rehabilitation, suitable remote consultation, and one clearly defined referral if needed. An existing small team may be more useful than several disconnected appointments.
- Name missing expertise without suggesting an unsupported substitute will solve the problem. Specialist consultation and telehealth may help some people, subject to suitability and follow-through; online groups or this site's Reference Library support questions but do not replace examination, professional care, or medical accountability.
- An informed choice to pause or decline a **low-value, nonurgent** consultation may be reasonable. Preserve care for urgent/new/changed problems and do not imply patients must abandon the search or accept inadequate care.
- Introduce the idea of choosing **one problem, one potential contributor, one owner, and a review point** as an optional light decision framework, **not** another multi-page form or expectation that the person must coordinate all services.

**Narrative possibility (not approved):** An adult in a small community who has a family doctor and one other treating clinician weighs a distant specialist referral against what that specialist could actually offer; a parent-supported young person encounters a similar travel/coverage problem. A past lengthy insurer-required assessment could inform a **de-identified fictional contrast** between assessment for an institution and an appointment intended to change care; avoid literal recreation of the project lead's identifiable experience.

**Person outcome:** “A sensible care plan can be small, honest about missing services, and adapted to my life.”  
**Supporter distinct contribution:** Help compare expected benefit and practical burden, support a choice rather than pressuring another consultation; protect caregiver time and the person's right to decide.  
**Clinician distinct decision:** Continuity and primary-care ownership despite unavailable specialists; referral quality and real indication; coordinated care for FND and coexisting conditions; accessible assessment, follow-through on remote specialist advice, alternatives when specialist treatment is not feasible, and meaningful outcomes.

**Do not repeat:** Module 10's pacing strategies, Module 13's detailed devices/accessibility catalogue, Module 15's records and appointment summaries, Module 16's disability-benefit navigation, Module 17's setbacks, or Module 18's personal-handbook assembly. Do not write a “how to find an FND specialist” directory or recommend an unverified commercial treatment.

## Detailed audience voice and language standards — carefully reconciled from Modules 6–10

### Across audiences: shared editorial promise

**Every section must teach something new, not merely restate what the reader already knows.** Show an observation, let a difference in interpretation become visible, and explain why that changes understanding or a decision. Do not tell readers every character is “learning a lesson” after every scene. New depth is *a more useful inference*, not greater word count.

- **Warmth and maturity:** Write conversational Canadian/British-friendly English. No slogans, inspirational platitudes, forced reassurance, condescending simplicity, clinical coldness or artificial drama. Occasional specific, gentle humour is welcome; never make the disabled person or supporter the joke.
- **At about age-14 reading level for the Person audience, not a 14-year-old emotional age.** Readers may be highly educated adults with limited semantic/episodic memory, reading endurance, speech or attention. Preserve intellectual honesty and meaningful choices.
- **Natural reading rhythm:** Usually several connected sentences per paragraph. Dialogue can stand on its own lines; do not stack single-line prose for effect, deliver a whole lesson as bullet points, or artificially elongate a thought across many micro-paragraphs. Keep enough context for slow reading; remove redundant setup and repeated conclusions.
- **Familiar before new:** A term like attention, interoception, adaptation, neuroplasticity, participation, diagnostic signs or available capacity is already in the course. A brief reminder in context may be useful, but do not reintroduce the concept with a fresh mini-lecture or glossary when an ordinary mention suffices. A genuinely new term can have a short **blockquoted, plain-language definition** at first useful appearance.
- **Show, then interpret:** Build distinctive, credible scenes with enough life and dialogue to expose the issue. Explain what the scene establishes and what it does *not* establish. Do not attribute motives or diagnoses to real clinicians or patients based on a fictional moment.
- **Evidence in the reader's voice:** Use short, natural research connections only when they help the reader understand something. **The project lead explicitly rejected a sudden academic register under “What does the evidence tell us?” in Module 10.** Do not write study-design digressions, caveat cascades, numerical limitations or method summaries into ordinary Person/Supporter storytelling merely because there is a research subheading. Keep sources clickable and full verification in Research and Sources and Work audit. Do retain a material limitation in public prose **when without it the claim would mislead**, and use greater methodological detail in the Clinician section when it matters to the clinical decision.
- **Certainty by evidence type:** Distinguish empirical results, current guidance, consensus, proposed mechanisms, fictional examples and public lived experiences. A case or quote cannot prove a general mechanism, treatment effect, rate of harm or national access pattern. Respect neurological, psychological and social contributions without assigning one psychological cause to all FND.
- **FND is real and involuntary.** Symptom fluctuation or apparently normal test performance is not malingering or proof of deliberate control. The reader's acquired insight is not self-diagnosis and does not eliminate medical uncertainty. Recognize coexisting illness and meaningful change.
- **Balanced tone:** Avoid repeated frightening medical warnings, defensive “this isn't saying…” paragraphs, blame/effort disclaimers, and adversarial assumptions about care. Keep an important safety qualification where it matters and be precise, but do not hijack a human story with risk management.
- **Varied perspectives:** People may be young, adult, rural, urban, financially constrained, living alone, supported by a parent, spouse or occasional friend, and have different symptoms. No character or outcome stands for everyone. Respect readers who have not improved and those who have.
- **Continuity without invented biography:** Natalie has severe fluctuating FND, functional seizures, mobility and cognitive problems, chronic migraine and sensory sensitivities; she lives alone and Leah can visit only occasionally. Do not turn Leah into a live-in aide, suddenly cure Natalie, claim a treatment for her that was not introduced, or copy a real disclosure report into her story. Reuse other established characters only when their actual lesson history supports it.
- **Exercises are optional:** Do not demand symptom monitoring, written journals, repeated recall, unsupervised test/exposure, medical self-experiments or at-home seizure challenges. Do not build a large form simply because teaching is practical. Leave a reader with one reasonable idea rather than homework overload.

### 1. For the Person With FND

**Teaching role:** Help someone recognize an important *new* idea about their life or healthcare decision. Do not make them the passive recipient of professional instruction.

**Voice:** Direct “you” naturally; clear, thoughtful, welcoming, lightly humorous when suitable. Begin with an ordinary recognizable moment rather than a list of definitions or eight sentences about what the chapter will cover. Never assume a reader has every symptom in a story, a spouse, transport, money or adult autonomy.

**Story structure:** The project lead valued the recent Module 10 **substantial, realistic stories with a decision point** followed by an answer key explaining observable clues and why they matter. But the **two-story-plus-two-answer-key format was module-specific, not a permanent mandate.** Choose one developed story or two *genuinely different* scenes per Module 11 lesson only after proposing the learning progression. Each narrative should have a believable decision, an imperfect or uncertain outcome, and a genuine change in reader understanding. Do not manufacture a tidy conversion to perfect healthcare by the last paragraph.

**Explanation:** Write sufficiently slowly for cognition without reiterating every premise. Show which information a person knows, what they suspect and what remains a question. If including a study or survey, explain the actual relevant message conversationally with a linked citation. Do not turn into a “study methodology” essay. Recognize that knowledge can make **communication and everyday life better before symptoms change**; don't promise this will always happen.

**Ending:** Prior modules used **“Something to think about,” “How might that feel?” and “One small step,”** each with a different purpose: cognitive reflection, meaningful emotional perspective and safe optional action. Module 10 *temporarily deferred* those because its embedded story questions already did the work. For Module 11 **do not assume either format automatically**; propose an ending structure to the project lead after the first Person section. If those headings return, the prompts must not be interchangeable, compulsory or require perfect memory. One small useful question or optional action may be enough.

**Quality test:** What precise new language or decision has the reader gained? Could someone with reduced memory still follow the story? Is there an authentic discovery rather than a restated maxim?

### 2. For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

**Teaching role:** Show the other person's perspective and how support can increase the person's voice in care *without becoming their clinical supervisor, parent by default, or adversary to doctors*.

**Voice:** **“Advice over coffee.”** Natural warmth, concrete details, realistic dialogue, rare gentle humour; treat a supporter as a person with responsibilities and limited time, not an endless resource or an incompetent outsider. Avoid a formal carers' handbook and repetitive lists of what to do/never do.

**Perspective:** Where appropriate, show the difference between what the supporter observes and what the person with FND experiences or tries to express. A supporter may be a spouse, **parent** helping a younger reader access care, friend, sibling, intermittent visitor, or absent. Parents should not automatically override a young person's voice; age, safety and legal consent requirements matter. Keep independent adults independent even when they need help.

**Story structure:** The **latest Module 10 approved pattern** was one connected, relatively concise story about Owen, Maya and Pip **per lesson**, followed by a **brief evidence/community voice** and reasoned synthesis of the resulting benefit. This superseded an earlier two-stories-per-Supporter prescription for that module. For Module 11, one solid supporter story with a distinctive practical insight per lesson is a strong starting proposal; do **not** mechanically transplant Owen/Maya/Pip or require exactly that structure before discussion. A supporter narrative should not retell the Person story, give all medical explanations again, or serve as a caricature of the “overprotective partner.” Dialogue must change the understanding.

**Evidence/quotations:** The revised Lesson 30 Supporter example is an important voice model: two concise ordinary-language evidence paragraphs and source links, **without a paragraph explaining selection bias or an invitation to read study limitations**. Original-source limitations still belong in Work audit, with only meaning-changing caveats in the public passage. Authentic first-person **supporter** quotes are desirable only if their speaker, context, wording and original URL are verified and genuinely contribute emotionally. No invented, paraphrased-as-verbatim or forced quote. If a quotation is missing, ask Work in the handoff; do not publish a placeholder sentence about quotation searching.

**Insights:** An invited supporter can help communicate symptoms and questions, write a note, observe a functional episode, pause a conversation or help a teenager speak to a clinician. They should not be assigned responsibility for determining FND causation, diagnosing a separate condition, challenging test validity, selecting medication, persuading every specialist, keeping a daily symptom tally, or managing a dependent adult's choices.

**Quality test:** Would a caring parent/spouse recognize themselves without feeling blamed? Is the person with FND the author of their own experience? Does the scene produce a new, sustainable way of helping rather than moralizing?

### 3. For Clinicians and the Care Team

**Teaching role:** Deepen a **real clinical decision**: understanding what a patient needs, assessing a coexisting/changed issue, keeping a referral appropriate, integrating constrained access, communicating respectfully, and retaining responsibility when ideal specialist services are unavailable.

**Voice:** Collegial, evidence-led, curious and clinically precise. Write **with** a knowledgeable practitioner rather than as a guideline commanding compliance. Prefer *family doctor* where appropriate to the Canadian narrative. A family doctor is central to this module but not the only relevant professional; don't pad every section with separate paragraphs for every discipline.

**Clinical reasoning:** The developed case style from Modules 7–10 uses:
- **What:** the specific patient question, observed facts, reported concerns, access constraints, strengths, uncertainty and what has not been established.
- **Why:** differential/coexisting conditions, mechanism hypotheses only when relevant, diagnostic/therapeutic evidence, biases such as diagnostic overshadowing, competing interpretations and the consequences of a choice.
- **How:** an individualized and proportionate clinical response, referral purpose, shared decision, reasonable accommodation, named follow-up, and meaningful outcome or reassessment point.

These are **reasoning goals**, not a requirement to print three robotic headings or offer a mandatory protocol. Real cases can end with unresolved disagreement, limited access and an honest plan rather than a perfect referral. Clinicians should know how to care **even when FND-specific rehabilitation is not accessible**.

**Source use:** Where a claim is clinically consequential, cite suitable original evidence/authoritative guidance and say what it supports (population and measured outcome when material). Clearly distinguish consensus from clinical-trial findings, cognitive test performance from real-world capacity, and the availability of a specialist from the implementation of specialist advice. A quote from a paper is never obligatory. Avoid falsely claiming a specific legal duty or standard of care based on a brief fictional scene. Detailed source verification and medico-legal judgments need designated independent review.

**Anti-repetition:** Do not reteach functional diagnostic signs, FND versus feigning, neuroplasticity, all neurological mechanisms, standard physiotherapy techniques, general biopsychosocial theory, or all of Module 10 pacing. Show how earlier understanding changes *this appointment, referral, reassessment, or collaboration*. The clinician section must be readable independently of the Person story, but need not retell it.

**Case quantity:** The two-clinical-cases pattern worked for some Module 10 lessons, but **Lesson 32 was deliberately shorter and focused on evidence and clinical decisions**. Choose one genuinely challenging case or two complementary cases only if they each earn their place. No fixed word count and no catalogue of diagnoses masquerading as depth.

**Quality test:** Would a clinician gain a practical insight for a real decision? Is the patient more than a list of symptoms? Does the scenario acknowledge why a reasonable clinician might disagree? Is the referral purpose, appropriate scope and review responsibility identifiable?

### A. A psychologist's causation formulation — assumptions worth examining, not an indictment of psychology

The reviewed records associate the 2020 assessor's formulation with:

- A **conversion disorder/FND** diagnosis plus a distinct **somatic symptom disorder** formulation. Psychological symptoms, health concerns, prior trauma, dissociation and financial/emotional strain were interpreted as clinically relevant vulnerability or contributing factors; the worker's business disruption and financial losses formed a prominent explanatory narrative about onset. These are **attributed interpretations in an insurer-requested causation assessment**, not scientifically established individual causal mechanisms.
- A distinction sometimes blurred in clinical conversations: **a positive diagnosis of FND**, a **psychological diagnosis**, **possible contributing associations**, **the reason a particular symptom appeared at a particular time**, and **what an individual patient's current healthcare needs are** are five different questions. A causation opinion may be useful for the commissioner's question but not establish the individual's unique neurological process or dictate which current concerns should be evaluated.
- Symptom-validity questionnaires were interpreted as showing unusually high symptom endorsement; later commentary discussed possible symptom exaggeration and the possibility of intentional embellishment, **while also noting limitations of such questionnaires for people with functional seizures** and the inability of one assessment to establish intent. **Neither the scores nor the reviewer's interpretation alone establishes deliberate fabrication.** In a public teaching case, do not portray a disability assessment's unusual-symptom score as a definitive lie detector or conversely as meaningless without authoritative evidence; clearly distinguish self-report, instrument interpretation, clinical context and actual demonstrated intent.
- The recorded formulation led to proposed psychological treatment. Psychotherapy can sometimes address relevant coexisting needs or FND symptoms, but **a treatment recommendation does not by itself confirm that trauma, stress or maladaptive coping caused an individual's FND**, or resolve another medical issue such as migraine.
- **Research task for Work:** independently examine contemporary authoritative FND diagnostic recommendations, appropriate limits of inferring individual causation from group trauma/stress associations, and published discussions of symptom-validity interpretation in FND/functional seizures. Evaluate **what the literature actually supports** rather than retroactively declaring the historical clinical report accurate or inaccurate. There is no independent expert review or misconduct finding here.

**Lesson fit:**  
- **33:** Show how knowing the difference between *having FND* and *knowing exactly why this person developed it* is a genuine literacy gain. Do not re-teach all causal theories; use one plain distinction to illustrate what the reader can now ask.
- **34:** Primary fit. A person may face a psychologist or doctor with a confidently expressed psychological causal account, while the person's own neurological and other medical care concerns remain unresolved. Model a respectful question about **what observation supports the diagnosis, what remains hypothetical, what the referral will accomplish, and which clinician owns the other concern**. The story is **not** “psychiatrists/psychologists are wrong”; acknowledge valuable psychological care, legitimate clinical uncertainty, the difference between an insurer's causal task and treatment, and room for disagreement.
- **35:** Secondary fit. Repeat assessments can produce conclusions without access to rehabilitation. Focus on purpose, expected value, available local follow-up and total cost, not the validity of a particular private report.
- **Avoid Module 12 overlap:** no comprehensive CBT/DBT or trauma-therapy discussion here; Module 11 is about interpretation, referral fit and clinical accountability.

### B. Wage-rate reconsideration: when deadlines and administrative capacity collide

The documents distinguish **three stages**, not an endlessly open wage-rate review:
1. An original **2019 long-term wage-rate decision** and a review request filed **39 days after the deadline**.
2. An **initial denial of extra filing time**, even though contemporaneous material described functional neurological symptoms and difficulties with memory and concentration.
3. A **2022 request to reconsider that time-extension denial**, submitted after FND had subsequently been accepted in the compensation claim, followed by a **January 2023 decision confirming the denial**. That decision did **not adjudicate a new wage amount on its merits**, because it did not grant the threshold extension/reconsideration request.

The worker's later submission described hospital episodes, cognitive and speech problems, severe sensory sensitivity, business closure, several moves and unstable housing, family/supporter responsibilities, missing or hard-to-obtain records, difficulty gathering financial evidence, disputed benefit/diagnostic recognition and years of pursuing other claims. These reflect **the worker's stated history and barriers**, not findings that every alleged administrative omission or act of withholding was established. The decision maker also considered procedural rules, the time elapsed, the nature of financial/tax evidence and the **worker's demonstrated ability to communicate and pursue other claims**. The refusal cannot accurately be portrayed as based on a single false statement about FND or ability, or as a decision on the underlying correct wage amount.

**Specific teaching question:** Can a person sometimes produce a long, coherent letter, research statutes or handle some forms, yet still be unable to consistently manage deadlines, complex paperwork or repeated follow-up **during an earlier period of disabling illness**? The existence of capability in one context or later period **does not by itself establish timely, consistent capacity in a different context**. But nor does a disability diagnosis by itself establish the legal criteria for an extension. This is an **important accessibility and fair-assessment question**, not a legal conclusion about the outcome of this appeal.

**Lesson fit:**  
- **33:** The ability to learn and advocate after years of experience is progress that may coexist with impaired memory, headaches and very uneven functioning; do not make accomplishments invalidate disability.
- **34:** When a professional uses evidence of *what someone managed once* as if it answered *what they can do consistently*, teach a calm distinction between setting, symptom variability, timing and functional demands. The patient's written presentation can be excellent while their need for accessible follow-up remains genuine.
- **35:** Primary fit. The **compounding burden** of difficult paperwork, delayed recognition, missing or disputed documents, rigid filing deadlines, multiple moves, financial instability, repeat appeals, and reliance on family/supporters can obstruct access. But this remains **healthcare-navigation and institutional-access teaching**, not advice about a particular wage formula, statutory extensions, legal proceedings, compensation appeals or how to challenge a decision. Detailed benefits and work/disability systems belong in **Module 16**. Distinguish clearly an insurer-required evaluation, an administrative appeal and appointments meant to diagnose or treat; they have different purposes and consequences.

**Authoring safeguards:** No verbatim private quotation. Do not assert that the insurer “hid” evidence or violated law based only on the worker's allegation; do not infer malingering from correspondence volume or from psychometric measures. Do not imply a patient's ability to complete a complex appeal proves they can work, or that every adverse decision is discrimination. Use de-identified *fictional composite circumstances* only with project-lead editorial approval. Be careful about jurisdiction: historical BC benefit and administrative procedures cannot be stated as current universal rules.
