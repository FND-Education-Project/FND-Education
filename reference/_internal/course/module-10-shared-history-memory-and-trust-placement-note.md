# Placement note — Shared history, memory gaps, trust and FND/FCD

**Status:** Unpublished editorial concept, parked for a future course revision. **Do not automatically insert, publish or present as a universal FND/FCD experience.** Requires project-lead editing, separate community/clinical context and both audits before publication.

**Source:** First-person lived experience supplied by the project lead during Lesson 11 editing on October 23, 2026. Preserve the insight without names, dates, relationship-identifying details or presumed clinical explanation.

## The idea to preserve

Living with significant memory and retrieval difficulties can affect much more than remembering appointments or tasks. Sometimes a person cannot access a shared event that someone close to them remembers confidently. Hearing a different account of their own history can feel deeply unsettling, even like being gaslit, while the other person may sincerely be describing what they recall. The person may wonder whether their own memory is incomplete or unavailable at that moment.

The most frightening part can be the feeling of losing **shared history** and a stable sense of what happened, especially when memory problems or dissociation are present. The emotional experience deserves validation without implying either person is deceptive.

Some couples or families may find it reassuring to **agree together on a short note about a particular event** while it is still accessible to both, if they choose to. Such a note is a practical shared reference, **not a test of credibility or proof that one person's account is always correct**. It should protect consent, privacy and the option to revisit an account as new information emerges.

### Candidate reader-facing passage (concept only, not approved text)

Imagine remembering a family outing in a different way from someone who was there with you. They sound certain. You search your memory, but the details won't come.

It's unsettling when your own story feels out of reach. And a disagreement can feel much bigger when you're already struggling to remember.

One option is to choose a few important moments and write down, together, what each of you remembers and agrees on. That shared note may help later, while leaving room for uncertainty and different perspectives.

The aim is to protect trust and give memories somewhere to rest—not to turn a relationship into a courtroom.

## Distinctions the eventual lesson must preserve

- **Experience versus explanation:** Feeling as though a memory disagreement is gaslighting does not establish intentional manipulation. Equally, genuine manipulation, coercion or abuse must not automatically be reinterpreted as a person's memory problem.
- **FCD versus other contributors:** Memory, recall, encoding, concentration, dissociation, medication effects, sleep, migraine and other medical conditions may differ. The experience alone does not diagnose FCD or prove which mechanism is responsible.
- **Shared notes:** Optional, consensual, very brief and revisable. Avoid a constant checking or reassurance loop, compulsory joint records or requiring a supporter to carry every memory.
- **Supporter wellbeing:** Agree on roles and capacity; a partner is not responsible for managing every cognitive symptom or adjudicating every difference in recollection.
- **Medical safety:** New or substantially changed memory/awareness problems warrant suitable clinical assessment, with urgency guided by the presentation.
