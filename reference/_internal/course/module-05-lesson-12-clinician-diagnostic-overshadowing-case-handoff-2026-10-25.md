## Purpose and conversation context

Robert asked whether a documented case of diagnostic overshadowing involving someone with FND could become the clinician section's clinical reasoning exercise. He wants to follow the published team's diagnostic process, with artistic additions where useful.

The agreed teaching approach is:

- Begin with a complicated presentation that permits several plausible explanations.
- Give the clinical information, then invite the reader to reason before revealing the interpretation.
- Move through evidence, reasoning and education at a measured pace.
- Keep a confident, professional clinician voice and positively framed explanations.
- Allow time to absorb each idea; give a small number of questions at each pause.

The younger reading level and casual metaphors used in the Person section do not replace the professional language requested for clinicians.

This handoff preserves the research recommendation and five-stage outline discussed in this chat. It does not reproduce the full uncommitted clinician draft from the other chat: that wording was not recovered.

## Recommended primary case

**Reference:** Das S, Yerraballa SRN, Shekhawat P. *Coexisting Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome on Autonomic Reflex Testing in Functional Neurologic Disorder.* American Journal of Case Reports. 2026;27:e953323. Published September 28, 2026.

- [DOI and publisher article](https://doi.org/10.12659/AJCR.953323).
- [Full article, including case report and limitations](https://amjcaserep.com/index.php/htmlGenerator/xmlMainBox/idArt/953323).
- [Table 2: reported clinical timeline](https://amjcaserep.com/abstract/table/idArt/953323/id/t2-amjcaserep-27-e953323).

### Published facts for the exercise

This compact fact bank is a paraphrased source summary. The labels below are editorial identifiers, not the authors' labels.

- **F1:** An 18-year-old woman had functional weakness and seizures supported by examination and previous video-EEG.
- **F2:** Standing worsened lightheadedness and leg heaviness; lying down helped. Earlier standing measurements were brief and nondiagnostic. Orthostatic symptoms had largely been attributed to FND, anxiety or deconditioning.
- **F3:** Repeat examination retained positive functional motor signs. The team quantified autonomic symptoms, reviewed medications and alternative causes, and arranged autonomic testing.
- **F4:** Tilt testing increased heart rate from 95 to 144 beats/min within ten minutes, reproducing symptoms without orthostatic hypotension. Blood pressure was elevated.
- **F5:** The authors considered blood-test findings, rhythm assessment and alternative explanations, diagnosing coexisting POTS.
- **F6:** POTS-directed management accompanied continuing FND care. Mobility improved during combined treatment; the authors could not assign causation to one intervention or explain every event through POTS.

### Editorial recommendation

Use this as the main exercise because it supports teaching a formulation that can retain existing evidence while making room for additional evidence. The task should be to explain the presentation as fully and proportionately as possible.

This is a case-based teaching choice, not evidence about how frequently diagnostic overshadowing occurs or a reason to generalize a single person's investigation pathway to all people with FND.

## Five-stage teaching outline

The questions and teaching aims below are our proposed educational additions. They are not a transcript of the treating team's discussion. Refer to the fact bank for the clinical content rather than expanding it from memory.

### Stage 1 — Begin with the established diagnosis

**Material to reveal:** F1.

**Reasoning questions:**

- Which findings support FND?
- How far does that evidence extend when assessing the person's other symptoms?

**After the pause:** Explain the distinction between evidence supporting a diagnosis and the scope of symptoms that evidence explains. Let readers assess the basis of the existing diagnosis before moving forward.

### Stage 2 — Introduce the unresolved pattern

**Material to reveal:** F2.

**Reasoning questions:**

- What deserves further assessment?
- What can—and cannot—be concluded from the earlier measurements?

**After the pause:** Compare the information available at different assessments. Ask what clinical question remains open and what information would help answer it. Acknowledge the person's description as part of the evidence being considered.

### Stage 3 — Show the reassessment

**Material to reveal:** F3.

**Reasoning questions:**

- Can both sets of findings be valid?
- What question should the next investigation answer?

**After the pause:** Show how the team can retain supported findings and investigate unresolved features. Distinguish selecting an investigation for a defined question from repeating investigations without a clear purpose.

### Stage 4 — Reveal the test results before naming the diagnosis

**Material to reveal:** F4 first. Invite the reader's interpretation before disclosing F5.

**Reasoning questions:**

- How does this change your formulation?
- Which symptoms remain incompletely explained?

**After the pause:** Present the authors' interpretation and the alternative explanations they considered. Keep the observations, the diagnostic conclusion and the remaining uncertainty visibly separate. Explain clinical terms as they enter the discussion.

### Stage 5 — Finish with care and uncertainty

**Material to reveal:** F6.

**Reasoning question:**

- What should now change in the symptom map, clinical handover and individual safety plan?

**After the pause:** Invite a practical account of what the person and care team now need to communicate, coordinate and review. Preserve the limits of what follow-up can establish.

## Secondary case retained for comparison

**Reference:** Berry AJ, Wiethoff S. *Revising a diagnosis of functional neurological disorder—a case report.* Oxford Medical Case Reports. 2020;2020(9):omaa073. Published September 22, 2020.

- [DOI and full paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/omcr/omaa073).
- [Publisher article](https://academic.oup.com/omcr/article/2020/9/omaa073/5909755).

**Published summary:** A 62-year-old woman with progressive voice problems, difficulty opening her eyes and falls initially received a functional voice disorder diagnosis. Progression, repeated examination and further investigations led to a clinical diagnosis of progressive supranuclear palsy. Indoor sunglasses had been interpreted as supporting FND, although they reduced aggravation of blepharospasm. The authors explicitly discuss diagnostic overshadowing and consider that functional symptoms may also have remained.

**Editorial use:** This could support a separate exercise about revisiting a previous formulation. Keep its distinction from the recommended primary exercise clear: the published account involves revision of the original diagnosis. It is not interchangeable with a case selected to discuss a retained diagnosis alongside additional findings.

## Bringing the case alive

Use the staged release of information as the main narrative device. A pseudonym, modest scene-setting or imagined dialogue can help with readability if clearly identified as additions.

Keep three categories explicit while drafting:

- **Reported:** Symptoms, examination findings, investigations, chronology and outcomes described in the paper.
- **Educational interpretation:** Our questions, comparison of possible explanations and teaching commentary.
- **Fictional addition:** Any invented name, dialogue or scene detail.

Suggested attribution for a future vignette:

> Educational vignette based on the cited case report. Any invented name, setting or dialogue is identified as fictional. Teaching questions and commentary were developed for this course.

Only use that wording when it accurately describes the finished vignette. At this handoff stage, no patient name, scene or dialogue has been invented.

Preserve the distinction between what the authors report the team did and how we reconstruct the reasoning for teaching. Describe undocumented thought processes as questions for the reader.

## Editorial checks before a future lesson draft is finalized

These are our analysis and drafting checks, not additional facts asserted about the patient:

- Keep the dates of earlier assessments distinct from later assessments. A later diagnosis cannot by itself establish what was present years earlier.
- Consult the full paper before expanding medication timing, investigation exclusions or the detailed diagnostic criteria. Avoid making an exclusion more certain than the report supports.
- Present questionnaire findings as one contribution to assessment; check the source before describing their diagnostic role.
- Preserve relevant blood pressure findings if the vignette expands the test results or discusses treatment. Keep management individualized rather than turning the case into a universal regimen.
- Preserve the difference between reported improvement and a demonstrated treatment effect.
- Check that every symptom attribution has support. Retain uncertainty where the report leaves an issue unresolved.
- Use the article's Case Report, Table 2 and Limitations to verify chronology and scope. Check the original paper for any further clinical details before adding them.
- Keep clear explanations of why assessment or non-investigation is appropriate to the particular clinical question.
- Retain the project's usual source checks, two editorial audits and clinical review for the later reader-facing lesson. This handoff does not claim that those reviews have occurred.
