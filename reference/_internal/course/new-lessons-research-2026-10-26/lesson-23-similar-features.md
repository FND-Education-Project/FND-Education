# Research brief — proposed Lesson 23: Similar Experiences, Different Features

**October 26, 2026 · Internal authoring material · Not a published lesson**

[Brief index](README.md) · [Source register](sources.md)

## Teaching purpose and explanations to develop

Use comparisons as vocabulary training, not a symptom quiz that secretly diagnoses. Keep timing, movement, awareness, responsiveness and memory on separate axes. Multiple features may coexist; vocabulary narrows a question rather than establishing a cause.

## Researched foundation and its limits

[R10] Positive diagnosis and differential assessment cannot be replaced by appearance alone. [R11] Typical event characterization and possible coexisting epilepsy matter in seizure assessment. Neither a single visible sign nor missing recall is a sufficient course-level diagnostic rule.

Sources: [R10](sources.md#r10), [R11](sources.md#r11). See the register for source type, access limits and publication details. These are drafting claims, not a completed independent clinical audit.

## Material for the three audience sections

Person: Pair A describes repeated back-and-forth shaking; B describes isolated sudden movements. Useful next question: “Is there a repeated rhythm, separate jolts, or both?” Pair C hears speech but cannot answer; D has no later memory of being spoken to. Useful question: “What was experienced and what was observed?” Pair E feels spinning; F feels about to faint. Ask for the person's own meaning of dizzy. No pair ends with “therefore FND.”

Supporter: Acknowledge uncertainty and report observations without staging an event or testing responsiveness repeatedly. A known personal safety plan governs familiar episodes; immediate danger changes the response.

Clinician: Describe what additional history, examination or testing would change the differential. Cases are incomplete intentionally. Differentiate phenomenology from etiology; include coexisting conditions and diagnostic overshadowing.

## Reflection, application and boundaries

Optional task: choose the question that would clarify a description, not the diagnosis. Keep examples brief and readable; do not make the lesson an encyclopedia. Do not suggest that all rhythmic movement is tremor, all jerks are functional, or all falls without recall are seizures.
