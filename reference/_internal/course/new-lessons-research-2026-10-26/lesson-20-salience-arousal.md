# Research brief — proposed Lesson 20: Salience, Arousal, and Feedback

**October 26, 2026 · Internal authoring material · Not a published lesson**

## Teaching purpose and explanations to develop

Salience means importance for current processing: a welcome voice or a desired object can be salient, not only danger. Arousal means activation/readiness and is broader than anxiety. Attention concerns prioritization; salience concerns what attracts priority. A feedback loop describes possible interactions, not one proven direction of causation.

## Researched foundation and its limits

[R08] General resting-state imaging distinguished salience-related and executive-control networks; this is not an FND diagnostic study. [R04] Salience/arousal are components of a proposed FND framework. Do not read one person's brain state from sweating, distress or symptom severity.

Sources: [R04](sources.md#r04), [R08](sources.md#r08). See the register for source type, access limits and publication details. These are drafting claims, not a completed independent clinical audit.

## Material for the three audience sections

Person: A conversation becomes harder when pain and noise compete with it; repeated requests for an immediate answer add another demand. Draw a possible loop using “may” arrows. Offer more than one entry point: quieter surroundings, written information, pain care, or ending the conversation. No claim is made that any one adjustment stops symptoms.

Supporter: Practise a fictional exchange in which the person asks for time and receives it. Calm support can be kind and useful even if symptoms persist.

Clinician: Discuss reverse causation and shared influences. Distress can follow disability. Assess new autonomic/systemic symptoms independently rather than translating them automatically into anxiety. Distinguish network associations from an individual formulation.

## Reflection, application and boundaries

Optional question: “Which part of this situation could be made less demanding?” No requirement to locate a trigger or regulate perfectly. Avoid “your nervous system thinks everything is dangerous,” “vagus reset,” universal fight-or-flight explanations, and repeated pulse monitoring.
