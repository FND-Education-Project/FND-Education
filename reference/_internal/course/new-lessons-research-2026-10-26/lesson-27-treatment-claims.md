# Research brief — proposed Lesson 27: Evidence, Expectations and Treatment Claims

**October 26, 2026 · Internal authoring material · Not a published lesson**

## Teaching purpose and explanations to develop

Teach a compact appraisal sequence: who was studied; what was compared; what outcome and time point; how much uncertainty; harms/burden/cost; relevance to this person. A mechanism story, testimonial or before/after clip cannot substitute for those questions.

## Researched foundation and its limits

[R13] Preserve Physio4FMD's primary-versus-secondary distinction and active comparator. [R16] CODES compared seizure-specific CBT plus standardized care with standardized care: the primary monthly seizure-frequency outcome at 12 months was not significantly different, although several secondary outcomes improved. [R11] The later AAN guideline judges psychological interventions possibly effective based on broader evidence. These statements answer different questions, not contradictory universal verdicts.

Sources: [R13](sources.md#r13), [R16](sources.md#r16), [R11](sources.md#r11). See the register for source type, access limits and publication details. These are drafting claims, not a completed independent clinical audit.

## Material for the three audience sections

Person: Compare fictional advertisements: “rewire in six weeks” versus a service explaining which people it treats, plausible benefits, uncertainties, risks and costs. Ask what evidence would make either claim more trustworthy. Do not name or accuse a real provider without separate verification.

Supporter: Help with reading or questions if invited; avoid using a success story to pressure someone into treatment.

Clinician: Discuss applicability, missing data, adverse events and secondary outcomes. State conflicts of interest and distinguish package efficacy from a proposed mechanism. Agree outcomes that include participation and quality of life.

## Reflection, application and boundaries

Optional output: one question to ask before a treatment decision. Do not generate an individual's success percentage from a group average, describe a nonsignificant result as proof of no benefit, or treat improvement as proof that prior symptoms were voluntary. There is no cure for FND; discuss outcomes without promising one.
