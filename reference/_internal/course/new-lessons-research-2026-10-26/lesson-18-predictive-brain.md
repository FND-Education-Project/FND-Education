# Research brief — proposed Lesson 18: The Predictive Brain

**October 26, 2026 · Internal authoring material · Not a published lesson**

[Brief index](README.md) · [Source register](sources.md)

## Teaching purpose and explanations to develop

Distinguish an ordinary expectation from a conscious belief and a formal predictive-processing model. In the model, prior information and incoming evidence contribute to perception and action; prediction error is their mismatch, not a personal mistake. Precision means estimated reliability in the model, not willpower or certainty that a person is right.

## Researched foundation and its limits

[R03] The Bayesian paper proposes an account of functional motor/sensory symptoms; it is a theory paper, not a diagnostic or treatment trial. [R04] The later framework describes interacting constructs with incomplete circuit knowledge. These sources do not establish a single explanation of an individual's symptom.

Sources: [R03](sources.md#r03), [R04](sources.md#r04). See the register for source type, access limits and publication details. These are drafting claims, not a completed independent clinical audit.

## Material for the three audience sections

Person: Someone lifts an empty mug expecting it to contain tea and uses more force than needed. Tell the scene, explain the expectation–feedback mismatch, then state that this ordinary example does not reproduce FND. Revisit the missing-stair analogy only in words. Follow with a person asking for clear information before a familiar appointment: reducing uncertainty can be worthwhile without proving a predictive mechanism.

Supporter: Expectations often arise without a conscious decision. Avoid “expect to improve and you will.” Invite the person to describe an experience that does not fit the explanation.

Clinician: Separate computational constructs from measured variables and retrospective narrative. Consider competing models and phenotype-specific data. Do not infer altered priors solely from symptoms persisting despite reassurance.

## Reflection, application and boundaries

Optional question: “What information would help me prepare for one ordinary activity?” Never test expectations with a concealed hazard, deceptive cue or actual missing step. The mug is a teaching scene, not an experiment. Keep the historical paper title in bibliography only.
