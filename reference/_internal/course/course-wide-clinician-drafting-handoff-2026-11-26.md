## 1. The educational purpose

**Teach how clinicians examine evidence and reason toward a proportionate decision; do not tell them how to practise their profession.** Each section must offer a **distinct new clinical inference**, not rehearse the Person section or the elementary science of prior modules.

Three interlocking questions discipline the authoring, but they are **not mandatory printed headings**:

- **What?** What is observed; what is the patient's goal; what is known versus missing; what else could matter in this particular context?
- **Why?** What interpretations compete; what does relevant research, professional guidance or existing uncertainty support; what does it **not** establish?
- **How?** What proportionate, shared decision or next question follows; who is realistically equipped to help; what observation or outcome would lead to revision?

**Order is flexible.** A short, credible professional case can open the lesson, followed by reasoning and relevant research. An important research question can also lead, followed by a standalone clinical application. Two concise but different dilemmas may be useful, or one developed case, or a case-free closing evidence guide. Choose the vehicle that yields new learning, not a mandatory case count.

## 2. Evidence as a reasoning tool

Use a study because its design, comparison, population, outcome or limitation **changes the reader's interpretation or decision**, rather than to decorate the page with a citation.

Distinguish explicitly when material:
- **Observation** from **interpretation**; a single clinic observation from reliable everyday function.
- **Original diagnosis/cause** from a potentially changeable present process, treatment target, associated condition or consequence.
- **Clinical consensus/authoritative guidance** from **direct comparative evidence**.
- **Association, hypothesis, mechanism and treatment effect**; a benefit from a multi-component intervention does not identify the active ingredient or prove the patient's original cause.
- **Study population, comparator, primary/secondary outcomes, timing, precision and limitations**; never extrapolate effectiveness to a different FND presentation without evidence.
- **Individual practical goal or participation** from a symptom outcome; an aid may enable an activity without changing FND symptoms.
- **Competing medical diagnoses** from known FND; do not let established FND replace appropriate consideration of a changing medical problem.

Put the *relevant* amount of evidence in readable prose. Avoid both a statistical catalogue and long defensive disclaimer cascades. Preserve source IDs and citation relevance; keep detailed methods/source apparatus in the lesson's source notes and citation system, and flag original-source verification as pending when it is. A caregiver account or fictional character contributes perspective, not scientific proof.

## 3. Professional voice and style

Write for knowledgeable, thoughtful interdisciplinary practitioners in **collegial, curious, precise language**, not a rule-making voice. Make a professional's first inclination reasonable. A disagreement should represent two legitimate considerations rather than a competent hero correcting a foolish colleague. Give the clinician a distinct viewpoint and show what information could actually change it.

Use **connected paragraphs with substantial thoughts**, natural, purposeful dialogue, and restrained headings. Avoid sentence-fragment dramatics, densely stacked lists, obligatory What/Why/How headings, long technique recipes, made-up forms or referral/medical record templates, and duplicated closing morals. Define specialist terms only where a distinction is central; do not explain routine clinician vocabulary or repeat earlier FND neuroscience. Let the implication become clear once; end with **one earned clinical insight**.

**Length is determined by the intellectual task**, not a fixed 700–1,000-word rule. The approved Module 12 clinician sections ranged approximately 1,067–1,647 words. Tighten repeated claims and caveats before cutting the clinical decision's substance.

## 4. How to use fictional cases and cross-audience continuity

- Prefer returning established course characters **when a fresh clinical question arises**. Preserve personal history and previously approved decisions. A clinician section should be intelligible without the Person section: give only enough essential context to establish the dilemma.
- Where helpful, add **one short optional link** inviting interested clinicians to read the relevant Person account, e.g. *“For the experience behind this situation, see [Aliyah's account](../../course/...#for-the-person-with-fnd).”* Use correct **relative path for the actual clinician lesson**; avoid a duplicated scene or an insistence that the reader must switch sections.
- One or two cases are legitimate when they test *different* uncertainties. A second case must add a new inference, not a different face for the same principle.
- The clinician is the **point-of-view problem solver**. The person with FND still sets relevant goals, supplies crucial real-life evidence, and retains informed choice.
- Decisions may remain imperfect or unresolved. Access and rehabilitation, or one clinician's expertise and another's, can coexist with real tensions. Avoid fictional miracles or invented certainty.

## 5. Practical authoring and review gates

Before drafting: identify a **single worthwhile clinical question**, which profession is genuinely relevant, the competing interpretations, any evidence needed to distinguish them, and the case's new learning compared with earlier lessons.

Draft one audience section at a time. In Chat, seek project-lead approval of a meaningful case direction and then of the *actual full prose*. Approved wording and user edits govern the next revision; do not quietly overwrite them. A handoff or research brief is **not** approval of the public lesson.

Before submission: ask whether a clinician could say what was observed, which research supports the interpretation, what remains unknown and **what new fact would change the decision**. Remove repeated recaps, basic science and lists that mimic a treatment protocol. Preserve the exact Person and Supporter texts and assets when editing a Clinician section.

After editorial approval: an independent research/source audit should check original-study relevance, comparison, outcomes, quotations, citation/source index mapping, clinical accuracy, possible omissions, and scope of claims. Editorial approval is **not** independent clinical review, accessibility acceptance, final CI or merge authorization.

## 6. Module 13 application — planning, not approved clinician prose

The module moves from **task analysis** (Lesson 39) through **mobility/transfer and equipment trade-offs** (Lesson 40) and **interacting sensory/home/communication access** (Lesson 41) to **reviewing a real-world aid over time** (Lesson 42). OT, physiotherapy, speech-language, medical and equipment services enter where relevant to a specific decision rather than being represented by a roll-call of occupations. Clinical consensus about FND adaptations and aids must not be presented as proof of specific long-term equipment efficacy or harms.

A proposed Lesson 39 clinician opening may follow an OT observing Aliyah's laundry routine: she can complete individual steps but her ability to finish the activity and participate later may vary. The central inference concerns the difference between a successful isolated movement and a **reliable, meaningful whole task**. A second short case is worthwhile only if it adds an **independent distinction** (for example, a bathroom task where privacy, transfer safety and fatigue change the question). Avoid duplicating the Person narrative. Proposed cases, research and text still require separate lead discussion and original-source review.
