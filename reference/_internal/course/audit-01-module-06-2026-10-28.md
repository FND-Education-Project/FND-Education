## Evidence checks and limits

- **Corbetta and Shulman (2002), 0307:** [original indexed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11994752/) supports interacting goal-directed and stimulus-driven attention systems. It is a general neuroscience review, not an FND mechanism or treatment trial. The lesson appropriately states that limit. Direct page retrieval encountered a challenge; primary indexed text was available.
- **Huys et al. (2021), 0220:** [original indexed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34145898/) and [article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8677517/) were checked through primary indexed material. The lesson's direction is reasonable; E3 makes its measurement and generalizability visible. An experiment manipulating attention supports task effects, not universal attribution of FND or established clinical effectiveness of the fictional tasks.
- **Dayan and Cohen (2011), 0306:** [original abstract and figures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22078504/) and [indexed full article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3217208/) distinguish acquisition, consolidation and retention across distributed motor networks. Human and animal research are reviewed. This supports the general learning explanation, not a demonstration that an individual patient's motor memory is intact.
- **Nielsen et al. (2015), 0028:** [original article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4602268/) provides specialist motor-physiotherapy consensus, including purposeful movement, attention and individual cues. Primary indexed passages were available; continuous direct full-page access was blocked. Use only the relevant motor-care claims: historical recommendations about ME/CFS exercise or attributing pain/fatigue to one process are not imported into these lessons.
- **Nicholson et al. (2020), 0011:** [original indexed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32732388/) confirms an expert-consensus process, with meaningful activity and individual adaptation rather than trial proof of a particular cue. Existing professional-role and activity-analysis applications fit that scope.
- **Baker et al. (2021), 0025:** [publisher article](https://jnnp.bmj.com/content/92/10/1112) checked through indexed full text. It covers automatic and volitional communication, procedural sequences, meaningful tasks and generalization. These are symptom-specific professional recommendations; the lesson correctly preserves alternative communication and assessment.
- **Ball et al. (2020), 0071:** [publisher article](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/143/10/2895/5892366) and author-hosted/indexed material support positive clinical assessment and internal inconsistency. The teaching correctly avoids diagnosing FCD from one lapse or a separate motor diagnosis. The [authors' 2021 clarification](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/144/9/e73/6353019) also notes that reliability and operationalization need further research; no simple bedside performance contrast is presented as a validated standalone test here.
- **Malaty et al. (2022), 0110:** [original indexed review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36362696/) and [article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9656241/) support presentation-specific clinical interpretation for functional tic-like phenomena. The narrow use in Lesson 17 is appropriate; the manuscript does not turn distractibility alone into a tic diagnosis.

The three earlier community excerpts (0308–0310) retain their recorded October 26 indexed-context verification. Fresh direct retrieval failed on October 28, and current searches often returned this project's own pages. Those self-reproductions are not independent verification. No new verification date is claimed for them. Q048 differs because it had already been held back and never adequately verified with its speaker context.

## Safety and symptom coverage

Existing boundaries are useful and should remain: medically assessed diagnoses; cognitive symptoms considered independently; professional oversight for falls, transfers, painful fixed postures and unsafe grip; no removal of aids to test symptoms; and no demand that supporters reproduce a clinic response. Optional activities are low risk, consent-based and allow rest or postponement. The learned-movement model is explicitly limited, rather than extended to functional seizures or every bodily process.

Motor examples are prominent because automatic motor control is the topic. Cognitive access, communication, sensory demands, fatigue, tic-like symptoms, jerks and fixed postures also appear. Further symptom examples should teach the relevant principle rather than imply that one attentional technique applies to every symptom. No mandatory diversity rewrite is indicated in this pass.
