## Lesson 30 — What Uses Your Available Capacity?

### Case 30A · Same route, different sequence

**Observed facts.** A person with an established functional gait disorder uses an agreed aid for the short route from reception to physiotherapy. On Monday, they wait upright before walking and a staff member explains two changes to their appointment while they walk. They arrive without additional physical help, but can repeat only one change. They also report brief dimming of vision during the wait. On Thursday, the same route and two comparable pieces of information are handled in a different order. They hear the details while seated and walk afterward. They arrive with the usual aid and can repeat both details. The team needs a plan for check-in on busier days.

**Information left open.** Whether staff, pace or assistance differed, what the person had already done that day, and whether the dimming is familiar and consistently posture-related. Both upright time and the timing of information changed.

**Question for clinicians.** Which observation should change the next assessment? What would a fair comparison tell you about both walking and understanding the message?

- **What.** Both visits involved arrival with support. Communication was more accurate at the second visit. The upright episode adds a symptom with a separate time course. Establish the person's usual gait, near-falls, aid use, duration upright and the exact posture and symptom sequence.
- **Why.** Delivering information during a walk adds a concurrent task, and a long upright wait adds a different demand before the walk. The visit comparison gives a reason to try a different sequence. It leaves the relative contribution of each change open. Gait studies tested particular tasks in selected groups. They give a basis for comparing conditions, with limited applicability to someone using an aid or reporting presyncope. Review posture-linked symptoms medically on their own evidence.
- **How.** Agree to preserve the established aid and offer essential information while seated before the next necessary walk. Record the person's two chosen outcomes, safe completion with the agreed assistance and accurate understanding of the instructions. Review the dimming separately with the family doctor and arrange appropriate assessment if it is new or recurrent. Use safe clinical observation and history to answer the question.
- **New inference.** An apparently successful combined task can conceal a decline in its second outcome. Changing sequence can improve the task as a whole. [Sources 1, 2 and 4.]

### Case 30B · Captions help, and a second stream competes for attention

**Observed facts.** A person with assessed functional cognitive symptoms and migraine attends a short online training session. With captions on, they can follow a single presenter and complete each step in an on-screen form. When people begin posting new instructions and questions in the chat pane, they switch back and forth and enter a response in the wrong field. The host suggests turning off the captions and chat to simplify the screen. The person says they miss important words when captions are unavailable, particularly when their headache is worse.

**Information left open.** Whether the presenter or the group chat introduced the change in the form, how well the captions match what was said, and whether the person has similar difficulty when messages are presented after the form is complete.

**Question for clinicians.** Which stream is helping the task? Which is asking the person to start a different task, and how would you check that changing its timing improves access?

- **What.** Completion of the form has two parts, understanding the instruction and applying it to the correct field. The person reports that captions help with the first. The error followed a shift between sources. The relative contributions of switching, visual symptoms and the quality of the audio or captions remain open.
- **Why.** Captions can supply the same message in an accessible format. A chat pane can introduce a separate goal. The functional cost depends on what the information asks the person to do. The cognitive group study supports asking about attention and switching. Its test results do not explain this person's error or validate a screen arrangement. Clinically changed visual or migraine symptoms deserve appropriate assessment.
- **How.** Keep the captions and, with consent, arrange for separate questions or changed instructions to be addressed after each form step is complete. At the next comparable session, ask whether the person could follow the words, resume the form accurately and take part in questions. Use one agreed measure of effort or repeat explanation if helpful. Review communication and device accessibility with OT as needed.
- **New inference.** Two simultaneous sources can do different jobs. Preserve the one that supplies access and change the timing of the one that introduces a competing goal. [Sources 2 and 3.]

## Lesson 31 — Flexible Pacing When Capacity Changes

### Case 31A · One recovery period and a second unexplained dip

**Observed facts.** A person with video-EEG-documented functional seizures has their familiar event after a social visit. A witness describes the usual event pattern. They are tired and slow to find words immediately afterward. By evening they report being back to their usual baseline. Two mornings later, they cancel a planned outing because of a new stretch of exhaustion and headache. They report no recognized event in the interval. The team is considering whether to shorten all visits.

**Information left open.** Sleep, migraine pattern, medication timing, interim activities, whether an unwitnessed episode is plausible, and whether the later worsening also occurs on days without a recognized event.

**Question for clinicians.** What can you reasonably link to the event, what remains open about the later worsening, and what change is justified while you learn more?

- **What.** Immediate symptoms followed the witnessed familiar event and reportedly resolved that evening. The later decline has a gap and an incomplete timeline. Use the person's account, their usual return to baseline and witness information if available. Identify any materially changed features or failure to recover as expected.
- **Why.** The immediate sequence is compatible with post-event recovery. The later symptoms could reflect activity, migraine, sleep, another event or another contributor. Fatigue is an outcome shared across conditions. A typical documented functional seizure identifies that event type. A new event pattern warrants its own assessment and consideration of epilepsy or syncope where relevant. Neither onset time nor fatigue alone assigns an event type or establishes PEM.
- **How.** Keep the existing agreed response for familiar events and let the person choose a manageable next visit with room for recovery. At review, use a brief account of naturally occurring days to compare event timing, activity and return to baseline. Coordinate medical review for changed events or an atypical recovery under the existing safety plan. Revisit the length or timing of visits if a repeatable pattern and the person's priorities support a change.
- **New inference.** One day may contain several clinically relevant time courses. Identifying where the person returned to baseline helps distinguish a continuous recovery period from a later problem. [Sources 2, 5 and 6.]

### Case 31B · The appointment looks tolerable and the next two days change

**Observed facts.** A person with assessed functional leg weakness completes a short rehabilitation session and is still able to talk comfortably at the end. On several occasions, they report that marked exhaustion, diffuse aching and more difficulty thinking begin the following day and persist into the second day. A lengthy seated online meeting has preceded a similar decline. They describe a reduction in everyday activity over the past two months following an illness. A therapist proposes a weekly increase because the session itself looked comfortable.

**Information left open.** The person's earlier baseline, frequency and duration of the full pattern, unrefreshing sleep, migraine course, new medications or illness, upright symptoms, and whether other explanations better fit the history. The selected examples do not show how often they occur after comparable activity or arise on quieter days.

**Question for clinicians.** What would you need to know before progressing the program, and what care can continue while the pattern is investigated?

- **What.** The repeated account includes delayed, disproportionate worsening across more than one symptom and after both physical and cognitive demands. Ask when it starts, how long it lasts, what activity becomes impossible and when the person recovers. Record the reduction from their earlier baseline and any new findings.
- **Why.** This pattern raises possible PEM and merits assessment for ME/CFS and other contributors. The rest of the clinical history and examination matter. NICE criteria also consider debilitating fatigue, unrefreshing sleep or sleep disturbance, cognitive difficulties, reduced activity and the persistence of symptoms. NG206 supports assessment when ME/CFS is suspected. Its diagnostic recommendation requires the full symptom pattern to persist for three months and other explanations to be considered. Use the case's two-month history to plan assessment and support now. FND occupational therapy consensus supplies a separate basis for individualizing ordinary activities. Improvement in motor performance during a session cannot establish later tolerability.
- **How.** Revisit the proposed increase with the person and coordinate family-doctor assessment. While investigating, prioritize the person's essential activities, agreed support and rehabilitation they have found tolerable across the relevant recovery period. Use experience from ordinary days to inform review. Leave automatic escalation and deliberate symptom provocation out of the case. If the clinical assessment supports ME/CFS, coordinate with appropriate expertise and apply its condition-specific activity guidance.
- **New inference.** The appropriate observation window depends on the symptom pattern. A positive in-session result can guide a movement goal while the delayed response governs whether a proposed dose is sustainable. [Sources 2, 6 and 7.]

## Lesson 32 — Balancing Practice, Rest, and Everyday Life

### Case 32A · Voice improves and the person wants a different balance

**Observed facts.** After laryngeal assessment and speech-language therapy for functional dysphonia, a person reports being able to complete a valued weekly phone call. Their therapist also hears improvement in connected speech. The person has migraine and reports that travel and waiting for the second weekly session leave little room for a chosen evening group and the following morning's household work. Their main goal is keeping the phone call and returning to the group. They ask whether they can keep getting help with their voice with fewer clinic visits.

**Information left open.** Which treatment elements need in-person assessment, whether the gain persists with fewer visits, whether a remote or local option is accessible, and how much of the cost comes from the treatment itself versus the journey, waiting or timing.

**Question for clinicians.** Which outcome would justify retaining the current schedule, and how would the team learn whether a less demanding plan preserves the gain the person values?

- **What.** Benefit already appears outside therapy. The person reports a competing participation cost. Compare the agreed communication target, total effort and recovery, including other medical care, and what the person can do in the rest of the week.
- **Why.** A functional voice trial supports selected voice therapy. It cannot determine the necessary frequency, delivery method or trade-off for this person. Participation is a treatment outcome in its own right. A plan can be effective on one measure and too demanding in its current form. Address changed migraine symptoms through the appropriate care pathway.
- **How.** Agree a time-limited review of a feasible change in frequency or delivery with speech-language therapy. Keep necessary in-person assessment available. At the review, compare whether the phone call remains possible, whether the person can attend the group and whether recovery demand changed. If voice function deteriorates or the alternative delivery method imposes new barriers, reconsider together. Discuss any shift of work onto the person or a supporter.
- **New inference.** A decision about continuing a helpful treatment requires a comparison of benefits the person wants to retain and the participation that different ways of delivering care allow. [Sources 8–11.]

### Case 32B · The supported shopping trip works

**Observed facts.** A person with assessed functional visual loss previously needed help to read short labels in clinic. At two recent visits they read several high-contrast labels in a quiet room. At their local shop, an accessible list and a scheduled support visit help them locate products and check labels when packaging is hard to read. On a week when that visit was cancelled, they bought only a few familiar items and left without ingredients for planned meals. A service review asks whether the reading improvement supports reducing the visit.

**Information left open.** Whether earlier and recent clinic tasks were comparable, how often the person can manage the ordinary shop, which part of the visit requires assistance and what alternative help is reliably available. The cancelled visit also changed when they went to the shop.

**Question for clinicians.** Which finding describes an ability under specified conditions, what makes shopping possible in the current environment, and what would justify changing a particular part of the support?

- **What.** Clinic success was observed on short, accessible samples. The person's ordinary shopping includes technology and another person's time. A missed visit coincided with a narrower shop. Find out whether the support is needed for navigation, reading unfamiliar labels, completing the list or another part of the task.
- **Why.** Use the ICF distinction between capacity in specified conditions and performance in the current environment. Here *available capacity* remains the course's descriptive term. The support can be part of why a gain is usable. Treatment evidence for functional visual loss remains limited. The short clinic task cannot establish the cause of every visual symptom, independent shopping or eligibility for a service.
- **How.** Document the observed gain and current assistance separately, with task conditions, frequency and consequences over ordinary weeks. Keep reliable access while OT and the relevant team consider a backup for specific tasks the support visit makes possible. Agree what observation would justify changing one component, including the person's ability to obtain what they need when conditions vary. Assess new visual changes medically. Review the support using the actual activity and safely observed information.
- **New inference.** Improvement can be conditional on supports already in place. Identify which task a support enables before deciding whether the person can manage that task with a different arrangement. [Sources 2, 9, 12 and 13.]

## Sources and verification

Source IDs below were checked against the citation index at the reviewed PR head. Where no matching entry was found, reconcile the source during authoring if selected. Study findings and guidance were checked in the indicated original material on November 11, 2026.

1. **Sandri et al., *J Neural Transm*, 2025 issue, online 2024. Direct FND observation.** Cross-sectional convenience sample of 87 adults with functional gait disorder and 48 healthy controls. Compared single walking with motor, cognitive and visual fixation tasks. The group-by-task interaction for swing time SD and stride time appeared in the cognitive comparison. Stride time variability did not discriminate groups well. The sample excluded people needing a device to maintain upright posture and comorbidities interfering with gait. The study measures brief gait performance with healthy controls, and its estimates do not predict individual fall risk, diagnosis or recovery. **Verified in Methods and Results.** No matching ID found for this DOI. Gandolfi et al. 2023, FND-CIT-0132, is a different study. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00702-024-02829-4) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11735589/).
2. **Nicholson et al., *JNNP*, 2020. FND occupational therapy consensus.** Specialist consensus for FND across presentations recommends reviewing everyday roles, routines, environments and aids, and observing meaningful activity. It includes fatigue management and individualized equipment decisions. It discusses short-term aids with a review plan when necessary for safe acute care, and adaptations for persistent disability. The recommendations are practice guidance, with no measured efficacy estimate for a particular pacing schedule or a rule for withdrawing equipment. **Verified in the original full text, assessment, fatigue and aids sections.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2019-322281) · [FND-CIT-0011](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0011).
3. **Pick et al., *Psychological Medicine*, 2026. Direct FND cognitive data.** Case-control testing compared 50 participants with functional seizures, 50 with functional motor symptoms, 50 healthy controls and 50 controls with depression or anxiety. Both FND groups reported more cognitive symptoms and showed difficulty on sustained attention and set-shifting tests. Group performance validity was comparable. The data concern the tested groups and tasks, with no measurement of the effect of interruptions, sensory exposures, or a combined everyday activity. **Verified in the original journal article, abstract and Methods.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291726103420) · [FND-CIT-0326](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0326).
4. **Wieling et al., *Lancet Neurology*, 2022. Clinical review, orthostatic hypotension.** The review describes evaluating the relationship between symptoms and posture with a history, examination and appropriate recumbent/upright measurements. It distinguishes forms of blood-pressure change by timing and discusses alternative causes. This gives a clinical route when posture-related symptoms accompany an FND activity. There is no FND comparison or proof of the cause of the illustrative dimming. **Original abstract and review record checked.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1474-4422(22)00169-7) · [FND-CIT-0333](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0333).
5. **Tolchin et al., *Neurology*, 2026 issue. AAN functional seizure guideline.** The guideline covers patients presenting with seizure-like events and people diagnosed with functional seizures. Recommendations 1 and 3 support history and semiology from patients and witnesses, video where available, appropriate evaluation of distinct typical events, and assessment of coexisting epilepsy. The guideline gives no validated cutoff for post-event fatigue or return to baseline. **Original executive-summary PDF checked.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214466) · [FND-CIT-0010](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0010).
6. **NICE NG206, 2021, and CDC clinical guidance, 2024. ME/CFS guidance.** NICE §§1.2–1.4 describe suspicion, assessment and diagnosis. §1.11 describes flexible energy management and advises against fixed increases in exercise for ME/CFS. CDC describes common worsening 12–48 hours after exertion, sometimes lasting days or weeks. Treat these timings as typical descriptions. Evaluate the full pattern and other explanations. These are recommendations and clinical descriptions for ME/CFS, with no estimate of PEM prevalence or pacing efficacy in FND. **NICE original guideline PDF checked, including §1.11.14, and CDC clinical page checked.** [NICE NG206](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206/chapter/recommendations) · [FND-CIT-0206](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0206) · [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/me-cfs/hcp/clinical-care/treating-the-most-disruptive-symptoms-first-and-preventing-worsening-of-symptoms.html).
7. **Gelauff et al., *J Neurol*, 2018. Direct FND fatigue data.** An analysis of data from 181 functional motor disorder participants in a trial compared questionnaire fatigue with 217 historical neuromuscular controls. Fatigue was associated with quality of life in the FND group. The analysis concerns symptom burden and associations, with a historical comparison and no direct measurement of delayed post-exertional exacerbation. **Original abstract checked.** No matching ID found for this DOI. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-018-8915-7) · [Abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29860667/).
8. **NICE NG56, 2016. Adult multimorbidity guidance.** Sections 1.5–1.6 support assessing disease burden, appointment and treatment demands, priorities and coordination, then agreeing a plan and reviewing it. Use this general care framework for the combined demands of FND, migraine and their care. The guidance supplies no comparison of FND treatment formats or prescribed interval. **Official guideline PDF checked.** No matching ID found. [NICE NG56](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng56) · [Recommendations PDF](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng56/resources/multimorbidity-clinical-assessment-andmanagement-pdf-1837516654789).
9. **Rutten et al., *J Neurol*, 2025. Direct FND qualitative evidence.** UK purposive sample of 22 people with FND, 18 carers and 21 clinicians. Interviews identified valued outcomes across symptoms, daily activities, wellbeing and independence. Participants valued patient report alongside clinical observations. Selection and qualitative design limit generalization. The study identifies outcome priorities and provides no treatment-effect or support-eligibility estimate. **Original article checked.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00415-025-12912-9) · [FND-CIT-0012](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0012).
10. **Ohlsson et al., *J Voice*, 2020. Randomized functional voice study.** Seventy-seven people with functional voice disorders were assigned to individual therapy, group therapy or no therapy (31, 31 and 15). The therapy groups improved more than the no-therapy group on total Voice Handicap Index at three months. The untreated group was not assessed at 12 months. The trial supports a voice-specific outcome, with no direct evidence here for visit spacing, remote equivalence, swallowing or other FND presentations. **Original abstract checked.** No matching ID found. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2018.08.023) · [Abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30344071/).
11. **Baker et al., *JNNP*, 2021. FND communication consensus.** The specialist recommendations cover functional voice, speech, language and related symptoms, with attention to symptom-specific assessment and communication practice. Use them to ground the role of speech-language therapy, while the trial above supplies measured voice outcomes. **Original consensus text checked.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2021-326767) · [FND-CIT-0025](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0025).
12. **WHO ICF Checklist, 2003. Functioning and environment framework.** The activity and participation section distinguishes actual performance in the current environment from capacity evaluated under specified conditions. Assistance, equipment and environmental modifications belong in the description of what makes performance possible. This is a classification tool, with no FND-specific test validation or criterion for a service decision. **Original WHO checklist, Activities and Participation, checked.** No matching ID found. [WHO checklist](https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/classification/icf/icfchecklist.pdf).
13. **Ramsay et al., *Eye*, 2024 and 2026. Specialist review and systematic review of functional visual loss.** The 2024 review describes assessment based on positive signs, ongoing consideration of coexisting eye and neurological disorders, and individually relevant management. The 2026 review found only two treatment cohorts with at least 10 patients and heterogeneous outcomes in children and adults. It provides limited grounds for attributing a functional gain to a particular treatment. **Original 2024 review abstract and 2026 review treatment section checked.** [2024 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41433-024-03126-w) · [FND-CIT-0024](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0024) · [2026 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41433-026-04648-1) · [FND-CIT-0103](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0103).

## Evidence gaps to retain when choosing cases

- The reviewed FND evidence gives no validated common measure for adding upright, sensory, emotional, cognitive and motor demands. Observation of how the task is performed, how much assistance it takes, what is missed and how the person feels afterward is the supported practical route. Proposed mechanisms or relative contributions stay hypotheses.
- The reviewed FND literature supplies no reliable cutoff for ordinary recovery versus PEM, and no validated progression schedule for the combined FND and possible ME/CFS scenarios. Use the patient's pattern and condition-specific guidance. Sanal-Hayes et al. included 17 studies in a 2023 ME/CFS scoping review. Pacing approaches and results varied, and the studies provide limited guidance for selecting a specific schedule. This is evidence from ME/CFS. Immediate and delayed symptoms can coexist. [Original scoping review](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-023-04587-5) · [FND-CIT-0066](../../../research/citation-index.md#fnd-cit-0066). Flexible changes can be reasonable even when an event or flare could not have been predicted.
- The reviewed intervention studies give no rule for an optimal balance of rehabilitation, recovery and daily life. Changes in a measured skill, ease of activity, safety, support dependence and what the person can do over a week are different outcomes. The team can choose a small set of outcomes with the person and agree what would change the plan.
- The 2024 functional visual loss review and 2022 orthostatic hypotension review were used for the points verified in their original abstracts. The original journal article bodies were not required for finer technique or diagnostic claims here. The voice trial detail likewise stays within the original abstract. New sources marked as lacking a matching ID are authoring candidates.
