## 3. How Module 11's editorial method was refined

The initial Module 11 handoff specified useful audience roles, but the working discussion refined both *how to teach* and *how much to explain*. The final approved texts are stronger exemplars than early drafts.

### A. Common pacing, clarity and story structure

- Start with a recognizable situation, specific decision, or uncertainty. **Show the discovery through what characters do**, not speeches explaining the whole curriculum.
- **Avoid immediately retelling in narration what dialogue and events have already demonstrated.** The reader should not hear the same lesson once from characters, once from a narrator, and once in the ending.
- The project lead repeatedly requested **faster pacing**: a roughly 15% tightening of Lesson 34 Supporter and roughly 25% of Lesson 35 Supporter materially improved their usefulness. This is editorial guidance, *not* a literal word-reduction target.
- Build paragraphs of connected thoughts; avoid runs of one-sentence paragraphs and unnecessary pauses, especially in the explanatory sections.
- Keep dialogue purposeful. Each exchange should change someone's understanding or decision. Do not let a minor communication detail displace a more important healthcare question.
- Use a small, separately displayed **block-quote definition** to refresh an earlier term when needed, rather than retelling an earlier neuroscience lesson.
- Recognize multiple valid outcomes: understanding, participation, access, confidence and a better question can be meaningful even when symptoms persist. **Do not equate education with recovery.**
- End with **one earned conclusion**. Avoid a long moral or an ending that restates each scene.
- Keep stories clinically credible: a specialist assessment does not automatically yield ongoing treatment; an interested generalist cannot automatically implement specialist protocols; agreeing to a referral does not endorse a proposed cause.
- The three audience sections should share continuity where appropriate but each contribute **a new viewpoint and discovery**. They must remain understandable independently and should not retell the same scene with the same conclusion.

### B. Person audience — what earned approval

The Person lessons (33–35) became substantial, thoughtful, human stories with a **meaningful decision or recognition** and clear afterward teaching. Callum's variable speech and changing ability to describe it, Nadia's migraine/referral mismatch, and Graham's expensive assessment with uncertain follow-up are exemplars. Their outcomes were imperfect, not reassuring fantasies.

- Reading accessibility: approximately **age 14**, warm conversational voice directed to “you,” without infantilizing adult readers. Slow enough to build meaning, **not slow because every point is explained repeatedly**.
- **Person section length: 1,500–1,900 words, favouring the lower end (~1,500–1,650) when the lesson remains complete.** This includes story, teaching, and the three end reflections. Treat this as a manuscript target rather than permission to thin out the lived scene; cut doubled narration, re-explanations of prior lessons, redundant dialogue and repeated morals first. Verify word count on every full revision and tell the project lead the count. Preserve sufficient depth and distinct new learning.
- Show different symptoms and circumstances across lessons; don't imply all readers have functional seizures, trauma, a daily supporter, travel money, or cognitive impairment.
- Introduce scientific ideas as **possible mechanisms or treatment rationales**, distinguishing observation from inference; do not tell readers their individual cause from a fictional narrative.
- Explain what research can and cannot demonstrate in ordinary language, placing specialized methods/limitations where they materially affect meaning instead of flooding the Person section with academic cautions.
- Approved Module 11 Person endings used **“Something to think about,” “How might that feel?” and “One small step.”** Keep their purposes distinct (cognitive recognition, emotional perspective, small safe optional action), avoid memory-heavy tasks and avoid implying any practice is required to obtain care. They are preferred starting patterns, not compulsory decorations.
- Important Lesson 33 realization: **shared knowledge is already a gain**, regardless of whether Callum's symptoms improve. Lesson 34 realization: **respect for a doctor and an unresolved care need can coexist**. Lesson 35: **an assessment is not treatment, and a referral's likely practical contribution matters**.

### C. Supporter audience — what earned approval

The project lead requested a **true supporter viewpoint**, not a Person story with added advice for families. The successful final sequence was:

- **Lesson 33:** Through his own eyes, Callum's father sees the effort behind walking while carrying plates; father and son revisit brief ideas about automaticity and competing demands **without either knowing the answer in advance**. Later, they choose to watch a film rather than analyze another familiar symptom. The initial draft was too long because it explained discoveries three times; user approved a shortened narrative and preserved the final father's-perspective reflection.
- **Lesson 34:** Nadia's occasional friend initially reacts more defensively than Nadia wants to a psychological referral. The friend begins talking over Nadia when she loses her thought. **Nadia herself raises a stop gesture**, looks back to the letter as a retrieval cue and completes her own more nuanced question. The point is **respecting Nadia's healthcare authority and clarifying her concern**, not teaching a whole module on cognitive pauses. The writer tightened dialogue/narration by ~15%; user edited the exact stopping scene in an interactive writing block; **commit the user-edited version, never the earlier assistant text**.
- **Lesson 35:** Lena is an occasional friend/driver. She may be able to help with **one** five-hour assessment trip but cannot promise repeated journeys or ongoing access to treatment. The shorter, approved version gets quickly to the real limit and ends clearly: **supporting access is not becoming responsible for providing inaccessible healthcare**. One long reasoning scene about calendar and money was cut by about a quarter.

**Reusable Supporter voice:** thoughtful **“advice over coffee,”** practical, observant, empathetic, very occasional gentle humour. Don't portray a supporter as inexhaustible, gullible, the clinical supervisor, or an opponent of doctors by default. Their concerns and limits are legitimate, while the person with FND retains voice, dignity, privacy and choice. Stories should not become mechanical “do/don't” lists or repeat already-demonstrated messages.

**Community voices:** The user wants relevant first-person **supporter quotes where they actually strengthen the lesson**. Check the speaker's role, original source, exact wording/context and prior reuse; no invented attribution or quote and no forced generic line. Earlier Module 11 use of a spouse's learning-intention quote was marked for source verification; one Lesson 35 version instead used an Australian self-selected caregiver survey as short context. **No quote or survey in the previous module is automatically reverified here.**

### D. Clinician audience — what earned approval

Early attempts were **too authoritative, too explanatory, or too reliant on sample clinical records**. The project lead preferred concise, focused clinical reasoning that develops a decision rather than supplying a write-up for a medical chart.

- Use a professional as the **point-of-view problem solver**. They have credible reasons for their first inclination; avoid portraying them as ignorant or malicious.
- The clinician weighs **what is known and desired; why evidence, uncertainty and competing interpretations matter; how to proceed proportionately**. These are thinking disciplines, not printed mandatory “What / Why / How” headings.
- Do **not** repeat a tour of diagnostic signs, voluntary movement terminology, neuroplasticity, coping or elementary mechanisms. Assume a qualified professional can recognize the fundamentals and move directly to the unusual decision.
- **Lesson 33:** Callum can discuss FND fluently in a quiet office but still has important real-world speech and mobility limitations. Knowledge, a one-time observed capacity and reliable daily functioning are different questions.
- **Lesson 34:** Dr. Chen's genuine efforts to read literature could bias a well-intentioned referral toward psychological explanation. Reading the actual study's population, outcome and historical terminology changes the decision; a migraine complaint remains separate. Don't infer deception from a positive functional sign. Earlier clinical-record examples were removed because they slowed the discovery.
- **Lesson 35:** Dr. Singh wants local rehabilitation based on specialist recommendations, while Graham's physiotherapist reasonably raises competence, adaptation, training and access concerns. Recommendations alone do not make a service deliverable.
- The Clinician prose must be **standalone, evidence-informed and collegial**, with a discernible decision and responsible follow-up; avoid technique recipes, a false universal clinical standard or extensive caveat paragraphs.
- A full clinical/source audit comes **after** project-lead human approval. Don't claim independent clinical review, original-source quote checks or site readiness prematurely.

## 4. Module 12's teaching responsibility — proposals, not decisions

**Existing canonical name:** *Psychological Treatment Without Blame*. Leave unchanged pending new-thread decision.

**Recommended unifying question:** “What could psychological assessment or treatment genuinely offer me, for which problem, with what evidence and conditions, and how could I know whether it is helping?”

This is **not** another module proving that FND is genuine, nor a sales case for psychotherapy, nor an argument that psychologists should be avoided. It is the practical **choice, rationale, evidence, fit and boundaries** of psychological care. That is a deeper and more useful next step after Module 11's clarification of a referral purpose.

### Proposed Lesson 36 — understanding the purpose of psychological care

**Working concept:** “What is this assessment trying to accomplish?” (Existing page title is *Why Psychological Treatment Does Not Mean Imaginary*; a change requires the project lead's decision and later navigation reconciliation.)

**Tentative narrative:** Return to **Nadia**, as Module 11 promised. She meets a psychologist after Dr. Chen's referral. Differentiate what this clinician is asking or assessing, a potential psychological treatment target, a hypothesis about contributing factors and any claim about original FND causation. Show an intellectually credible, thoughtful psychologist, not an automatic villain. Consider what useful psychological care could exist while **worsening migraine remains a separate medical matter**. Do not decide in advance whether Nadia accepts treatment or what conclusions the psychologist reaches.

**Distinct learning by audience:**
- **Person:** Can ask which problem is being assessed or treated without being expected to accept a hidden cause or reject therapy outright.
- **Supporter:** Could learn what useful support/privacy looks like when someone receives a psychologically framed referral, without seeking confidential session details or pressuring the patient.
- **Clinician:** Psychologist reasons from referral information and the patient's stated goal to an appropriately bounded assessment and provisional formulation, with explicit distinction between an inferred contributor, established psychiatric condition, FND diagnosis and therapy target. Maintain relevant medical care.

**Possible turning point:** Nadia or the psychologist notices that the referral's description and Nadia's immediate priorities don't align; their discussion creates a more useful shared clinical question rather than a forced causal conclusion. **Do not lock this in**; invite the lead's own direction.

### Proposed Lesson 37 — choosing therapy and recognizing meaningful change

**Working concept:** “What might this particular therapy change, and how would we know?” Existing title: *Choose a Therapy and Set Goals*.

**Tentative narrative:** Consider another person with a different FND presentation, perhaps functional seizures, exploring or beginning a psychological approach with a **specific goal that matters to them**, not a generic “learn to cope.” Allow a realistic improvement, no improvement, or mixed result without implying therapy has proved the cause. Therapy may address symptom-related processes, fear/avoidance, distress, participation or a separate anxiety/PTSD/depression condition; these are **different goals and indications**.

**Distinct learning by audience:**
- **Person:** Learn why fit includes target, therapist, approach, goals, access, review, and the difference between symptom frequency and quality-of-life participation.
- **Supporter:** Recognize and support the person's goals without auditing sessions, deciding efficacy by one bad day, or demanding a cure.
- **Clinician:** Deliberate over a real treatment selection, evidence population and measured outcome, comorbidity and patient preference; distinguish “therapy helped X” from “X caused FND.”

**Scope:** May briefly explain CBT-informed, ACT, mindfulness, psychodynamic or trauma-focused concepts **in context**. Do not use a long therapy acronym catalogue instead of a teaching story. The systematic review and CODES trial require careful original-source checks and correct endpoint/population framing.

### Proposed Lesson 38 — consent, trauma and treatment fit/harm

**Working concept:** “What if this approach isn't right for me, or something needs to change?” Existing title: *Consent, Trauma, and Recognizing Harm*.

**Tentative narrative:** A credible therapy situation where purpose, boundaries, accessibility, patient comfort or interpretation becomes unclear. A **reasonable but poorly matched initial approach** might provide a subtler decision than another cartoonishly harmful psychologist. An unmistakably harmful conduct example can be addressed where necessary but should not dominate the full module.

**Distinct learning by audience:**
- **Person:** Consent and boundaries are ongoing. They can seek explanation, adjust, pause, decline or change therapy without having to prove trauma or reject all care.
- **Supporter:** Respect privacy and choice, notice a person's stated safety concerns, protect their boundaries, and obtain separate help for their own needs where appropriate.
- **Clinician:** Revisit formulation and treatment fit if distress worsens, consent is uncertain, or the patient dissents. Recognize trauma as potentially relevant for some people and separate coexisting PTSD treatment from any assertion that trauma caused FND. Understand safeguarding and medical follow-up within scope.

**Important open question:** This third lesson potentially encompasses too much—consent, trauma, therapist fit, adverse effects, and coercion. Ask the project lead what should be the central **single story/discovery**, and what belongs in short context or the reference library.

## 6. Evidence, research and quotation handling

**Known current Module 12 source leads (from existing course drafts; verify original claims during evidence audit):**
- **FND-CIT-0077** — Gutkin et al. 2021, systematic review of psychotherapy in adults with FND. Literature is heterogeneous; do not present all psychotherapies as having equivalent evidence.
- **FND-CIT-0078** — British Psychological Society 2024 briefing, psychological and neuropsychological management of children and adults with FND. Interpret it as guidance/briefing, not trial data proving a particular mechanism.
- **FND-CIT-0079** — Ludwig et al. 2018, stressful life events and maltreatment in FND, a case-control systematic review/meta-analysis. Group associations **cannot determine one individual's cause**; never infer that trauma is necessary or hidden.
- **FND-CIT-0033** — Goldstein et al. 2020 **CODES trial** for adults with dissociative/functional seizures. The **primary 12-month seizure-frequency comparison was not statistically significant**; some secondary outcomes favoured seizure-specific CBT with standardized medical care. Explain both, accurately, without equating secondary benefits to proof of causation or universal effectiveness.
- Relevant expert guidance on individualized care from Module 11 may inform case framing, but is not a substitute for therapy-specific outcomes.

**Clinically material distinctions:**
- **Psychological assessment** and **psychotherapy** are not interchangeable.
- FND diagnosis, possible psychosocial factors, psychiatric comorbidity, a proposed cause, and chosen psychotherapy targets are different clinical propositions.
- PTSD, depression, anxiety and other mental-health conditions can warrant care **whether or not they caused FND**.
- Psychotherapy response does **not** validate a proposed cause, nor is failure to improve evidence of resistant or intentionally produced symptoms.
- A decline/pause in nonurgent therapy does not mean declining FND diagnosis or all healthcare. Keep medical problems, especially migraine, independently considered.
- Investigate treatment fit and harm seriously; do not make every difficult emotion during therapy proof of harm, or every negative reaction proof that “the work is working.”
- High-stakes language about recovered memories, symptom validity, functional signs, intentionality, coercion and retraumatization needs disciplined sourcing and cautious phrasing.
- Describe a credible professional's reasoning with empathy and the bounds of the actual evidence; **do not use private WorkSafeBC opinions or named clinicians as research validation or as public case material**.

**Quoted voices:**
- User values authentic public lived-experience/community quotes and relevant supporter voices, but only if exact text, speaker identity/role (without unnecessary personal identification), context, primary permalink and prior reuse have been verified.
- The current Module 12 drafts contain **quotation options** marked for review. None should be assumed verified or approved because they appear in a generated draft.
- Public stories of therapy benefiting, not helping, or harming someone are **individual experiences**, not a measured intervention success rate. Make space for genuine benefit and genuine negative experiences without false balance or turning a quote into medical authority.
- Reuse existing stable citation IDs; reconcile full page-local bibliography, Figure mappings, and central index after prose approval.

### De-identified discoveries for the teaching

- **Separate observation from inference.** A witnessed symptom, positive diagnostic feature, test result, psychiatric history, possible contributing factor, hypothesis about an original cause and an actual treatment goal are different statements. Older historical interpretations sometimes move too quickly between them; a plausible factor is not proof of causation.
- **Avoid misusing symptom-validity language.** Concern about response patterns on an instrument, limitations of a measure, and deliberate fabrication are not interchangeable. Do not use results from a fictional examination to label the person as deceptive; describe findings and uncertainty within the actual purpose and limitations of the test. Historical terminology and ideas must be identified as historical, not endorsed.
- **Separate conditions and care needs.** A psychological assessment might address a useful target while migraine or another medical concern still requires its own follow-through. Engaging with psychological care does not concede an unproven psychological origin for FND; declining a poorly matched therapy does not amount to rejecting the diagnosis.
- **Performance is not continuous capacity.** A person may speak clearly or complete difficult cognitive work during a supported assessment, yet struggle with speech, seizures, sensory load, memory, endurance or repeated tasks elsewhere. Equally, do not discard valid assessment findings simply because function varies. Ask what the measure established and what it did not.
- **Assessments and ongoing treatment are not the same.** Referrals, formulation reports, short runs of treatment, matching a therapist and obtaining sustained, appropriate care are different steps. A treatment can stop for reasons outside the person's effort or willingness. An unsuccessful or interrupted intervention cannot by itself prove a lack of motivation.
- **Communication changes the evidence available.** In periods of severe symptoms, the person may need extra time, accessible surroundings, written prompts, or a supporter to provide details **by consent**. Another person doing more talking should not automatically be interpreted as the patient lacking insight, credibility or a meaningful preference. Disagreement with a clinician is not itself proof of psychopathology.
- **Treatment can cause hope, fear and loss of trust simultaneously.** Returning to psychological support after a dismissive encounter can require courage; being offered a generic approach when the person seeks help for a specific problem can deepen uncertainty. Do not portray every psychologist as harmful or every difficult session as either a success or an injury.
- **Give the supporter a complete human reality.** Loved ones may be frightened, mentally and physically depleted, responsible for a considerable amount of invisible practical care, and nonetheless loving, funny and respectful of autonomy. They should neither disappear from the story nor become inexhaustible unpaid clinicians. Do not suggest intimate support is required for a person to receive dignified care.
- **Preserve pleasure and agency.** Even in sustained disability, meaningful participation, small moments of humour, a clearer question and mutually understood boundaries matter. Do not manufacture a recovery ending or reduce the patient to symptoms.
