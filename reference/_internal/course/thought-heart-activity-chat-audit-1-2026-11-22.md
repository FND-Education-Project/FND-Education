## Scope and review principles

Reviewed the [authoring standard](thought-heart-activity-authoring-standard.md), [59-lesson inventory](thought-heart-activity-inventory.md), [earlier verification](thought-heart-activity-harmonization-verification-2026-11-19.md), PR diff and affected Person lesson contexts, emphasizing the lesson-specific learning objective, Thought–Heart–Activity progression, age-14 readability, cognitive demands, inclusion, consent, safety and meaningful progression. The reviewed changes affect Lessons **1–29 and 33–35** (32 lessons). Module 10 Lessons **30–32** remain intentionally outside harmonization; Module 12 Lessons **36–38** keep their previously approved reflection wording.

The existing harmonization is a substantial improvement: it consistently offers cognitive understanding, a personally meaningful response, and optional small action; it generally avoids journaling prerequisites, symptom provocation, psychological blame, recovery promises and assumptions about clinicians or daily supporters. The principal remaining editorial issue is that a run of activities in Lessons 15–22 converges on essentially the same environmental adjustment despite teaching different mechanisms. **This is a targeted refinement, not a request to rewrite all 105 prompts.** The original November 19 verification remains a record of its own review, not a replacement for this independent pass.

## Required editorial corrections proposed for approval

| Lesson and location | Finding | Minimum proposed correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [5: Positive Signs](../../../course/module-02-how-fnd-is-diagnosed/01-positive-signs-and-the-neurological-examination.md), Thought / Activity | “If your clinician showed you a positive sign … what did they observe?” cannot readily be answered by a reader whose clinician never demonstrated or explained one. The Activity also assumes a specific clinical explanation is available. | Give a clear alternative to use a positive-sign example **from the lesson**. Retain clinician-only interpretation and the warning against self-testing. Do not imply the example diagnoses the reader. |
| [18: Predictive Brain](../../../course/module-07-prediction-interoception-and-agency/01-the-predictive-brain.md), Thought | Asking “What did you expect?” alongside personally experienced FND symptoms risks confusing a **conscious expectation** with rapid, automatic predictive processing. The lesson explicitly distinguishes these, especially in its functional-seizure example. This ambiguity existed before the PR and was retained. | Anchor Thought in the **missing-step** example: ask what the nervous system prepared for, what happened, and how that differs from consciously worrying about an event. Leave prediction as an investigated mechanism, not an individual diagnosis or symptom cause. |
| [20: Salience, Arousal and Feedback](../../../course/module-07-prediction-interoception-and-agency/03-salience-arousal-and-feedback-loops.md), Thought / Activity | Current prompts mainly ask which café demand is distracting and whether a quieter location would help. These are close to Lessons 15, 17 and 19, and do not apply the lesson’s distinctive **feedback loop**, despite its strong worked café example. | Ask readers to notice how greater effort, tiredness and difficulty hearing can affect one another **in the fictional café story**; optionally identify one low-demand way to interrupt that loop. No personal symptom monitoring or deliberate overload. |
| [21: Cognitive Difficulties](../../../course/module-07-prediction-interoception-and-agency/04-cognitive-difficulties-in-fnd.md), Thought | The binary “Was the information lost while they were using it, or was it hard to bring back later?” may imply that a reader can reliably identify a cognitive mechanism from a single example. The lesson teaches distinctions **and assessment uncertainty**. This simplification largely predates the PR. | Ask which different abilities *might* be involved and why the exact explanation cannot be concluded from one lapse; readers may answer from the story rather than their own memory. Preserve the existing low-demand place-marker Activity. |

These are editorial clarity and accessibility findings. The scientific validity of any detailed mechanisms is **for Work verification**, not an established error determined by this audit.

## Worthwhile improvements — optional, narrow changes

| Lesson | Rationale and narrow direction |
| --- | --- |
| [4: Available Capacity](../../../course/module-01-what-fnd-is/04-available-capacity-spoons-and-early-action.md), Activity | Three fill-in statements may feel like a mandatory mini-plan for a reader with limited cognitive capacity. Permit **just one** of the statements, without removing safety limits or the option of having no warning signs. |
| [19: Three Sensory Information Sources](../../../course/module-07-prediction-interoception-and-agency/02-interoception-proprioception-and-outside-senses.md), Activity | The lesson teaches how to **describe sensory information more precisely**, but the Activity returns to quieter/brighter/safer surroundings, overlapping neighboring lessons. Consider practising **one clear, cause-neutral description** from a story or personal example; avoid requiring a sensory symptom. |
| [22: Emotional Regulation](../../../course/module-07-prediction-interoception-and-agency/05-emotional-regulation-and-involuntary-symptoms.md), Activity | Another “choose an environmental or communication arrangement” blurs this lesson with the attention/senses exercises. Consider a small fictional example that separates **having a feeling** from **choosing a response**, without suggesting a choice controls involuntary FND symptoms. Keep the existing sympathy for legitimate anger, difficulty and mixed emotions. |
| [25: When the Familiar Explanation Does Not Fit](../../../course/module-08-recognizing-symptoms-and-finding-information/02-similar-experiences-different-features.md), Activity | “What does this result tell us, and what does it leave unanswered?” closely repeats the Lesson 6 test-result question. Consider using **Natalie’s specific new finding/changed symptom** to practise asking what evidence or next assessment would clarify the *new* concern, instead of a general test-result script. Do not invite self-diagnosis. |

Reviewers should not revise every “environmental adjustment” Activity. Some deliberately practise the lesson’s principle and are useful spaced rehearsal. Revisions should make each adjacent lesson teach a visibly distinct cognitive or participation skill.

- All previously approved Module 12 Lessons 36–38 prompts, especially consent and the safe, non-exposure CBT-informed reflection in Lesson 38; no repackaging as a therapy protocol.

- Lesson 2’s project-lead-authored first-person note that journaling can be impossible, and Lesson 11’s compassionate one-page-map ending; if placement is improved, keep their meaning and authorship.
- Lesson 8’s measured finding vs hypothesis distinction; Lesson 13–14 individualized safety/supporter accommodations; Lesson 16–17 usual aids and physical-safety limits; Lesson 23 adaptations-as-progress; Lesson 24 symptom vs participation outcomes; Lesson 26 limited Reference Library exploration; Lesson 28 no-rehabilitation alternative; Lesson 29 treatment evidence appraisal; Lesson 33 gains without symptom recovery; Lesson 34 referral vs other medical concern.
- The full narrative, quotation, citations, supporter and clinician sections, figures and approved illustrations, absent a specific reason requiring change.
