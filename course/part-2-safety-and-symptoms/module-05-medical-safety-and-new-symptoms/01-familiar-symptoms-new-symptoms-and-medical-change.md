# Familiar Symptoms, New Symptoms, and Medical Change

> **Working draft:** This page was automatically generated and is looking for [contributors and reviewers](https://fnd-education-project.github.io/FND-Education/).

FND can explain real and disabling symptoms. It does not protect you from another illness, injury or medical emergency. The safest question is often not “Is this FND?” but “Is this my usual, medically assessed pattern?” (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))

## For the Person With FND
{: #for-the-person-with-fnd .shareable }

### When something feels different

You're making lunch when your hand begins to shake.

"Oh, this again."

You've experienced this before. Usually, the shaking settles after a while, and you know what helps you get through it.

But today, it's lasting much longer.

Hmm. That's different.

Is this still your usual FND? Or is something else going on?

That's one of the tricky things about FND. Your symptoms might change quite a bit, even when nothing new is wrong.

So how do you know when a change is worth checking out?

You don't have to figure out what's causing it. That's a job for your healthcare team.

But knowing what makes an experience different can help you explain what's happening.

### Familiar, changed, or new?

There are three terms worth knowing. They're useful when you're talking with a doctor.

A **familiar symptom** is something you've experienced before that fits a pattern your clinician has already assessed.

Maybe it comes and goes. Maybe some days are worse than others. It can still be familiar.

A **changed symptom** is one you recognise, but something important about it is different.

Remember that shaking hand? Perhaps it usually settles within a few minutes, but today it's lasted much longer.

A **new symptom** is something that hasn't been part of your previously assessed pattern.

It might turn out to be related to FND. It might have another explanation.

That's what an assessment can help work out.

![Three cards compare a familiar symptom with a changed symptom and a new symptom.](../../../assets/illustrations/module-5-page-1-familiar-changed-new.svg)

*Illustration: Familiar, changed and new symptoms. These terms can help you explain what's happening.*

### If you remember one thing

**A familiar symptom can have a familiar response. Something new or meaningfully different may need a fresh medical look.**

### So, what's different this time?

Let's go back to your shaking hand.

You've had shaking before, but this time something has caught your attention.

Perhaps you're thinking, "I know this symptom, but it doesn't usually happen quite like this."

That's useful information.

When you're talking with a clinician, a few details can help:

- **The beginning:** Did it start the way it usually does?
- **The symptoms:** Is it affecting the same parts of your body?
- **The length:** Is it lasting about as long as usual?
- **The recovery:** Are you recovering in your usual way?
- **Anything else:** Have you been ill, injured, or started or changed a medication?

You might remember only one or two of those things.

That's still useful.

And if memory is giving you trouble, perhaps your symptom map has something that helps. Someone who was there might remember a detail, too.

The idea is to describe what stood out, rather than spend your day watching for every little change.

A symptom behaving differently doesn't automatically mean something dangerous is happening.

It gives you something worth considering with your care team.

### When everything gets blamed on FND

Here's something that can be especially frustrating.

You develop a new problem, visit a doctor, and the conversation quickly turns to your FND.

But you're sitting there thinking, "Hang on. Could we look at this new problem?"

There's actually a medical term for what can sometimes happen.

It's called **diagnostic overshadowing**.

That's when an existing diagnosis gets so much attention that another possible health problem is overlooked.

One person in an online FND community described developing light sensitivity after being diagnosed with FND.

They eventually saw an eye doctor, who identified a pollen allergy and prescribed treatment.

The person wrote:

> "He gave me steroid droplets ... and since then my light sensitivity is gone."

*— A comment from a now-deleted account in an [FND community discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1ievray/sharing_my_success_story/).*

For this person, getting their eyes checked led to treatment that helped.

Someone else's light sensitivity could have a completely different explanation.

That's the point, really.

**You can have FND and another medical problem at the same time.** We covered that in earlier lessons, but it's especially important when something changes. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

### But more medical treatment isn't always better, either

Now let's look at the other side.

Imagine someone who has familiar functional seizures.

Their episodes have been assessed. Their healthcare team knows what usually happens, and they've worked out what kind of help is useful.

Would it make sense to put that person through the same emergency tests and treatments every single time?

Sometimes an episode really does need emergency attention.

But when it's a familiar, previously assessed event, an agreed response may be safer and more helpful than repeating treatments that aren't needed.

Medical procedures and medications can have risks of their own. And repeated emergency visits can be exhausting.

This is where an **individual safety plan** comes in.

It's guidance worked out with your clinicians about what helps during familiar symptoms, how to keep you safe, and when it's time to get more medical help. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3), [4](#citation-4))

You may already have one.

If you haven't, that's something we'll work toward in the next lesson.

### Wait. Didn't we just make a symptom map?

We did!

And the good news is that the work you put into it can help here, too.

Think of it this way:

**Your symptom map describes what's going on with your health.**

**Your safety plan explains what to do when help is needed.**

The map can hold a short safety summary or point to the plan.

You certainly don't need two enormous documents. There's enough paperwork in life already!

In Lesson 13, we'll look at turning the useful information you've gathered into a safety plan with your care team.

Then, in Lesson 14, we'll see how to use it.

### When is it time to get more help?

Sometimes a change can be discussed with your regular healthcare team.

Other times, getting help quickly is important.

For example, sudden symptoms that could be a stroke, such as new one-sided weakness, facial drooping or difficulty speaking, need emergency assessment. Severe breathing problems, serious injuries or other immediate dangers also call for emergency help.

**When something could be a medical emergency, seek emergency care right away.**

For changes that aren't emergencies, your healthcare team can help decide what needs checking.

Maybe it's a conversation and an examination. Maybe some tests would be useful.

You can start with something as simple as:

"I've had this symptom before, but this time something feels different. Could we check it out?"

You don't have to walk in with a theory about what's wrong.

A good description of what's happening is a great place to start.

### Something to think about

Think of a symptom or episode you've experienced before.

What makes it familiar to you?

Now imagine something important about it changes.

Would you know who to ask for medical advice?

If you're unsure, that's a useful question to bring to your next appointment.

### One small thing you can do

Remember your symptom map?

If you'd like, choose one familiar, medically assessed symptom and add a short description of what usually happens.

For example:

"My usual episodes involve shaking and difficulty speaking. Afterwards, I generally need some quiet time to recover."

Already have something like that written down? Great. You can leave it as it is.

You could also ask your clinician which changes would mean getting more medical help.

And if writing or remembering feels like too much today, this activity can wait.

**The idea is to make getting care easier, not give yourself another chore.**

Next, we'll use what you've learned to help prepare your individual safety plan.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters
{: #for-family-friends-and-other-supporters .shareable }

### When what you're seeing doesn't quite add up

Imagine someone you care about is struggling to move their arm.

They reach for a cup, but their hand barely moves.

A little later, they're laughing at something on television and reach for their phone without much trouble.

Hang on. How does that work?

It's the sort of thing that can leave a supporter scratching their head.

Perhaps you've seen something similar with walking, speaking, shaking, memory or another symptom.

And sometimes the changes seem to happen when someone mentions the symptom or draws attention to it.

It's understandable to have questions. You might even wonder whether the person has some control over what's happening.

That's where everything you've learned about FND so far becomes useful.

### Remember what we've learned about FND?

Earlier lessons introduced the roles of attention, prediction and the way the brain processes information.

You also learned that symptoms can change depending on the situation.

For some people with FND, movement or another ability may become easier when their attention shifts. A symptom might also change in response to a suggestion.

That can look pretty strange from the outside.

But here's the important part.

**A symptom changing with attention or suggestion doesn't mean the person is choosing to produce it.**

These changes can be part of how real, involuntary functional symptoms behave.

Clinicians may even use certain changes during examination as *positive diagnostic signs*—findings that help them recognise FND.

Of course, the way a symptom behaves in one moment isn't enough for a supporter to diagnose it.

And this is where you can give someone something especially valuable.

**You can believe what they're experiencing, even when you don't fully understand how it's happening.**

You might still have questions. That's okay.

Being curious and being supportive can go together.

### Those little details you've been noticing

Over the previous lessons, we've introduced several terms that help make sense of symptoms.

Let's bring a few of them together.

Imagine someone with FND is having a particularly difficult afternoon.

They've been in a noisy environment for a while. Then they notice a familiar strange feeling. Shortly afterwards, one of their usual episodes begins.

When it's over, they're exhausted.

There's quite a bit of information in that little story.

The noise might have been a **trigger**—something that contributed to making the episode more likely.

That strange feeling might have been a **warning sign**, something the person noticed as the episode was approaching or beginning.

And the exhaustion afterwards? That could be a **consequence** of the episode, although other things may contribute to it too.

Notice the word *might*.

Seeing two things happen close together gives you a clue. It doesn't necessarily tell you what caused what.

Some people have episodes without an obvious trigger or warning.

Others experience **persistent symptoms** that stay with them for much of the day, perhaps becoming better or worse at different times.

An **episode**, by comparison, has a recognisable period of symptoms, although recovery may take longer.

Why are these distinctions useful?

Because describing *what happened* is often more helpful to a clinician than trying to explain *why it happened*.

And that takes some pressure off you.

You can be a helpful observer without having to become a neurologist.

### What if you remember it differently?

Here's another situation that can get complicated.

Perhaps you remember an episode happening on Tuesday.

The person you're supporting is certain it happened weeks earlier.

Or you remember a long period when they couldn't speak, while they recall only a few moments.

It can feel strange when two people who were there remember the same event differently.

Earlier in the course, we talked about **functional cognitive disorder (FCD)** and the way thinking and memory can become unreliable.

Someone may genuinely remember an event differently from the way you do.

And, of course, supporters can forget details as well. Our memory isn't exactly a perfect filing cabinet.

Sometimes two people are also noticing different parts of an experience.

One person may remember the fear or confusion they felt during an episode. The other may remember how long it lasted or what was happening around them.

Both perspectives can provide useful information.

When memory differences become frustrating, a shared note can sometimes help.

For instance, you might agree to record an important medical event together while the details are still reasonably fresh.

The symptom map from Lesson 11 can hold the information that matters.

**The aim is to build a clearer shared picture, rather than decide whose memory wins.**

And when someone finds it difficult to remember their own experiences, being listened to with patience can make an enormous difference.

### What if something really has changed?

Remember the difference between familiar, changed and new symptoms from the Person section of this lesson?

Supporters can sometimes notice changes that the person experiencing the symptoms hasn't recognised.

Perhaps an episode begins differently.

Maybe recovery is taking much longer than the pattern their clinician has assessed.

Or perhaps there's been an injury or an entirely unfamiliar symptom.

These observations may help the healthcare team decide what needs attention.

A known FND diagnosis can explain familiar symptoms while another medical problem may still need assessment.

And some situations, such as serious injury, severe breathing difficulty or sudden signs of a possible stroke, require emergency care.

**The important thing is recognising when the situation deserves medical attention, rather than feeling responsible for deciding the diagnosis yourself.**

You can help describe what's different and seek appropriate assistance when needed.

### Listening is part of keeping someone safe

Something interesting happens when supporters become better at observing.

They may start recognising patterns the person with FND hasn't noticed.

That can be helpful.

But the person experiencing the symptoms also has information that nobody else can see.

Perhaps a particular sensation tells them an episode may be approaching.

Maybe they know that certain kinds of help make things easier, while others make recovery more difficult.

Or perhaps they need a different kind of support than the one you've been offering.

That's why being receptive matters.

You bring what you've noticed.

They bring what they've experienced.

And together, you may discover something neither of you had understood quite as clearly before.

Of course, conversations about symptoms can sometimes become tiring, especially when both people have already had a difficult day.

There's room to choose when a conversation would be useful and when it's better to leave it for another time.

**Good observation helps you understand someone. Good listening helps you use that understanding in a way that respects them.**

### Something to think about

Has there been a time when a symptom surprised you because it looked different from what you expected?

How might what you've learned about FND change the way you understand that experience?

And is there something the person you support has told you about their symptoms that you'd like to understand better?

### One small thing you can try

The next time the person you're supporting describes a symptom, try giving their explanation a little room.

Perhaps there's something they'd like you to notice, or a detail that could help their healthcare team.

If you both think it's useful, that information can find a place on the symptom map you learned about in Lesson 11.

There's no need to turn everyday life into a constant observation exercise.

**The goal is to understand one another a little better, so that when help is needed, it fits the person and the situation.**

You've spent quite a few lessons learning about FND and the different ways it can affect someone's life.

Now we're going to put that understanding to work.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team
{: #for-clinicians-and-the-care-team .shareable }

### Research quotations for review

**Option 1 — Finkelstein et al., 2021**

> “the presence of positive clinical signs of FND does not exclude the presence of a concomitant neurological condition.”

**Option 2 — Mcloughlin et al., 2025**

> “A balanced approach is needed not to over or under-investigate new symptoms on their own merits.”

*Figure 1 — Research quotations offered for editorial selection. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))*

### Reassess the presentation, not the person’s credibility

Start with physiological stability and time-sensitive differentials. Then compare the current semiology with documented event types. Separate: confirmed diagnoses; the usual presentation; current differences; objective observations; and unresolved risk.

Positive FND signs can support a diagnosis, but they do not rule out comorbidity. Conversely, a stereotyped event with an established plan may not benefit from repeated low-yield investigation or harmful acute treatment. Explain the reasoning for either investigation or non-investigation. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3), [4](#citation-4))

When uncertainty remains, name it. Arrange a route for follow-up and revise the safety plan after a new diagnosis, medication change, injury or material change in events.
