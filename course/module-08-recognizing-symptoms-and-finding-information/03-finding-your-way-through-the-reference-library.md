## For the Person With FND

### The library is not a diagnostic quiz

Imagine asking a librarian for “the book about shaking.” They would probably ask a few questions before choosing a shelf.

Our Reference Library works much the same way. You can start with your own words and explore more than one relevant page.

The goal isn't to keep clicking until a diagnosis appears. The goal is to **understand possible descriptions and arrive at better questions**.

### Three places to begin

The [Diagnostic Signs Library](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/README.md) describes how clinicians evaluate particular functional presentations, including other explanations that may need consideration. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

The [Recovery Techniques Library](../../reference/recovery-techniques/README.md) describes possible care and rehabilitation approaches, their limits and relevant safety information.

The [Co-occurring Conditions Library](../../reference/co-occurring-conditions/README.md) describes conditions that may accompany FND or share some symptoms with it.

Each symptom page has separate reading paths for the **Person**, **Supporters**, and **Clinicians**. You can read the part that helps you. The technical section is there when you need it, not as a test you must pass.

### Look for what the page does—and doesn't—cover

Suppose you have episodes where your legs shake and you lose your balance. You might explore pages about tremor, gait problems or falls. You may also need assessment for a different medical condition.

First read **Refers to**: what sorts of experiences belong on the page? Then look for a **scope boundary**: what does the page leave for another topic?

A recovery method being described isn't proof it works. Someone's story can show what mattered to them without predicting your outcome.

### When several pages fit

Sometimes a few descriptions sound familiar. That's not a failure to pick correctly.

You might ask your care team, “I noticed these experiences overlap. Which features are important in my assessment, and what is still uncertain?”

**Something to think about:** Would you rather begin from a symptom word, an everyday description or a problem in daily life?

**One small step:** Choose one previously assessed symptom and visit one relevant page. Read the opening and scope boundary. You can stop there. One useful question is worth more than an exhausting afternoon of searching.

Next comes rehabilitation. We'll explore how learning and practice may help, without assuming every symptom needs the same approach.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### The best help isn't always finding the answer

You search for a symptom and discover five pages that sound almost right. You may want to pick one and say, “This is what's going on.”

But the Reference Library is designed to **teach and organize questions**, not to diagnose by resemblance. Reading it together can help the person prepare for a more useful appointment.

### Read the page the person wants to read

Ask first whether they'd like company, help reading, a written summary or to explore alone. The **Person** section is usually the most accessible starting point. You may find useful practical material in the **Supporter** section, and the **Clinician** section can help the care team evaluate assessment or treatment—not replace that team. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

Look at **Refers to** and the scope boundaries. If two different pages seem relevant, keep both as possibilities. Help form one question, such as “Which symptom presentation has been diagnosed, and which techniques fit that finding?”

Never use a recovery-technique page to coach unsupervised risky movement, breathing, swallowing or seizure interruption.

### Decide when the reading should stop

A few helpful pages may be enough. Repeated searching can exhaust both of you without resolving uncertainty. If you find conflicting information or an unclear diagnosis, bring the question to the appropriate clinician instead of searching until one explanation wins.

An **OT or PT** can help adapt an activity or select symptom-specific rehabilitation; an **SLT** can guide communication/swallowing work; **neurology/primary care** can clarify diagnosis and other medical questions; **psychology** can support adjustment or appropriate treatment where chosen.

> “I will check when we meet her OT next week when/if neuro physio is planned. If not I’ll request it.”

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Translate a positive diagnosis into a usable referral and technique choice

An FND label without a clearly identified presentation does little to guide rehabilitation. After explaining the observed evidence, define the care question: which symptom, what task is affected, which coexisting conditions matter, what can be tried safely, what needs specialist input, and what outcome the person values?

**Step 1 — Diagnostic fit.** Start with the [Diagnostic Signs overview](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/README.md). Review “Refers to,” scope boundaries, positive signs and appropriate differentials. Some pages describe descriptive observations or investigation for alternative disease, not independent diagnostic tests. Do not simply count positive-sign entries.

**Step 2 — Technique fit.** Choose an entry from the corresponding [Recovery Techniques index](../../reference/recovery-techniques/technique-index.md), noting its evidence label (trial, guideline, consensus, emerging, or lived-experience report as indicated), prerequisites and contraindications. A technique demonstrated for functional tremor should not silently become the default treatment for seizures, paralysis, swallowing or functional cognition. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

**Step 3 — Profession and care setting.** PT may lead movement or gait retraining when appropriate; OT may lead task and accessibility adaptation; SLT may lead speech, voice, swallowing or upper-airway intervention; psychology may offer evidence-informed seizure-focused or other agreed treatment, where indicated. Neurology, primary care, emergency care, vestibular/eye specialists and others address diagnosis, comorbidities and risk. These are examples of roles, not rigid ownership or substitutes for the patient's preferences.

**Step 4 — Measure what matters.** Document baseline function, safety, symptom burden, confidence, access and quality of life. Describe which clinician is responsible for review and when changed symptoms require reassessment. A response to any chosen technique does not prove or disprove the initial diagnosis.

### Worked example — from diagnosis to rehabilitation discussion

A person with an assessed functional tremor wants to use kitchen utensils. The team could review the tremor diagnostic overview, then discuss a task-oriented or external-focus recovery page with PT/OT. They might change grip or environment, agree on a manageable goal and review actual utensil use and post-task effects. If the person instead describes repeated falls with altered awareness, the team should **change reference pathways and safety assessment**, not recycle tremor retraining.

### Accessible shared decision-making

Offer one or two selected links and their reasons, rather than a catalogue of dozens. For patients with memory difficulties, provide a brief written summary, including what is confirmed, what is uncertain and who will act next. For supporters, obtain consent before sharing. Recognize when the reference contains consensus rather than comparative efficacy evidence, or when a technique depends on equipment, trained personnel or services that the person cannot access.

**Evidence boundary:** The Reference Library is a clinically oriented working collection, not a validated point-of-care decision support system. Its human and specialist reviews remain ongoing. Positive diagnosis and safe ongoing assessment are supported by the [1](#citation-1) and [2](#citation-2); intervention selection needs its own supporting studies or consensus.
