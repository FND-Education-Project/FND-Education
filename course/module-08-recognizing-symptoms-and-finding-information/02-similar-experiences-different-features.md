## For the Person With FND

### “Dizzy” can mean several things

Imagine three people telling a doctor they feel dizzy.

One says the room seems to spin. Another feels close to fainting after standing. The third feels unsteady among bright shelves in a supermarket.

They have all used an honest word. But the experiences are not identical, and the appropriate medical questions may be different.

That's the skill we'll practise: **notice differences in symptoms without choosing their cause**.

### Compare what happens, not what you think it is

A shaking hand might move rhythmically, suddenly jerk or make repeated brief movements. People use words such as *tremor*, *jerk* and *tic* to describe different patterns. These words are useful starting points; the full history and examination still matter.

“I couldn't move” can also mean several things. Perhaps a leg had almost no strength. Perhaps it felt fixed in one posture. Maybe a movement wouldn't begin, or the inability to move happened during an episode of altered awareness.

Or consider a fall. Functional seizures, epileptic seizures, fainting and other events can sometimes look similar. The beginning, responsiveness, breathing, observed movements, time course and recovery may all matter. No single feature makes a safe diagnosis, and a home recording needs expert context.

This is why our Reference Library includes **scope boundaries**. An article about a functional symptom tells you what *that article* covers. It does not decide what happened to you.

### How the previous modules help

We now know words such as *attention*, *automatic movement* and *interoception*. We can use them to describe how a task feels, but they cannot tell us whether a collapse was a functional seizure, faint or something else. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

If a symptom is new, dangerous or substantially different, do not delay urgent assessment to search for its matching description.

**Something to think about:** When you say weak, dizzy, absent or foggy, what exactly are you trying to describe?

**One small step:** Add one concrete detail to one familiar symptom. For instance, “The room seems to turn when I move my head,” or “I feel faint after I stand.” That is more useful than attempting a diagnosis.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### “I've seen something like that before” can be both useful and misleading

A person might call a movement a tremor, jerk, spasm or tic. Someone else might call a sudden collapse fainting or a seizure. The appearances can overlap, but the causes and safe responses may differ.

### Compare the features—not the people

Suppose two people both say they're dizzy. One describes spinning; the other feels close to fainting after standing. Rather than decide that one symptom is “more FND” than the other, notice what the descriptions actually say.

The same applies when someone cannot move: is the limb weak, fixed in a posture, shaking, in pain, or part of an episode with changed awareness? A supporter can help locate the words without putting a medical label on the experience.

Avoid rehearsing symptoms, testing responsiveness, asking for repeated movements or using distractibility as a home diagnostic method. Those belong to appropriately trained clinicians, and some can be unsafe. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

### Hand the medical distinction to the right team

**Neurology** and other specialists assess relevant positive FND signs, epileptic events or movement patterns; **primary care, emergency, cardiology, ENT, ophthalmology and vestibular clinicians** may need to assess alternative or additional explanations. **OT/PT/SLT** can help with daily adaptations, functional abilities or swallowing/communication when appropriate.

Sudden stroke-like changes, serious breathing difficulty, injuries or other urgent symptoms require emergency assessment, even when the person already has FND. Refer back to the safety plan from Module 5 rather than making decisions from a symptom list.

**Something to discuss together:** “What did this feel like to you, and what did I actually see? Is there a question we want a clinician to answer?”

**One small thing:** Choose a familiar descriptive word—*dizzy*, *shaking* or *weak*, for example—and note one detail the person agrees is useful. Stop there.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Differential diagnosis is a disciplined comparison—not an exclusion ritual

The patient should learn to distinguish observable *features*, while clinicians retain responsibility for assessing *causes*. Functional signs and other medical conditions may coexist. An examination sign is meaningful only where it has sufficient specificity and reliability for the presentation under investigation. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

Use a short case with incomplete information and ask three questions: **What is observed? What remains unknown? What would change assessment or urgent management?** Avoid giving a correct diagnosis based on a story alone.

### Three teachable cases

**Case A — A movement called “shaking.”** A person reports repeated hand movements, sudden jerks and occasional limb stiffening. Neurology may need to distinguish tremor, jerks, tic-like phenomena, dystonia or another movement disorder, based on phenomenology, medications, examination and appropriate tests. Movement-disorders clinicians can interpret positive findings in context; PT/OT then use the confirmed presentation and functional goals to select corresponding recovery techniques. A rhythmic task response alone does not classify all movements.

**Case B — An episode called “passing out.”** The person has falls, apparent unresponsiveness and uncertain recall. Evaluate syncope (including potential cardiac causes), epileptic events, functional seizures, drop attacks and other relevant medical explanations using the whole event and targeted tests. A reported warning, silence or remembered speech cannot by itself confirm any one category. An event in water, abnormal breathing, injury, new focal deficit or medical instability changes immediate safety decisions; individualized episode instructions do not replace acute medical care.

**Case C — “Dizzy, foggy, and can't see properly.”** Describe rotation versus faintness versus disequilibrium, upright/motion/visual triggers, headache, light sensitivity, visual phenomena and cognitive complaints. Possible contributing disorders include vestibular conditions, PPPD, migraine, autonomic/cardiac illness, visual disease, FCD or several together. Vestibular PT, OT and ophthalmology/neurology may be needed; psychotherapeutic support cannot substitute for omitted ocular or cardiovascular assessment.

### Make teaching the differential safer and more useful

After each case, describe why a *particular kind of information* is clinically useful. For example, knowing whether a fall followed standing is useful for a blood-pressure/rhythm work-up; it does not mean every standing fall is syncope. Knowing a tremor changes with rhythm is an examination observation; it does not establish the person's control over it.

**Psychologists** can help with the burden of uncertainty and repeated medical experiences. They should not be tasked with deciding neurological etiology based on a person's stress history. **OT and PT** can provide access and injury reduction while the diagnostic process continues.

**Evidence boundary:** The specific 17 presentation pages in [Diagnostic Signs](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/README.md) and current source-guided neurological assessment govern interpretation; the fictional cases are pedagogical, not validated triage algorithms.
