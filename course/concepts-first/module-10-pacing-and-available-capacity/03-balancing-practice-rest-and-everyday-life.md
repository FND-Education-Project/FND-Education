## For the Person With FND

### There's only one day to spend

Imagine your care team recommends some practice for a movement difficulty. You also need to eat, attend an appointment, spend time with someone you love, and perhaps recover from yesterday.

The practice could be useful. It could also take up some of the capacity you need for the rest of life. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [3](#citation-3))

That is why rehabilitation and pacing belong next to one another in this course. **A useful exercise still needs to fit the person who is doing it.**

### Different kinds of progress

Sometimes progress means an activity becomes easier. Sometimes it means learning to stop before an avoidable injury or prolonged worsening. Sometimes it means finding an accommodation that lets you participate now.

None of these outcomes requires a steady upward line on a chart.

If you are in rehabilitation, a helpful review may ask: Is the activity useful? What happens afterward? Do I need a different dose, schedule, method, aid or treatment? Is another medical condition affecting how I respond?

If an activity repeatedly makes you much worse or causes harm, tell the treating clinician. Pushing harder isn't automatically the answer.

### Plans are allowed to change

An earlier version of you may have had different abilities. You might also have a day when the plan works and another when it doesn't.

A plan is a tool, not a promise. You can adjust it as life changes.

For some people, practice needs professional supervision. With delayed worsening, even seemingly modest increases may be too much. The person's response matters; this is not an instruction to push.

### Making room for living

You may want to use some capacity to practise a skill, and some to enjoy the results of having that skill—or simply to do something that matters today.

That's not a failure of commitment. Rehabilitation exists to serve your life, not to replace it.

**Something to think about:** Does your current routine leave room for something you care about besides appointments, chores and symptom management?

**One small step:** Name one activity you'd like to protect. It might be talking with a friend, watching a show comfortably, time outside or something entirely different. Then ask what would make space for it. A support, rest period or change of priorities may be as important as another exercise.

In the next stage of the course, we'll discuss the people who can help you build care around these goals.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### When helping becomes another full-time job

A rehabilitation plan might be sensible in a clinic. But then comes travel, cooking, appointments, family responsibilities, symptoms and the next day. A supporter may find themselves arranging everything and trying to keep the entire plan going.

It's possible to care deeply about someone and still be exhausted by what they're asking of you—or what the system has left you to manage.

### Preserve the relationship as well as the plan

Ask together: Which activities are genuinely helpful? Which feel unsustainable? Is therapy leaving enough room for meals, meaningful connection and rest? Is an accommodation helping right now, even when symptoms haven't improved?

You can offer support without taking responsibility for recovery. The person remains the authority on their priorities; your ability to help has limits worth respecting too.

One useful way to talk is: “I want this plan to work for both of us. I can help with transport twice this month, but not every day. Can we ask the team what other support is available?”

### Hand the clinical work back where it belongs

If a home routine is too difficult, unsafe or worsening symptoms, request review by the **physiotherapist, OT, SLP, psychologist or other treating clinician** who recommended it. Ask about the goal, dose, safety instructions, review criteria and whether another route is available. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [3](#citation-3))

For complex needs, **primary care or a designated care coordinator** may need to clarify responsibilities and referrals. If supporter exhaustion threatens safe care, ask about additional practical services or respite options where available. Those services depend on locality, eligibility and consent; they cannot be assumed.

Persistent or markedly changed medical symptoms still belong to the appropriate medical team, and emergencies require emergency services—not a renegotiated family pacing schedule.

### A shared review, not a scorecard

Together, choose one part of the current plan to **keep, modify, pause or ask the team about**. Include what the person gains and what each person's contribution costs. Continuing aids, written reminders, sensory accommodations or assistance can remain necessary alongside rehabilitation.

> “I don't blame him for any of this, I just need help!”

**Something to discuss together:** “What part of our plan makes life better, and what part would we like professionals to help us rethink?”

**One small thing:** Prepare one question for the treating team about a task or support that has become difficult to sustain. You don't need to solve it alone.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Treat the person's week, not the therapy session alone

A treatment session may appear tolerable when considered in isolation but become unsustainable when the person also travels, cooks, manages medicines, cares for others or recovers from prior episodes. Ask about the whole day and delayed effects. Participation and quality of life are independent goals; a technically improved performance that makes an ordinary week unmanageable is not a complete rehabilitation success.

**OT** may map the sequence of daily activities and available supports. **PT** reviews the dose, movement strategy, safety and functional transfer. **SLT** adjusts session communication or swallowing/voice task demands as relevant. **Psychology/neuropsychology** can help people and families make realistic decisions without making every setback a behavioural failure. **Neurology/primary care** provide ongoing medical reassessment when symptoms change.

### Work through a review with the person

A concise shared review has five questions:

1. **What matters now?** The person may value independent meals, visiting a friend, safer transport or shorter recovery from a familiar episode more than one specific test score.
2. **What did the plan actually change?** Compare function, symptom impact, equipment need, falls/injuries, confidence and support burden, not only exercise repetitions.
3. **What did it cost?** Discuss immediate and next-day pain, fatigue, cognitive fog, migraine or post-event problems. Keep PEM and other medical conditions on the assessment pathway when relevant.
4. **What needs adjusting?** Consider timing, task simplification, environment, accessible transport, less frequent sessions, clinical referral or stopping an ineffective component.
5. **Who will review it?** Specify the owner and a meaningful interval or change trigger, including urgent-care boundaries from Module 5.

### Recovery does not invalidate continuing disability support

A person can make meaningful gains and still need a wheelchair, written reminders, sensory protection, communication alternatives or assistance with some activities. The ethical risk of withdrawing access before an alternative is reliable belongs in treatment planning. A lack of improvement should prompt reassessment of goals, fit, complications, evidence and resources—not blame or automatic discharge.

Consider a person who manages a supported therapy task but needs several days to recover. The team should not infer everyday independence from the isolated task or automatically intensify practice. It should establish what the session adds to or subtracts from the person's life, while preserving appropriate assessment of any new medical changes.

### Close the module with a shared skill

Model one conversation in which the patient and clinicians jointly choose **keep, modify, replace or discontinue** a component and document why. A plan that is respectful, safe, clinically defensible and usable is a valuable outcome even when the person continues to have substantial symptoms. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [3](#citation-3))

**Evidence boundary:** Patient-centred task and environmental adaptation are grounded in OT consensus [1](#citation-1). Physio4FMD shows the importance of interpreting clinical and functional outcomes separately [2](#citation-2). PEM-related safeguards require condition-specific guidance [3](#citation-3); none establishes a universal rest-to-practice ratio.
