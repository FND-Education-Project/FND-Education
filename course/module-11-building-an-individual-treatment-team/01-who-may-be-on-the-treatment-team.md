## For the Person With FND
{: #for-the-person-with-fnd .shareable }

### The same symptoms, a different understanding

Callum is seventeen. He lives with his father and has been dealing with FND for nearly two years.

Some days his legs are unreliable. At other times, speaking becomes difficult, even though he knows exactly what he wants to say. His symptoms have affected his schoolwork, friendships and confidence.

When Callum first became ill, he and his father spent a frightening amount of time trying to understand what was happening. There were hospital visits, examinations, unfamiliar medical words and a diagnosis neither of them fully understood.

Once they had a name for his condition, they wanted to know how to make him better.

They searched for treatments, read whatever they could find and tried to understand every strange thing his body did. Callum frequently told his father about new observations, hoping another piece of information might finally explain everything.

But knowing the name of his condition didn't mean they understood it.

Over the last several months, Callum has been learning about FND. He's followed this course, explored some of the Reference Library and become quite interested in how the nervous system works.

He hasn't started a rehabilitation programme. His symptoms haven't improved very much, either.

But something important has changed.

### A difficult afternoon at school

Callum is standing in a crowded hallway with two classmates. They're discussing a group project when the school bell rings.

Students begin moving in every direction. Lockers slam, people call to one another and someone brushes past Callum's shoulder.

One of his classmates asks which part of the project he'd like to work on.

He knows the answer, but when he tries to speak, the words won't come.

He tries again, concentrating harder on getting the first word out. His mouth isn't cooperating, and the more he concentrates on speaking, the more difficult it seems.

Then he remembers something from the course.

Until a moment ago, he'd been talking without thinking about how to do it. Now he was paying attention to every little movement involved in speaking.

*Automaticity. Maybe that's part of what's happening.*

He remembers learning how attention can sometimes interfere with familiar, automatic skills.

Rather than continuing to struggle with his words in the noisy hallway, he gestures toward a quieter area near the library. His friend follows him.

Callum takes out his phone and types:

"Having trouble speaking. Give me a minute."

His friend nods.

After a while, Callum manages a few words. His speech is still difficult, but he can begin explaining which part of the project interests him.

They return to the others, and the conversation continues with a mixture of speaking and messages on his phone.

It hasn't been a particularly good afternoon, but Callum is pleased that he managed to stay involved.

### Teaching Dad a little neuroscience

When Callum gets home, his father notices that he's quieter than usual.

"Rough day?"

"Yeah. My speech went wonky again."

"Oh, no. What happened?"

"Just after lunch, in the hallway. Bell ringing, everyone shouting, people everywhere. Then someone asked me a question and my mouth decided it was taking the afternoon off."

His father smiles sympathetically.

"Did you manage all right?"

"Mostly. Actually, I think I figured something out."

Callum explains how he started concentrating on every little part of speaking when his words wouldn't come.

"You know that automaticity thing we learned about? How you normally just talk without thinking about making your mouth move?"

"Yeah, I remember."

"Well, I think I was trying to do everything manually. Like when you start thinking about exactly how you're walking and suddenly it feels all awkward."

His father nods.

"So you distracted yourself and got your speech working again?"

"Not really. I stopped trying to force the words and used my phone instead. We moved somewhere quieter, too. My speech came back gradually, but I could still answer the question while it wasn't working."

"Ah. So you found another way to do it."

"Yeah. And I think the noise was part of the bigger picture. The bell grabbed my attention, then I started focusing on my speech, and the more trouble I had, the more I focused on it. Sort of a feedback loop."

His father thinks about that.

"Wasn't there a word for the way the bell grabbed your attention?"

"Salience. Look at you!"

"Thank you. I have been paying attention."

"Clearly."

His father laughs, then asks whether Callum thinks he could do the same thing next time.

"Maybe. Depends what happens. But at least I've got another way to deal with it."

"That's good. Anything you want me to do?"

"Actually, could you help me explain it to my teacher? If I can't speak, it doesn't mean I can't understand what's happening. Sometimes I just need a different way to answer."

"Of course. We could mention it to your doctor, too."

"Yeah. But we don't need to tell him everything that happened in the hallway. Just what was different and what I noticed."

His father reaches for a pen.

"All right. What should I write?"

Together, they put together a few short notes.

For once, their conversation hasn't turned into a lengthy investigation of everything Callum's body did that afternoon. They understand one another, have something useful to discuss with his teacher and know what they want to mention to the doctor.

His father asks how the group project is going.

"Pretty well," Callum says. "Except apparently I'm responsible for the presentation."

His father raises an eyebrow.

"Interesting choice."

"Yeah. We may need to discuss that one."

### Think back to where you started

Callum's afternoon wasn't an example of his FND disappearing. He still had trouble speaking, and he still needed another way to communicate.

But compare how he handled it with what might have happened two years earlier.

Back then, Callum and his father were frightened and confused. They wanted explanations, treatment and some idea of what the future might hold. Every unusual symptom seemed like another mystery they needed to solve.

Now Callum has a general understanding of processes that might contribute to his experiences. When something familiar happens, he can sometimes recognize what might be involved and consider what could make the situation easier.

He can also explain his thinking to his father, who is gradually learning the same language.

That's a considerable change, even though Callum hasn't yet received rehabilitation and his symptoms remain troublesome.

Think about your own experience of learning about FND.

We've explored how attention, automatic movement, predictive processing, sensory information, memory retrieval and emotional regulation all contribute to how our nervous system works.

You've learned about symptoms, possible mechanisms, rehabilitation, adaptation and the many ways people experience FND differently.

You probably won't remember everything. You may even struggle to remember something you understood perfectly well yesterday.

But perhaps some of those ideas are beginning to feel familiar.

You might recognize why concentrating intensely on a movement could sometimes make it more difficult. Or you might better understand why retrieving a memory can be unreliable, even when the information hasn't necessarily been lost.

Perhaps you've started noticing when several demands are competing for your attention, or why an activity that went well yesterday seems much harder today.

You don't need to work through all the mechanisms every time something happens. Sometimes you may simply recognize a familiar pattern and have a reasonable idea about what could be contributing.

And sometimes, that understanding may suggest something useful you can change.

### Being understood changes things

There's another part of Callum's story that's easy to overlook.

His father understood what Callum meant.

Not perfectly—he mixed up a couple of ideas—but well enough that they could have a meaningful conversation about something that would once have confused them both.

Callum didn't have to begin by explaining what FND was. He didn't have to describe every sensation, movement or difficulty he'd experienced that afternoon.

He could talk about attention, automaticity and a possible feedback loop because those ideas now meant something to both of them.

And when his father misunderstood, Callum had enough knowledge to explain the difference.

Imagine how useful that could be in your own life.

Perhaps your spouse has spent years listening to observations about symptoms neither of you understood. Now you may occasionally recognize something familiar without needing another long conversation about what it means.

Perhaps a friend understands why a particular environment is difficult for you, or a parent better understands why your ability to speak, move or remember something can vary.

You might even find that you spend less time talking about your symptoms because you no longer feel the need to investigate every unusual experience.

That leaves more room for other conversations, other interests and simply enjoying someone's company.

Of course, not every symptom will be familiar. An experience that is new or significantly different may still need medical assessment. Understanding FND doesn't make you responsible for diagnosing yourself.

In fact, your new knowledge may help you explain why something seems different from your usual difficulties.

You might be able to tell a doctor, "I've experienced this other symptom before, but this one doesn't seem to follow the same pattern."

That distinction can be useful without requiring you to know what the new symptom means.

### What have you gained?

When FND first enters someone's life, the desire to recover can become urgent.

There may have been frightening symptoms, hospital visits, uncertainty about the diagnosis and an enormous amount of searching for answers.

Once the diagnosis is made, the next question often seems obvious: *How do I get better?*

You may have spent months or years looking for the right treatment, waiting for appointments or wondering whether you should be doing more.

And perhaps, even after everything you've learned, you're still waiting for rehabilitation.

But something important has happened along the way.

You now have a foundation of knowledge that can help you understand your experiences and communicate them to other people.

That knowledge doesn't have to wait until rehabilitation begins to become useful.

You may be better able to explain something to a parent who helps arrange your healthcare, describe an experience to a clinician, or tell a friend what kind of support would be helpful.

You might understand why a particular adjustment makes an activity more manageable, or recognize a familiar experience without feeling compelled to investigate it all over again.

None of these changes requires your symptoms to have improved.

And understanding something isn't the same as being able to control it. Callum recognized a possible mechanism behind his speaking difficulty, but that knowledge didn't give him the ability to switch his speech back on.

What it gave him was a way to think about the difficulty, make a useful choice and communicate more clearly.

Those accomplishments matter.

They can improve relationships, reduce confusion and make parts of everyday life easier. Being understood can itself contribute to quality of life, even when the illness remains disabling.

### Slow is smooth. Smooth is fast.

There's an old saying: **"Slow is smooth. Smooth is fast."**

It doesn't mean that going slowly guarantees faster recovery. It's a reminder that taking time to understand what you're doing can sometimes be more useful than rushing toward the next thing.

We've spent ten modules building an understanding of FND before turning our attention more fully toward navigating care.

That learning has taken us through the frightening and confusing parts of the condition, the workings of the nervous system, the possibilities for rehabilitation and the realities of living with symptoms.

There may still be a great deal you want to understand, and there may be treatments you'd like to explore.

But you don't need to solve everything at once to recognize what you've already accomplished.

Callum still has FND. He still hopes for more reliable speech, easier movement and opportunities for rehabilitation.

But he has something he didn't have at the beginning: knowledge he can use, questions he can ask and words that help other people understand what he's experiencing.

**That's an accomplishment in its own right.**

In the next lesson, we'll explore what happens when you bring that understanding into a healthcare appointment—particularly when you and a healthcare professional don't see a problem in quite the same way.

### Something to think about

What did you relate to in Callum's story? Perhaps struggling to communicate, recognizing a familiar mechanism, finding another way to do something, or explaining FND to someone else.

### How might that feel?

How does it feel to have a better understanding of the processes that might be affecting you?

### One small step

If something you've learned about FND comes to mind during everyday life, try explaining it in a sentence or two to someone you trust. Or simply enjoy recognizing it yourself.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters
{: #for-family-friends-and-other-supporters .shareable }

### The drawer labelled "brain stuff"

Callum's father had spent nearly two years watching his son struggle with things that once came naturally. Some days his walking was unreliable. At other times, speaking became difficult, even when he knew exactly what he wanted to say.

His father had learned a great deal about FND during those years, although some of the terminology still escaped him.

One Saturday afternoon, they were finishing lunch when Callum offered to help clear the table.

His father watched him collect two plates and head toward the kitchen. Callum had walked through the room several times already that morning, but carrying the plates seemed to make the journey considerably harder.

His steps became awkward, and he stopped partway through to steady himself. When he finally reached the counter, he put the plates down and leaned against it.

"That seemed harder than usual," his father observed.

"Yeah. And I'm really tired now. All I did was carry two plates."

His father offered to finish clearing the table while Callum rested.

As he put the remaining dishes away, he wondered what had changed. Callum had managed the same walk earlier. Why had carrying something made such a difference?

He remembered learning about attention and automatic movement, but couldn't quite put the ideas together.

### Finding an explanation together

Later, when Callum had rested, his father brought it up.

"Wasn't there something in the course about doing two things at once?"

"I think so. Was it automaticity?"

"Possibly. I've filed all those words in the same drawer."

"What drawer?"

"The one labelled 'brain stuff.' Very sophisticated filing system."

Callum laughed.

Neither could remember the explanation clearly, so they opened the FND Education Project and found the earlier lessons.

One familiar idea caught his father's attention:

> **Automaticity:** The nervous system's ability to carry out familiar actions without consciously controlling every movement.

They also found the discussion about doing two things at once. If walking already requires considerable attention, carrying something while walking may add competing demands and make the activity more difficult.

That seemed relevant to what he'd witnessed.

As they read, his father realized he'd been concentrating on whether Callum could make it to the kitchen. He hadn't appreciated how much effort the journey might require.

The explanation didn't establish exactly why Callum had struggled. The plates had weight, and fatigue could have several contributors. But they had found a useful possibility to consider together.

"Well, that makes a bit more sense," his father said.

"Yeah. And at least we found the right drawer."

They put the tablet away.

### Learning alongside someone

When Callum first became ill, his father had often felt helpless. They'd spent hours searching for explanations, sometimes finding useful information and sometimes ending up with more questions.

Their conversations had gradually changed. They shared enough knowledge now that neither had to begin from the beginning whenever something familiar happened.

His father had also started learning independently. He didn't want Callum to carry all the responsibility for understanding his condition.

One spouse, writing about supporting someone with functional seizures, expressed a similar intention:

> “I'm trying to be supportive and to learn about it on my own so she doesn't have to fully educate me.”

*Community voice — spouse describing an effort to understand a loved one's condition. [Original public discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/WellSpouses/comments/1q2nlh9/pnes_experience/).*

Callum's father recognized the sentiment. Sometimes Callum remembered a term he'd forgotten. Other times, his father remembered an example that helped them find what they were looking for.

Neither needed to become the expert. They could remain curious together.

### Knowing when to leave things alone

Later that afternoon, they settled down to watch a film.

Callum's legs were still tired, but he seemed comfortable on the sofa. He laughed at something on the screen, and before long they were debating which character was going to cause the next disaster.

When the film ended, Callum shifted forward as though preparing to stand, then settled back again.

His father noticed and briefly wondered whether attention was affecting his movement.

After their earlier reading, it was tempting to ask.

But Callum seemed content, and there was no need to turn the moment into another investigation.

"Want anything while I'm up?" his father asked.

"Some water would be great."

His father brought him a glass and returned to his chair.

A few minutes later, Callum recognized an actor on the television.

"He's been in something else we watched. What's his name?"

His father searched his memory without success.

"No idea. I know the face, though."

They spent a minute trying to remember before looking him up.

"Another visit to the brain-stuff drawer," Callum remarked.

"Apparently the filing system needs some maintenance."

They laughed and returned to their film.

### Recognizing progress from the supporter's side

For a long time, Callum's father had naturally looked for progress in his son's symptoms.

Was his walking becoming more reliable? Was speaking getting easier? Could he participate in more activities than before?

Those questions still mattered. Callum continued to experience considerable disability, and his father hoped that appropriate rehabilitation might eventually help.

But he was beginning to recognize other changes, too.

Their conversations were easier. They shared a language for discussing some of Callum's experiences, and his father could better appreciate why an apparently simple activity might be difficult or exhausting.

He also felt less pressure to find an explanation for every familiar symptom. When they encountered a question worth exploring, they could look for information together. When something needed professional assessment, they could bring that concern to the appropriate clinician rather than trying to resolve it themselves.

And when Callum didn't want to discuss FND, his father could respect that choice.

None of these developments meant Callum's symptoms had disappeared. They didn't establish that his rehabilitation needs had been met, either.

But his father no longer measured the value of everything they'd learned solely by whether Callum could walk farther or speak more reliably.

Their shared understanding had become useful in its own right.

**He was learning how to understand his son's experiences without needing to explain every one of them—and how to remain his father, rather than becoming an investigator of everything his son's nervous system did.**

There was still a great deal they hoped would improve. But that Saturday afternoon reminded him that they had already come a considerable way together.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team
{: #for-clinicians-and-the-care-team .shareable }

### When the patient already understands

Callum is seventeen and has an established diagnosis of FND, with unreliable walking and episodes when speaking becomes difficult or temporarily impossible. He attends an appointment with his father, hoping to discuss an upcoming school presentation.

His family doctor notices a change since their last meeting. Previously, much of the consultation was spent discussing what FND meant and why Callum's abilities varied. Today, Callum speaks comfortably about automaticity, attention and the possible processes contributing to his symptoms.

He's been following an educational course about FND, and his father has been learning alongside him.

"I still have all the problems," Callum explains, "but at least I understand some of what's happening now."

He describes a recent episode in a crowded school hallway. When speaking became difficult, he recognized that concentrating intensely on producing words might be interfering with an automatic skill. He moved somewhere quieter and used his phone to communicate until speaking became easier.

The doctor is interested in his reasoning. Callum has developed a useful understanding of his condition, and his father reports that they no longer spend so much time trying to explain every unusual symptom.

But Callum has something more immediate on his mind.

"I've got a group presentation coming up. I've done loads of research for it, but I don't know if I'll be able to talk when it's my turn."

### From explaining symptoms to deciding what matters

Callum's teacher knows about his FND but isn't sure how to handle the unpredictable speech difficulties. She has wondered whether someone else should present Callum's part.

Callum would rather remain involved.

"I want to do it myself. If I can't talk, maybe I could type something and have someone read it."

His father asks whether a medical letter might help the school understand his needs.

The doctor now has a useful clinical question. Callum isn't asking to avoid the presentation, and he isn't expecting an assurance that his speech will work perfectly. He's asking for a way to participate despite uncertainty.

This opens a discussion about two related goals: making the upcoming activity accessible and exploring rehabilitation that could improve the reliability of his communication.

The distinction is familiar clinically, but Callum's understanding allows him to take a more active role in deciding how those goals fit together.

### When accommodation and rehabilitation meet

The possibility of typed communication raises an interesting question. Could an alternative way of communicating become something Callum relies on instead of practising spoken communication?

It's worth considering, particularly when rehabilitation may involve working toward more automatic, reliable speech. Yet removing an accommodation doesn't necessarily promote rehabilitation, just as providing one doesn't necessarily discourage recovery.

Callum offers a useful perspective.

"I still want to talk. I'd just like another option if my speech stops working."

That helps clarify the immediate goal. The presentation is an educational activity, not a rehabilitation exercise. Callum wants to participate using speech when available, with an alternative that keeps him involved when it isn't.

The doctor and family can discuss how this might be explained to the school without turning the accommodation into a prediction about his future abilities.

The longer-term question concerns whether Callum would benefit from assessment and treatment by a speech and language therapist familiar with functional communication difficulties.

Baker and colleagues' 2021 consensus recommendations describe individualized approaches to assessing and treating functional communication disorders, including attention to meaningful communication activities. These provide a professional framework, rather than evidence that one particular strategy will work for Callum. (*citation* [3](#citation-3))

If an appropriate service is available, Callum's knowledge and observations may give the therapist a useful starting point. His school accommodation and any rehabilitation plan can then be considered together, with each serving a clear purpose.

### Making use of the patient's understanding

There's another opportunity in Callum's account.

He has begun developing explanations for his experiences rather than simply listing symptoms. His ideas about attention and automaticity are informed by established FND concepts, although they don't establish which processes caused a particular episode.

The doctor could spend considerable time evaluating each proposed mechanism. Some discussion may be useful, especially where it changes the clinical formulation or raises a question requiring assessment.

But the more productive development may be that Callum can now describe what happens, what he notices and what he wants help achieving.

His understanding has changed the starting point of the appointment.

The conversation can move directly toward meaningful decisions while leaving room to examine his interpretations as rehabilitation progresses.

This also offers an opportunity to recognize the work Callum has done without suggesting that understanding should have made his symptoms disappear.

For someone who hasn't yet received appropriate rehabilitation, education may be an accomplishment and a preparation for further care, rather than evidence that recovery has already occurred.

### What has changed clinically?

The doctor considers what Callum's teacher needs to understand. Callum speaks comfortably during today's consultation, but his communication difficulties still disrupt school activities. More importantly, Callum has explained what he wants: to present his own work, using spoken communication when possible and another method when necessary.

That gives the doctor a practical direction for discussing school arrangements without making assumptions about Callum's future abilities.

His father can help with those conversations, but Callum has taken an increasingly active role in identifying his needs. The doctor also has a clearer reason to consider specialist speech and language assessment: Callum wants to explore whether rehabilitation could make his communication more reliable.

These are two opportunities arising from the same appointment. One addresses participation now; the other considers possible improvement through rehabilitation. **Callum's growing understanding has helped him take part in shaping both.**

### A different benefit of FND education

Much of FND care understandably focuses on whether symptoms improve and whether treatment changes everyday function. Yet education can have value before either outcome occurs.

Callum's learning hasn't restored reliable speech or movement. It has helped him describe his experiences, communicate with his father and participate more confidently in decisions about his care.

For the clinician, this creates an opportunity to build on understanding already established rather than repeatedly returning to the foundations of the diagnosis.

The important question may no longer be whether Callum understands FND.

It may be **what that understanding now allows him and his clinical team to accomplish together**.

In this case, it has helped turn a difficult school presentation into a clearer conversation about participation, communication needs and possible rehabilitation.
