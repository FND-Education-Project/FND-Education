## For the Person With FND
{: #for-the-person-with-fnd .shareable }

### An appointment five hours away

Graham is forty-six and lives in a small community several hours from the nearest major city. He's been living with FND for six years.

His walking is unreliable, and functional seizures sometimes leave him unable to move or respond. He also has difficulty concentrating and remembering information, particularly when several things are happening at once.

Over the years, Graham has seen numerous specialists, tried different treatments and spent a considerable amount of time looking for help. Some appointments have been worthwhile. Others have left him with more questions than answers.

He has a family doctor, Dr. Singh, and a local physiotherapist who's been supportive, although she has limited experience treating FND. What he hasn't found is a rehabilitation programme that he can realistically attend.

One morning, a letter arrives from a neurological rehabilitation clinic in a larger city.

After months of waiting, he's finally been offered an appointment.

At nine o'clock in the morning.

Nearly five hours away.

Graham looks at the letter and sighs.

He no longer drives because of his seizures, so someone will need to take him. They'll probably need a hotel room the night before. Then there's fuel, meals, parking and the journey home.

And that's assuming he can manage the trip without his symptoms becoming too difficult.

He starts adding up the expenses.

*This is going to cost a fortune.*

But he's been waiting a long time for help. What if this appointment finally leads to something useful?

The letter says he'll receive an assessment and recommendations.

Graham remembers an assessment he attended a couple of years earlier, required as part of a disability application. He'd spent hours answering questions and completing tests. The process was exhausting, and although the resulting report was detailed, it hadn't led to any treatment.

He understands now that the assessment was intended to inform a disability decision, not arrange rehabilitation. Still, he remembers how disappointing it was to put so much effort into something that didn't improve his access to care.

Looking at the new appointment letter, Graham realizes he doesn't know what will happen afterward.

*Are they actually going to treat me, or am I coming home with another report?*

### A question worth asking

At his next appointment with Dr. Singh, Graham brings the letter.

"I finally got into that rehabilitation clinic," he says.

"That's good news. You've been waiting a long time."

"Yeah, but I'm not sure it's worth the trip."

Dr. Singh looks up.

"It's an opportunity to have your condition assessed by people who know about FND."

"I know. But it's ten hours of driving, probably a hotel, and someone taking time off work. What happens after they assess me?"

Dr. Singh checks the referral information.

"I'm not sure whether they provide ongoing treatment for someone living that far away."

"That's what worries me. I've had plenty of assessments. I don't really need another person telling me my walking is difficult. I've noticed."

Dr. Singh smiles.

"Fair enough. What would you most like their help with?"

"My walking. I'd like to know whether there's something my physio could actually work on with me."

"That's worth asking. The clinic may be able to provide guidance, even if they can't see you regularly."

"Could we find out before I spend a thousand dollars getting there?"

"Yes. Let's contact them."

A couple of weeks later, they receive a reply. The clinic can provide a specialist assessment and recommendations, but it doesn't offer regular remote rehabilitation appointments for patients in Graham's area.

Graham is disappointed.

"So they might tell us what to do, but there's no guarantee anyone here can do it?"

"That's right. Their advice could still be useful, but we should talk to your physiotherapist about what she might be able to offer."

Graham thinks about that. His physiotherapist knows him well, and she's been interested in learning more about FND. Perhaps specialist advice would help her. Or perhaps the treatment would require training or supervision she doesn't have.

"Let's ask her first," he says. "I'd rather know what we're getting into."

They agree to keep the specialist appointment for now while Dr. Singh helps explore the local options.

Graham still doesn't know whether he'll make the trip. But he now has a better idea of what he needs to find out before deciding.

### The work involved in getting healthcare

There's something important about Graham's situation that deserves attention.

Getting healthcare takes work.

> **Treatment burden:** The time, money, effort and other demands involved in getting and managing healthcare. This can include appointments, travel, paperwork, treatment routines and arranging help from other people.

Think about what a specialist appointment might involve for you.

You may need to arrange transportation, deal with an unfamiliar building, sit beneath bright lights or explain your medical history while struggling with pain, fatigue or cognitive symptoms.

Perhaps you need someone to accompany you. They may have to take time off work, arrange childcare or set aside other responsibilities. You might also have expenses that aren't covered by insurance or your healthcare system.

And when you get home, there may be forms, prescriptions, follow-up calls or further appointments to manage.

We've already learned how the demands of an activity can affect your available capacity. What's different here is that **the healthcare intended to help you can place considerable demands on the very abilities you're struggling with.**

It's reasonable to consider those demands when deciding whether another appointment is worthwhile.

That doesn't mean avoiding difficult appointments. Some assessments and treatments are important even when attending them is challenging.

But your circumstances matter, and the effort involved deserves to be part of the conversation.

### An assessment isn't necessarily treatment

Remember Graham's earlier disability assessment?

He'd spent hours being examined and answering questions, hoping the process might lead to something useful for his recovery.

But the assessment had been arranged to answer questions about his disability, not to provide treatment.

The rehabilitation clinic has a different purpose. Its assessment might help Graham and his local clinicians understand which treatments could be appropriate.

Yet there's another distinction worth recognizing: **receiving recommendations doesn't mean the recommended treatment will be available.**

Imagine being told that a particular kind of rehabilitation might help you, only to discover that nobody within travelling distance offers it.

You'd have learned something useful, but you'd still have the original problem of accessing care.

This is why Graham wants to know what might happen after the specialist appointment. He's not questioning whether the clinic's expertise is valuable. He's trying to understand whether that expertise can make a practical difference in his life.

The same question can apply to other referrals. An appointment might clarify a diagnosis, offer treatment, provide an opinion or help another clinician make a decision. Knowing its purpose can help you decide what value it might have for you.

### What if the specialist isn't available?

Perhaps you've already spent years looking for someone with experience treating FND.

You've been referred to specialists who aren't accepting patients, discovered that a clinic is too far away or learned that a recommended treatment isn't covered.

Maybe someone has suggested another professional, but you're no longer sure you have the money, time or capacity to keep searching.

Those are real barriers. Understanding FND doesn't make them disappear.

You may also have encountered professionals who know relatively little about the condition. That can be frustrating when you've spent considerable time learning about it yourself.

But the absence of an FND specialist doesn't mean all the healthcare available to you is useless.

Consider Graham's situation.

He has a family doctor who knows his medical history and a physiotherapist who wants to help. Neither can offer everything he needs, but they may be able to provide useful care within their areas of expertise.

His family doctor can continue treating other health problems, reviewing new concerns and helping decide whether further referrals are needed.

His physiotherapist might be able to build on her existing skills with appropriate specialist guidance. Or she might recognize that a particular treatment requires expertise she doesn't have.

Either outcome gives Graham something important: a clearer understanding of what care is realistically available.

Sometimes, the answer will still be disappointing. A treatment may exist but remain out of reach.

You can recognize that limitation without pretending an unsuitable alternative is just as good.

### When is another appointment worth pursuing?

There's no single answer, but Graham's experience gives us something useful to consider.

Before receiving the clinic's letter, he knew he wanted rehabilitation. Once the appointment arrived, however, he began asking a more specific question.

*What might this appointment change?*

For Graham, the answer depends partly on whether the clinic can offer guidance that his local physiotherapist could reasonably use.

Your situation might be different.

Perhaps you're uncertain about an earlier diagnosis, and another specialist could help clarify it. Or you may have a separate health problem, such as migraine, that still needs treatment.

You might already have a useful care arrangement and want another opinion only if something important changes.

Sometimes, another appointment could open a door that has been closed for years. At other times, it might repeat an assessment you've already had without offering anything new.

And sometimes, even a potentially valuable appointment may be too difficult or expensive to attend right now.

Choosing to postpone or decline a nonurgent consultation can be reasonable when you understand what it might offer and what you may be giving up. That doesn't mean deciding that you no longer deserve treatment.

You can continue hoping for better care while making choices that reflect the life you're living today.

### You don't need an enormous treatment team

You may have seen descriptions of FND treatment programmes involving neurologists, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, psychologists and several other professionals.

When those services are available and suited to your needs, working with different specialists can be helpful.

But what if your healthcare consists of a family doctor and the occasional specialist appointment? What if you depend on a parent to arrange appointments, or you live alone and have nobody available to take you?

An ideal treatment programme isn't much use if you can't access it.

That doesn't mean you should accept inadequate care. It means your plan needs to take account of what's actually possible.

Graham has decided to concentrate on one problem: finding out whether suitable rehabilitation for his walking can be provided through his existing clinicians, perhaps with specialist advice.

He hasn't abandoned other healthcare needs, and he hasn't ruled out travelling to the clinic. He's simply trying to find an approach that might lead to treatment rather than another disconnected recommendation.

You may have a different priority. Perhaps getting better migraine care matters most right now, or you need help with a daily activity that's become particularly difficult.

You don't have to solve every healthcare problem at once.

And you shouldn't have to organize every part of your healthcare yourself. If a family doctor or another clinician can help coordinate the next step, that assistance may make care more manageable.

**A small care arrangement that addresses your actual needs may be more useful than a long list of referrals that never come together.**

### What have we learned from this module?

We've now met three people facing very different situations.

Callum learned how understanding FND helped him recognize and explain his experiences, even though his symptoms hadn't improved very much.

Nadia discovered that she could respect her doctor while asking whether a referral addressed the problem she'd originally brought to him.

And Graham began looking beyond simply obtaining appointments to consider whether the care offered could realistically help him.

None has found a perfect solution. Callum still struggles with his speech, Nadia still needs migraine care, and Graham doesn't yet know whether suitable rehabilitation will become available.

But each has learned something that changes how they approach the next step.

Perhaps you recognize parts of your own experience in their stories. You may still have severe symptoms, unanswered questions or limited access to treatment. You may also be tired of searching for help that never quite arrives.

The knowledge you've gained throughout this course doesn't remove those difficulties, but it may help you understand your choices, explain your needs and recognize which questions are worth pursuing.

Sometimes the next step will be another specialist. Sometimes it will be a better conversation with a professional you already know. And sometimes it will be recognizing that a particular service isn't meeting your needs and considering what else might be possible.

**You deserve healthcare that addresses the problems that matter to you, and the process of obtaining that care should take your circumstances into account.**

In the next module, we'll return to Nadia as she attends her psychological assessment. We'll explore what happens when psychological care is offered, what questions it raises and how someone with FND can decide whether that care is appropriate for them.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters
{: #for-family-friends-and-other-supporters .shareable }

### It's more than a lift

Lena has known Graham for years. Since his functional seizures made driving unsafe, she's occasionally taken him to appointments or helped with errands when his walking was unreliable.

She's delighted when he tells her he's finally been offered an appointment at a neurological rehabilitation clinic. He's spent years looking for appropriate FND treatment.

Then she sees the letter.

Nine o'clock in the morning. Nearly five hours away.

Lena checks her calendar. They'd need to travel the day before, stay overnight and drive home after the appointment. She'd have to rearrange work, and there would be fuel, accommodation and meals to consider.

She wants to help. But what she'd imagined as a lift has become a two-day commitment, possibly longer if Graham has difficulty travelling.

A few days later, she visits to discuss the arrangements.

"I think I could get the time off. Have you decided whether you're going?"

"Not yet. Dr. Singh contacted the clinic. They'll assess me and make recommendations, but they don't provide ongoing remote rehabilitation where I live."

Lena is surprised. She'd assumed the clinic would either treat Graham or arrange treatment closer to home.

"So what happens after the assessment?"

"That's what we're trying to find out. My physiotherapist might be able to use some of their advice, but she hasn't had specialist FND training."

Lena considers what that could mean. Graham might make the exhausting journey, receive useful recommendations and still have nobody available to provide the treatment.

There's something she needs to clarify, too.

"I could probably help with this appointment. But if you ended up needing weekly treatment in the city, I couldn't keep making the trip."

"I know," Graham replies. "I wouldn't expect you to."

He's already been through assessments that produced detailed reports without leading to treatment. He doesn't want to spend another thousand dollars simply to discover what he still can't access.

Lena understands. He isn't looking for an excuse to abandon the appointment. He's trying to find out whether it could lead somewhere useful.

Dr. Singh is exploring what support the clinic might offer Graham's local physiotherapist. They've decided to keep the appointment while they learn more.

Lena agrees to check her work schedule, and they leave the travel arrangements open until Graham knows more.

### What can a supporter realistically offer?

On her way home, Lena feels better about their conversation.

She'd worried that admitting her limits might discourage Graham. Instead, they'd both been able to explain what mattered.

He wanted appropriate rehabilitation. She wanted to help him access it. Neither could promise that one specialist appointment would achieve those things.

The practical demands of supporting someone through healthcare are easy to underestimate.

A 2019 Australian report on FND experiences included 66 carers, many of whom described financial and other effects of caring. The respondents were a self-selected group, but their experiences illustrate why supporters' circumstances deserve consideration, too. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

*[Experiences of Functional Neurological Disorder — Summary Report, Australian National Mental Health Commission (2019)](https://www.mentalhealthcommission.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-04/experiences-of-functional-neurological-disorder---summary-report.pdf).*

Lena has responsibilities beyond helping Graham. She can rearrange some of them, but not indefinitely.

And Graham understands that. He doesn't want his friend's kindness to become an obligation she can't sustain.

### One journey isn't a treatment plan

The following week, Graham phones with an update. Dr. Singh is still clarifying what professional support the clinic could offer his physiotherapist.

Lena has checked her schedule. She could probably arrange the time away for the initial appointment if Graham decides to attend.

"Thanks. I really appreciate it," Graham says.

"Of course. I just wish getting treatment wasn't so complicated."

"Me too."

They agree to revisit the travel plans once he has more information.

Lena hopes Graham will finally find rehabilitation that helps. But she won't pressure him to make the journey simply because she's offered to drive.

If he decides the assessment is worthwhile, she'll help where she can. If he postpones it because the likely benefit remains uncertain, she'll respect that decision.

Graham's healthcare choices are his to make.

### Helping without becoming the solution

There's an important distinction in Lena's experience.

**A supporter may be able to help someone attend an appointment without being able to help them access the treatment that follows.**

A specialist assessment might be valuable, but written recommendations don't automatically create a qualified local provider, an affordable treatment programme or reliable transportation.

Those gaps belong to the healthcare-access problem. They aren't evidence that the person with FND hasn't tried hard enough, or that their supporter hasn't offered enough.

Some people have a friend like Lena who can occasionally help. Others have nobody available to accompany them. Even a willing supporter may face limits involving work, finances, health or family responsibilities.

The person with FND needs to understand what care is actually being offered. The supporter needs to be clear about what assistance they can realistically provide.

Both sets of information matter when considering the next step.

Lena hasn't removed Graham's barriers, and she hasn't decided whether he should attend. She's offered the help she can manage and left him free to make an informed choice.

**Supporting someone's access to healthcare doesn't mean becoming responsible for providing that access.**

Graham still deserves appropriate rehabilitation, whether or not Lena can drive him to every appointment. And Lena can remain a caring friend without promising more than she can deliver.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team
{: #for-clinicians-and-the-care-team .shareable }

### When specialist advice isn't the same as specialist care

"I'm not sure I could provide that treatment."

Dr. Singh is speaking with Graham's local physiotherapist, who practises in the small community where he lives. They've been discussing an assessment Graham has been offered at a neurological rehabilitation clinic nearly five hours away.

Graham has longstanding FND, including unreliable walking and functional seizures. He's been hoping to obtain specialized rehabilitation, but the distant clinic has confirmed that it can provide an assessment and recommendations, not ongoing remote treatment.

Dr. Singh had hoped the specialist recommendations might help Graham receive rehabilitation closer to home.

The physiotherapist shares his interest, although she has concerns.

"I've read some of the FND rehabilitation guidance," she explains. "But if they recommend a particular movement-retraining approach, I'm not sure I'd know how to apply it properly. I haven't had specialist training."

Dr. Singh wonders whether a sufficiently detailed report could provide the direction she needs.

"Perhaps for some things," she replies. "But what happens if Graham responds differently than expected? I'd want to know whether I was adapting the approach appropriately."

It's a reasonable concern. Dr. Singh is thinking about how specialist knowledge might become accessible locally. The physiotherapist is considering what she can competently deliver once a recommendation becomes an actual treatment.

They agree that a report could be valuable. What remains uncertain is how much of it could be put into practice.

### Where does local expertise fit?

Graham's physiotherapist already knows his mobility difficulties and has been interested in learning more about FND. Her existing skills and therapeutic relationship offer something useful, even though she doesn't have extensive experience with FND-specific rehabilitation.

She can continue helping within her professional expertise, including appropriate mobility assessment and support for everyday activities. If the specialist clinic recommends an approach she understands and can safely provide, that may offer another opportunity.

The harder question concerns recommendations that depend on specialist clinical judgment.

A treatment report might describe a useful rehabilitation principle without showing how to adapt it to Graham's particular presentation. Would the physiotherapist need a demonstration, additional training or an opportunity to discuss his response with someone experienced in FND rehabilitation?

That question interests Dr. Singh because it changes what he'd want from the specialist clinic.

Professional FND management recommendations discuss individualized rehabilitation and cooperation between disciplines, including the possible contribution of clinicians who aren't based in specialist centres (Lehn and colleagues, 2025). But those general approaches cannot determine the skills available in Graham's community or what support his physiotherapist would need. (*citations* [2](#citation-2))

The two clinicians begin identifying which aspects of care are already available locally and which would depend on further specialist involvement.

This is more useful than assuming either that the physiotherapist can provide the recommended rehabilitation or that she has nothing to contribute.

### What would the referral need to accomplish?

Dr. Singh has already learned that the clinic won't provide ongoing remote rehabilitation. That doesn't necessarily make the assessment unhelpful, but its value is becoming more dependent on what follows.

There are several possibilities.

The specialists might identify a rehabilitation approach Graham's physiotherapist can reasonably use. They might offer a consultation with her, helping clarify what to do when questions arise during treatment.

Alternatively, they might recommend a programme requiring expertise or facilities that Graham cannot access.

Dr. Singh and the physiotherapist don't yet know which outcome is most likely.

This leads them to a more specific clinical question: **Could the clinic provide enough information or professional support to make any recommended rehabilitation practical in Graham's community?**

They decide that clarification would be useful before Graham undertakes the expensive journey.

There are different ways to obtain it. Graham could ask the clinic what patients typically receive after an assessment. Administrative staff may be able to explain which services are offered.

The question about training, supervision and implementation is more clinical. With Graham's agreement, Dr. Singh could discuss it with the specialist service or help arrange communication between the clinic and the physiotherapist.

In this situation, Dr. Singh chooses to pursue that conversation because the answer may influence both the referral decision and the care his team can offer.

It isn't simply another task assigned to Graham, who already faces considerable difficulty arranging and attending appointments.

### A decision Graham can participate in

When Dr. Singh next speaks with Graham, he explains what the physiotherapist has raised.

"She wants to help, and she thinks specialist advice could be useful. But there may be recommendations she'd need additional training or support to carry out."

Graham considers this.

"So they could tell us what treatment I need, but we might still have nobody who can provide it?"

"That's one possibility. We don't know yet whether the clinic could help your physiotherapist use its recommendations."

Graham has previously undergone assessments that didn't lead to treatment. He's understandably reluctant to spend more money and energy on another consultation unless it has a realistic purpose.

At the same time, he recognizes that the specialist opinion might identify something useful that hasn't been considered locally.

Dr. Singh explains what he hopes to clarify with the clinic. Graham agrees that a discussion with his physiotherapist would be worthwhile, particularly if the specialists can help identify what might actually be delivered near home.

For now, Graham chooses to retain the appointment while they seek that information.

He hasn't committed himself to travelling regardless of the outcome, and Dr. Singh hasn't promised that the referral will produce an accessible treatment.

They've agreed on what they need to know before making the next decision.

### When the available services cannot provide everything

Even if the clinic offers useful recommendations, some aspects of Graham's rehabilitation may remain unavailable.

That possibility doesn't change the value of the care his local professionals can competently provide. Nor does it mean that ordinary physiotherapy should be presented as a substitute for specialist FND rehabilitation.

For Dr. Singh, an important part of coordinating care is keeping those distinctions visible.

Graham continues to need appropriate medical care, review of his other health concerns and a clinician he can return to when circumstances change. His physiotherapist can continue providing support within her expertise while considering whether specialist input would expand what she can offer.

What remains uncertain is whether Graham will obtain the rehabilitation he has been seeking.

Dr. Singh considers what remains unresolved. Graham has an appointment for a specialist assessment, but no established route to the rehabilitation he hopes to receive. His physiotherapist is willing to collaborate, although some recommendations might require training or supervision she doesn't currently have.

That distinction matters beyond the referral itself. If the clinic cannot offer suitable support, Graham may still have an unmet rehabilitation need, even after receiving a detailed assessment.

With Graham's agreement, Dr. Singh will clarify what the specialist service can provide and arrange another discussion before the journey. Graham's local care continues in the meantime, within the expertise of the professionals already involved.

The next conversation may reveal a realistic opportunity for rehabilitation, or it may confirm that an important service remains inaccessible. Either result will help Graham and his clinicians understand what has actually become possible, rather than confusing the existence of a referral with access to treatment.

### What makes the assessment worthwhile?

The conversation between Dr. Singh and the physiotherapist has changed the way they think about Graham's referral.

Initially, the value seemed to lie in obtaining an assessment from someone with specialized knowledge. That remains a potential benefit, but they've identified another consideration: what would need to happen for the assessment to lead to useful care?

A specialist opinion may be valuable even when it doesn't result in immediate treatment. It can clarify uncertainty, inform local decisions or identify options that weren't previously considered.

For Graham, however, the considerable cost of attending makes the practical consequences particularly important.

The clinicians may ultimately discover that the clinic cannot provide the professional support needed to implement its recommendations locally. Graham might still choose to attend, or he might decide that the likely benefit doesn't justify the journey at present.

Neither decision establishes that the referral was inappropriate.

What has improved is their understanding of its purpose, its limitations and the choices available.

**The interesting clinical question isn't simply whether specialist expertise would benefit Graham. It's how that expertise might become useful in the care he can realistically access.**

Dr. Singh and the physiotherapist may not be able to remove the barriers, but their collaboration gives Graham a clearer basis for deciding what to pursue next.
