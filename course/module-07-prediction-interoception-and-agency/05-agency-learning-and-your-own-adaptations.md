## For the Person With FND

### “That didn't feel like me.”

You move your hand to pick up a glass. Usually, you experience the movement as something you intended and carried out.

Now imagine your arm jerking without warning. Even though it's your arm, the movement doesn't feel chosen. People with FND may describe movements, sounds or other experiences that feel outside their usual control.

Scientists call the sense that **“I caused this action”** a *sense of agency*. It is different from whether the movement really happened, whether you own your body, or whether you are responsible for an action. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

Agency is a subject of active FND research. An altered sense of control is real as an experience; it cannot establish a mechanism or diagnosis by itself.

### What about learning?

Think of learning a route through a new building. At first, you read every sign. After several visits, the route becomes familiar. Learning has changed how much work the journey takes.

The nervous system can learn patterns of movement, attention and expectation. Researchers investigate how learning may influence functional symptoms, although they cannot assume the same pattern developed in everyone.

Learning is not the same as pretending. It doesn't mean you chose a symptom or that you can simply unlearn it on command.

### Bringing the ideas together

We have explored attention, automatic actions, predictions, body signals, salience, arousal and agency. That's quite a cast of characters.

The point isn't to work out which single character is “to blame.” It is to develop better questions.

Here's an example. Imagine that answering questions is hard during an appointment. You might wonder whether unexpected questions, fatigue, several people talking or word-finding difficulties make the task more demanding. You don't have to choose one cause.

You could ask for written questions, extra time or a quieter room. These changes are useful possibilities because they make communication more accessible—not because they prove a particular brain theory.

### A small way to reason through adaptations

**Understand:** What ordinary brain or body function have I learned about?

**Observe:** What actually happens in my life, leaving unknowns open and without self-testing?

**Consider:** What are some possibilities? What remains unknown? Does something need medical assessment?

**Adapt:** What safe, reversible adjustment might make the task more possible?

**Review:** Did it help safety, effort, independence or enjoyment? If not, can I leave it behind?

**Something to think about:** Is there an adaptation you've already found that makes an important part of life easier? You may have learned something useful long before you knew the scientific words.

**One small step:** Take one existing accommodation and finish the sentence: “This helps me because it makes _____ easier.” That answer matters, even if you cannot explain the precise biology.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### Wanting to do something is not the same as being able to do it

Imagine someone wants to lift a cup but their arm jerks, stiffens or refuses to move. You can believe their intention **and** recognize that the movement isn't reliably under their control.

A person's sense of agency—the experience of initiating an action—can be affected in some FND experiences. It doesn't tell you what they want, what they are capable of tomorrow or whether they are responsible for symptoms. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

### Ask before you take over

That distinction works at home, too. You might ask: “Would you like me to move the cup, help you hold it, or wait?” The answer belongs to the person. They may welcome assistance with one task and not another.

### Adaptation is collaboration, not treatment by the family

Try the course's reasoning sequence together:

**Understand** what the task involves. **Notice** what is difficult without provoking symptoms. **Consider** safety, other diagnoses and alternatives. **Adapt** only with consent. **Review** whether it helped.

The supporter can offer practical help or observations; **OT, PT, SLT or psychology** can guide techniques when specialized assessment, training, safety or symptom-specific therapy is needed. No family member needs to become a clinician.

> “I always offer and then it's her choice if she wants me there or not.”

*Figure 1. Community experience.* A spouse describes offering to attend appointments. [Original public source](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1o5s0b8/wife_just_diagnosed_with_disassociative_fnd_feels/). (*citations* [4](#citation-4))

**Something to discuss together:** “What kind of help feels respectful to you, and when would you prefer I ask rather than act?”

**One small thing:** Agree on a phrase that makes it easy to accept or decline assistance without giving a long explanation.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Separate intention, experience and actual control

The **sense of agency** concerns the experience of initiating or controlling an action. It is distinct from intention, responsibility, body ownership, voluntary effort and the reliable ability to repeat an action. Explicit self-report, intentional-binding paradigms, imaging and naturalistic daily performance are different measures and cannot be substituted for one another. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

Small motor-FND imaging studies have examined agency-related processes. They are not validity tests, biomarkers or evidence that patients lack insight. Describe agency findings as emerging research, not an explanation that can be assigned from behaviour alone.

### What would an adaptation look like in each profession?

**Physiotherapy:** Let a person identify a valued movement and decide whether a clinician-guided task change improves consistency or reduces risk.

**Occupational therapy:** Use task analysis to identify where intended action and accessible execution diverge; offer a tool, positioning change, task sequence or alternative method while protecting autonomy.

**Psychology/neuropsychology:** Work with loss of confidence, dissociation, identity or the effects of unpredictable control *where present*, not as required causes or prerequisites for care.

**Speech-language therapy:** A person may intend to speak but lose voice or fluency; support their chosen communication channel without making spontaneous speech a test of credibility.

### A shared reasoning method

A concise clinical sequence is: **define the task → ask what the person experienced → check alternative/coexisting medical explanations → agree on one safe adjustment → review reliability and life impact**. A positive response is useful for care, not proof of the proposed brain mechanism; an absent response does not invalidate the diagnosis.

In shared discussion, say “the symptom is involuntary; we can work with the conditions in which the activity is possible” rather than “your brain is making you do it because you expected it.”

**Evidence boundary:** Agency imaging is limited and phenotype-specific [1](#citation-1). Broader circuit frameworks [2](#citation-2) remain proposals.
