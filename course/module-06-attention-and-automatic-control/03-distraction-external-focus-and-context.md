## For the Person With FND

### A change is information, not an accusation

Picture trying to fasten a button while three people watch. You become aware of every movement. Someone says, “Just relax,” which is rarely as useful as they seem to think.

Later, you fasten the same button while thinking about what to have for lunch. It may feel easier—or it may not.

The difference is worth understanding without turning it into a judgement.

**Distraction** means attention has been drawn elsewhere. **[External focus](../../glossary/README.md#external-focus)** means concentrating on a goal outside the movement, such as where an object needs to go. They overlap, but they aren't identical. **Context** includes the surroundings, demands, support, timing and purpose of the task.

A familiar movement can change when any of these change. Some functional movement symptoms also vary with attention during a specialist examination. That variability can be diagnostically useful, but it does not mean the person is performing the symptom.

### Distraction isn't the same as ignoring

A person with chronic pain, dizziness or a functional seizure may still need to notice important warnings. Pretending a dangerous situation isn't happening is not a treatment.

Nor does every symptom improve when attention moves. Some people find extra sensory input overwhelming. A second task can make balance worse, especially if they already have a high fall risk.

People report different responses. **The relationship between attention and a symptom may be worth discussing with a qualified clinician.** (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

### Asking better questions

Instead of thinking, “If I can do it another way, why can't I just do it?”, try: “What changed between the two situations?”

Was there less noise? A clearer goal? Less pressure? A different position? A helpful support? More energy? Perhaps you don't know. That is an acceptable answer.

> “The tremor stops when I think about my answer and speak it.”

*Figure 1. Community experience.* One person reports a change during conversation; this does not predict another person’s response. [Original public source](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1nzuyuz/comment/ni918wf/). (*citations* [5](#citation-5))

**Something to think about:** What is one change to your environment or task that already makes life easier, even if it hasn't changed your symptoms?

**One small step:** Choose a low-risk accommodation, such as fewer distractions during a conversation or putting supplies within easy reach. If you want to explore movement-retraining strategies, ask a suitable rehabilitation clinician which approaches fit your presentation.

You have now learned three connected ideas: what attention selects, how familiar actions can run automatically, and why changing a task may change what happens. Next we'll explore how the brain prepares for events and interprets information from the body.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### Helpful isn't always more stimulation

Suppose fastening a button is difficult. Would a conversation help take the pressure off? Would it make things worse? Perhaps the person would rather work quietly, focus on where the button goes or stop altogether.

**Distraction**, focusing on an external goal and adding another task aren't the same thing. Extra instructions, music or counting can increase cognitive and sensory demands. There is no universal rule that “taking their mind off it” will help.

### A shared experiment should be gentle, reversible and optional

For an already safe activity, ask whether the person wants one adjustment: fewer observers, a clearer goal, a different position or simpler instructions. Afterward, let them tell you whether it improved ease, function or comfort.

Don't narrate every symptom change or tell the person that a symptom is “gone” because you noticed it shift. A change in context can be informative without revealing the cause of the symptom.

It's also fine if the person doesn't want to try anything. Sometimes the most useful adaptation is respecting a rest or a request for quiet.

### Hand off symptom-specific methods

For **functional tremor, gait changes or weakness**, a suitably trained physiotherapist may consider task focus or rhythm strategies. **OT** can adapt hand use and household tasks; speech-language therapists have their own approaches for communication symptoms. Technique selection belongs with the qualified professional when falls, pain, swallowing, breathing, episode safety or other risks are involved.

You may be invited to use a particular cue **after** the clinician and person agree that it is helpful. Ask what to say, when to stop and whether the person wants the cue at all. You do not become the therapist. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

**Something to discuss together:** “Would a cue help right now, or would fewer words be better?”

**One small thing:** Choose an already helpful environmental adjustment and agree when the supporter should offer it and when to leave things alone.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Use the correct term for the intervention

**Distractibility** is an examination observation when symptom expression changes while attention is engaged elsewhere. **Attention redirection** is an intentionally selected strategy; **external focus** refers to attending to an action's target or effect. **Entrainment** means the tremor adopts the rhythm of a requested competing movement. Merely stopping, pausing or suppressing a movement is not entrainment. Adding a dual task may increase risk or overload and should not be equated with simplifying instructions.

**Physiotherapy** can select a meaningful, safe task target or rhythm when its specific rationale is relevant. **OT** can compare instruction formats, environmental demands and the actual task outcome. **Psychology** can examine anticipation, fear of injury and stress-related burden where identified, while avoiding pressure to distract away from medical warning signs. Document the strategy, baseline context, patient preference, intended effect and any adverse responses.

**Symptom application — Functional tremor:** Specialist assessment may demonstrate distractibility or entrainment in an appropriate setting. Rehabilitation could trial a functional task or externally focused cue, documenting hand use, fatigue and interference rather than symptom suppression alone. [Tremor examination](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/02-functional-tremor.md) · [task-focused recovery](../../reference/recovery-techniques/functional_tremor/03-external-focus-and-meaningful-task-practice.md).

**Symptom application — Functional jerks/myoclonus:** Establish warning availability, falls, medication and other causes before discussing attention-based redirection. A no-warning jerk may require injury-prevention and environmental adaptations rather than a cue the person cannot use in time. [Jerks assessment](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/03-functional-jerks-and-myoclonus.md) · [jerks care](../../reference/recovery-techniques/03-functional-jerks-and-myoclonus.md).

**Symptom application — Functional tics and tic-like symptoms:** Clarify phenomenology, onset, motor/vocal features, possible premonitory sensations and differential diagnoses. Behavioural treatment principles derived from tic disorders must not be presented as proven equivalent for all functional tic-like presentations. Combine suitable care with dignity, school/work access and avoidance of punitive response training. [Tic assessment](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/17-functional-tics-and-tic-like-symptoms.md) · [tic recovery](../../reference/recovery-techniques/17-functional-tics-and-tic-like-symptoms.md).

**Symptom application — Functional dystonia/fixed posturing:** Painful or sustained posturing may not respond to distraction or simple rhythm. Prioritize complications, non-forceful positioning, task suitability and specialist coordination; do not force a fixed posture toward normal alignment. [Dystonia assessment](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/04-functional-dystonia.md) · [dystonia recovery](../../reference/recovery-techniques/04-functional-dystonia.md).

### What would make the trial worthwhile?

Use a negotiated outcome such as safer utensil use, reduced interference during a chosen activity, less pain or fewer injuries. Stop or modify an unsuitable cue. No response—or a response in the opposite direction—neither confirms nor refutes FND.

**Evidence boundary:** FND motor rehabilitation consensus and selected tremor experiments do not establish that distraction is a universal treatment or that a given manipulation demonstrates malingering. [1](#citation-1) · [2](#citation-2). (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))
