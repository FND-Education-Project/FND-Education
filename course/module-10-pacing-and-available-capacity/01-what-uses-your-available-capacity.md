## For the Person With FND

### How much can you find?

We've already learned about available capacity and the many things that can affect it. Now we're going to put that knowledge to work.

> **Available capacity:** What you can manage at a particular time, considering your symptoms, the demands of an activity, the support available and what recovery may involve. It is a useful way to understand daily life, not a measured amount of energy.

You'll read two stories about people we've met earlier in the course. Some of the clues are easy to spot. Others are hidden among ordinary details.

As you read, look for what makes an activity more demanding, what might make it easier, and which nervous-system processes could be involved.

**For example:** If someone is trying to read while a radio plays, the sound is an extra *demand*. *Selective attention* is the process that helps them concentrate on the words rather than the radio.

You'll find more than one process at work in our stories. See how many you recognize. Also consider what we **can't** know from the information we've been given.

Your own symptoms may make some of these decisions easier or more difficult. Notice how your experiences might change the choices you would make.

After each story, we'll look at what you might have discovered.

### Story 1 — Natalie's unexpectedly busy morning

Natalie has two things she wants to accomplish before lunch. She needs to speak with her pharmacy about a prescription, and she's expecting a grocery delivery.

She's arranged things so she can manage both. Her tablet is nearby, her prescription information is written on a note, and she's chosen a comfortable place to sit. The curtains are partly closed because her migraine is particularly troublesome today.

There's also a bowl of oatmeal on the small table beside her chair. She made it before sitting down.

**Your turn:** Natalie has a migraine, her breakfast is ready, and the groceries aren't expected until noon. Would you eat first or make the pharmacy call? What might influence your decision?

The groceries aren't due until noon, so she decides to call the pharmacy first.

Unfortunately, the pharmacy is busy.

Natalie spends several minutes listening to recorded messages before finally reaching someone. They begin discussing her prescription, and the pharmacist asks her to check some information on the label.

Natalie reaches for her note.

That's when the entrance buzzer sounds.

The groceries have arrived nearly two hours early.

"Oh, you've got to be kidding," Natalie mutters.

The pharmacist is still talking. Natalie tries to hold on to what they're asking while looking for the button that opens the entrance door.

The buzzer sounds again.

**Your turn again:** Natalie is trying to follow the pharmacist's instructions while someone waits at the entrance. What would you do first? What might become more difficult because of that choice?

Natalie explains that she needs a moment. The pharmacist agrees to call her back, and Natalie turns her attention to the delivery.

Getting to the entrance isn't always easy with her movement difficulties. Fortunately, the delivery arrangements allow her groceries to be left in an agreed accessible place.

By the time everything is settled, Natalie has lost track of which prescription details she was meant to check.

She looks at her note. It has the prescription name and the question she wanted answered, but she hadn't written down the pharmacist's latest question.

When the pharmacy calls back, she asks them to repeat it.

They do, and the conversation continues.

A little later, Natalie begins putting away her groceries. She manages the refrigerated items first and leaves the others for later.

As she sits down again, she notices how hungry she is.

Then she spots the untouched bowl of oatmeal beside her tablet.

Breakfast!

She'd intended to eat before getting caught up in everything, but somehow that never happened.

Her phone announces a new message from the pharmacy confirming the next step for her prescription. She's relieved to have it in writing.

Natalie looks around her apartment.

The groceries are only partly unpacked, and the towels she meant to put away are still sitting in a basket. She's spent most of the morning dealing with what should have been two ordinary tasks.

And she's exhausted.

She sends Leah a message.

"Groceries arrived early. Pharmacy called twice. I think the door buzzer and I are no longer speaking."

Leah replies with a laughing face.

Natalie smiles, puts down her phone and decides the towels can wait.

### Answer key — What did you notice?

**The interruption changed the demands of the morning.**

Natalie had arranged to handle the pharmacy call and the grocery delivery separately. When the groceries arrived early, she suddenly had to deal with both.

She was trying to remember the pharmacist's question, respond to the buzzer and decide what to do first.

> **Working memory:** Holding and using information for a short time, such as remembering a question while preparing an answer.

> **Executive functions:** The abilities that help us start, organize, plan, switch between and complete activities.

Both processes may help explain why the interruption made the morning more demanding.

Natalie had to change her plan, complete another task and then return to the unfinished conversation. The extra work wasn't simply answering the door. It also involved finding her place again afterward.

**The buzzer competed with the conversation.**

Natalie's migraine was already making the morning uncomfortable. Then the unexpected buzzer demanded her attention while she was trying to listen to the pharmacist.

> **Salience:** The importance the nervous system gives to information, helping decide what deserves attention.

The buzzer was important because someone was waiting at the entrance. Natalie needed to respond, even though she was already concentrating on something else.

**Did you notice what helped?**

Natalie's prescription note meant she didn't have to remember everything herself. The grocery delivery arrangements reduced the movement required to receive her shopping.

Later, the pharmacy's written confirmation gave her something she could refer to without having to remember the whole conversation.

These arrangements helped her complete her tasks. They didn't require her symptoms to disappear.

**And what about breakfast?**

Did you remember the oatmeal sitting beside Natalie's chair?

She didn't eat it, and only noticed how hungry she was once the interruptions had settled.

> **Interoception:** Sensing and interpreting conditions inside the body, such as hunger, thirst, heartbeat and breathing. Detecting a signal and consciously noticing it aren't always the same thing.

We can't know whether Natalie had difficulty sensing hunger. She might simply have been concentrating on everything else.

But it raises an interesting question: how much attention was available for noticing her own needs while she was dealing with the demands around her?

**One thing we can't know**

Natalie's morning was exhausting, but we can't determine how much each part contributed. We also can't know whether the activity caused any particular FND symptom to worsen.

Her planning was sensible. Something unexpected happened.

The important discovery is that two familiar activities can become much harder when their timing and circumstances change.

### Story 2 — Jamie's photo project

Jamie and Alex have been working on a family photo album. They have plenty of old photographs, and Jamie's brother has helped them make digital copies.

They've arranged to spend an afternoon choosing photographs and writing a few short captions. They hope to send their first selection to the print shop before four o'clock.

At first, things go rather well.

Jamie finds a photograph of his niece wearing enormous rubber boots in the middle of summer.

"She insisted they were her dancing shoes," he tells Alex.

Alex laughs.

They find several more photographs, and Jamie begins sorting them into groups. He particularly enjoys deciding which ones belong together.

Then Alex notices the time.

The photographs need to be sent before the print shop's deadline.

**Your turn:** Jamie is enjoying the photographs, but there's still work to finish before the deadline. If you were Jamie, how would you decide what to do next? What might make that decision difficult?

"Have you chosen the eight we're using?" she asks. "And did you tell your brother we're getting them printed? We also need to finish the captions."

Jamie looks toward the photographs.

He knows he was about to do something, but the next step has disappeared.

"Hang on. Which one are we doing?"

Alex repeats the questions, trying to help him remember.

At the same time, her phone announces a message, and the dishwasher begins its noisy drying cycle.

Jamie looks at a photograph and tries to remember when it was taken. He knows it was during a family trip, but the year won't come to him.

Alex suggests a date.

"No, that doesn't sound right."

She offers another possibility.

Jamie becomes frustrated. A few moments ago, he was having a wonderful time remembering old stories. Now he's struggling to give a simple answer.

"Can we just finish one thing?" he asks, more sharply than he intended.

Alex looks surprised.

"I'm trying to help. We've got a deadline."

"I know," Jamie says. "But I can't keep all those questions in my head."

**Your turn again:** Jamie has lost his place, Alex is worried about the deadline, and the room has become noisy. What could they change to make the activity easier? Would you change the surroundings, the task, or both?

For a moment, neither says anything.

Then Alex stops the dishwasher and sets her phone aside.

"All right. Let's choose the photographs first."

They return to the pictures.

Jamie finds one showing his niece standing beside a collapsed picnic table. He begins laughing before Alex even sees it.

"Remember that?"

Alex does.

The story comes back to Jamie, including a detail neither of them had mentioned earlier. He tells Alex what happened, and they agree it deserves a place in the collection.

They save the photograph and write a short caption.

A few minutes later, Jamie remembers that they still haven't told his brother about the printing.

This time, he makes a note so they can deal with it after the photographs are finished.

Before the deadline, they've selected six photographs rather than eight.

Alex looks at the unfinished pile.

"I suppose six will do?"

"Six excellent photographs," Jamie replies. "We offer quality, not quantity."

They send them to the print shop.

The family album is coming together.

But Jamie's pleased with something else, too. For much of the afternoon, he was able to enjoy an activity that had become difficult for a while.

### Answer key — What did you find?

**The task changed before Jamie's abilities appeared to change.**

At first, Jamie was comfortably sorting photographs and telling familiar stories.

Then he was asked to choose photographs, remember another arrangement, answer several questions and think about a deadline. The phone and dishwasher added further demands.

His working memory and attention now had more to manage.

The photographs themselves hadn't become harder to understand. But the activity around them had become more complicated.

**His memory was doing something interesting.**

Jamie could spontaneously recall amusing family stories, but he struggled when asked to remember a particular date.

> **Retrieval:** Bringing information we've learned back into awareness when we need it.

The photographs sometimes helped Jamie remember. At other times, the information remained difficult to reach.

We can't know exactly why the date was unavailable. But we've learned before that struggling to retrieve something doesn't necessarily mean all knowledge of it has disappeared.

**Did you notice when the frustration began?**

Jamie was enjoying himself before the questions started piling up. His frustration followed the difficulties with remembering and organizing his answers.

> **Feedback loop:** When two or more things influence one another over time, sometimes making a difficulty greater and sometimes helping it settle.

His thinking difficulties may have contributed to frustration. That frustration could then have made the activity feel even more demanding.

We can't establish the exact relationship. But the story gives us a reason to consider how these experiences may affect one another.

**Alex was managing demands of her own.**

She had a deadline to meet and several things still needed doing. Her questions made sense from her point of view, even though asking them together wasn't helping Jamie.

When Alex reduced the competing demands, they could return to the photographs.

They also changed their goal. Six photographs were enough for this afternoon.

Notice that neither person needed to be wrong for the original plan to become difficult.

**What actually improved?**

Jamie's memory difficulties hadn't disappeared. He still couldn't recall every detail, and there was still a deadline.

But the quieter surroundings, clearer task and written reminder helped him continue.

He could enjoy the photographs again without having to keep several unrelated jobs in mind.

Sometimes, changing how an activity is arranged makes it more manageable even when the person's symptoms remain.

---

### Bringing the stories together

Natalie and Jamie weren't attempting anything unusually difficult. Their problems began when familiar activities were interrupted or became more complicated than expected. Both benefited from arrangements that reduced some of those demands, even though their symptoms remained. **Understanding available capacity means looking beyond the activity itself and noticing everything else the situation asks of us.** That's something we can learn to recognize, even when we cannot predict or prevent every difficulty.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### The household rule nobody could keep

Owen and Maya had an agreement: they were never supposed to be sick at the same time.

It was an excellent rule, except neither of them had any idea how to enforce it.

On Tuesday morning, Maya woke with a miserable cold. Her throat hurt, her head ached, and she wanted nothing more than to curl up on the sofa beneath a blanket.

"I think the rule's been broken," Owen announced.

Maya gave him a sleepy smile. "You'll have to file a complaint."

Owen wanted to look after her. Maya usually handled many of the household jobs when his FND symptoms were troublesome, and he was glad to have an opportunity to return some of that care.

He made breakfast, dealt with the dishes and took their little terrier, Pip, outside. Pip was delighted with the unexpected attention and thoroughly investigated every interesting smell along the path.

Back inside, Owen remembered that Maya needed something from the pharmacy. He checked what they already had, made a list and arranged to collect a few things. He also prepared lunch, answered a telephone call and returned to the kitchen to finish cleaning up.

None of the jobs was particularly unusual. Owen had done them all before.

But as the afternoon progressed, he noticed that his walking was becoming less reliable. His migraine was worsening, and he kept forgetting what he'd gone into a room to do.

Maya asked whether he wanted to leave the remaining jobs.

"You're sick," Owen replied. "I can manage a few more things."

She appreciated his kindness, although she was beginning to worry about how tired he looked.

That evening, Owen experienced one of his familiar functional seizures. Maya followed their agreed safety plan and remained with him until he recovered sufficiently.

The following morning, Maya was still sick. Owen had a severe migraine, little energy and considerable difficulty moving around.

Pip stood beside his empty breakfast bowl, looking deeply disappointed in both of them.

"Apparently," Maya said, "he didn't read the memo."

They had very little capacity between them, but Pip still needed feeding and a walk. There were also dishes in the kitchen and a growing list of things they'd hoped to accomplish.

This time, they approached the day differently.

Maya arranged a simple grocery delivery. Owen fed Pip while seated and left the dishes for later. A friend agreed to take the dog for a short walk.

With the immediate needs covered, they spent much of the afternoon resting together. Pip eventually curled up between them, apparently satisfied that the household had returned to its proper priorities.

### What does the evidence tell us?

Occupational therapists working with people with FND look beyond whether someone can complete a particular task. They also consider everyday routines, surroundings and the help available. All of these can affect what a person manages. ([1](#citation-1))

Supporters are part of that picture, too. Their experiences show that caring responsibilities can affect their own health and wellbeing, especially when a household is already managing illness and disability. ([Australian FND carer report](https://www.mentalhealthcommission.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-04/experiences-of-functional-neurological-disorder---summary-report.pdf))

### Better recognition of shared demands

Owen didn't suddenly become responsible for more because his FND had changed. Maya became ill, and responsibilities that were normally shared differently shifted toward him.

Each task had its own demands. Together, they required more walking, planning, remembering, physical activity and attention than he ordinarily managed. His concern for Maya was also part of the experience.

We cannot establish that these additional activities caused his seizure or the following day's difficulties. But the events give Owen and Maya a reason to reconsider how they handle a day when their usual arrangements aren't possible.

The important change wasn't that Owen stopped wanting to help. It was that they recognized the household needed a different plan when both were struggling.

**Better recognition of shared demands means noticing when responsibilities have shifted, considering what each person can realistically contribute and finding alternatives when their combined abilities aren't enough.**

A household doesn't fail because the dishes wait until tomorrow. Sometimes taking care of each other means agreeing that tomorrow can handle the dishes.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### What would you decide?

Earlier lessons explored how attention, memory, sensory information and automatic movement can influence functioning. Now we'll bring those ideas together to examine more complicated clinical situations.

You'll follow two patients through ordinary activities. Each story contains observations that could influence assessment or care.

**For example:** Suppose a patient completes a familiar activity in a quiet room but repeatedly loses their place when other people interrupt. You could examine what changes during interruptions, arrange a more predictable sequence and compare whether the person completes the activity more reliably. You haven't established the neurological cause of the difficulty, but you've identified a useful question and a way to investigate it.

At decision points in each story, consider the information available, what you would do next, and which findings would support changing your approach.

### Case 1 — The walk was successful. Was the appointment?

Morgan attends physiotherapy for an established functional gait disorder. They use an agreed mobility aid to travel the short distance from reception to the treatment room.

On Monday, the physiotherapist is running behind schedule. Morgan waits standing near reception for about ten minutes before a staff member arrives to collect them.

As they walk toward the treatment room, the staff member explains that Morgan's next appointment has been moved to a different time and that another clinician will be attending.

Morgan listens while navigating the corridor. They slow near a doorway, adjust their grip on the mobility aid and continue. The staff member notices no need for additional physical assistance.

When they reach the treatment room, Morgan remembers that the appointment time has changed but cannot recall which clinician will be present.

"Walking was all right," Morgan tells the physiotherapist. "I'm having trouble remembering what I was just told."

The staff member confirms that Morgan completed the route using their usual aid.

**Your turn — First clinical decision**

You're reviewing how the clinic manages Morgan's arrival.

Would you continue providing appointment information during the walk, move that conversation to reception before walking, or make another arrangement?

**Choose an approach and identify two outcomes you would record** to determine whether it works better for Morgan.

#### New information

At the next appointment, Morgan travels the same route.

This time, a staff member explains two comparable appointment details while Morgan is seated. Morgan repeats both correctly before leaving reception.

The staff member then accompanies Morgan to the treatment room. Morgan completes the walk with the usual mobility aid and without additional hands-on assistance.

During the session, the physiotherapist mentions that Morgan seemed to remember the information more easily this time.

"Yes," Morgan replies. "It was easier when I wasn't walking."

After a moment, they add something else.

"Last time, when I was standing around waiting, my vision went dim for a few seconds. It cleared up afterward."

The physiotherapist asks whether it has happened before. Morgan isn't sure.

There were other differences between the visits. The waiting room was quieter on Thursday, a different staff member accompanied Morgan, and nobody recorded precisely how long Morgan stood before walking.

**Your turn — Second clinical decision**

You now have observations from both visits.

Would you keep the revised check-in arrangement? What would you compare at the next appointment to find out whether it provides a reliable benefit?

Morgan has also reported brief visual dimming while standing. **What separate assessment or referral would you consider, and why?**

### Answer key — What, Why and How?

#### What did the observations establish?

Morgan completed the walk on both visits with the usual mobility aid and without additional physical assistance. However, the communication outcomes differed.

On Monday, Morgan recalled only one of two appointment details. On Thursday, after receiving comparable information while seated, they recalled both.

This gives the clinician two useful observations: Morgan's mobility remained sufficient for the route under the conditions described, while their ability to recall information appeared better when communication was separated from walking.

The visual dimming provides another important finding that should be assessed on its own merits.

#### Why might the difference matter?

Monday's journey combined several demands. Morgan had spent time standing, then needed to navigate the corridor, manage their mobility aid, listen to unfamiliar information and remember it. Thursday's arrangement separated the communication task from the walking task.

> **Dual-task demands:** The demands involved when someone attempts to perform two activities at the same time, such as walking while following a conversation.

Research into functional gait disorder has examined how concurrent tasks affect movement under different conditions. Sandri and colleagues found differences in selected gait measures during particular dual-task conditions. These findings support looking carefully at how tasks interact rather than evaluating walking in isolation. ([6](#citation-6))

But Morgan's case offers another useful lesson. The outcome that changed most clearly was not the ability to complete the route. It was the ability to remember information.

The clinician therefore has a practical reason to examine the order in which information and movement are required.

We should also consider the visual dimming. Symptoms occurring during standing may suggest a posture-related medical problem, including possible changes in blood-pressure regulation. The episode deserves appropriate clinical history and assessment rather than being absorbed into Morgan's existing FND diagnosis. ([8](#citation-8))

The two visits differed in several ways, so the observations do not identify one certain cause for the improvement. That doesn't erase their value. They provide a reasonable basis for examining whether a different arrangement consistently helps Morgan.

#### How could the findings change care?

The team could retain the seated explanation before walking, provided Morgan finds it helpful and the arrangement remains practical.

At subsequent appointments, staff could briefly confirm that Morgan understands the essential information before leaving reception. The team could also observe whether Morgan completes the route safely using the agreed assistance.

The comparison would consider **two outcomes together: safe mobility and successful communication**. It would also be useful to note whether busy surroundings, longer waiting periods or unfamiliar staff affect the result.

For the visual dimming, the clinician should clarify its frequency, associated symptoms and relationship to standing, with appropriate medical assessment or referral. There is no need to deliberately recreate the episode to investigate the communication problem.

### Case 1 — Concluding reflection

#### What have we learned?

Morgan completed the walk on both visits, but the two arrangements produced different communication outcomes. Separating the conversation from walking appeared to help Morgan remember important information without preventing them from completing the route.

The team now has something useful to evaluate: whether providing instructions while seated consistently supports both communication and safe mobility. Morgan's reported visual dimming also deserves medical assessment, independently of the improvement in recall.

**The important discovery is that successful performance of one task doesn't establish successful performance of everything happening alongside it.** Recognizing the different demands allows clinicians to make and evaluate changes that improve access to care.

### Before the next case

Morgan's difficulties were relatively straightforward to observe, and changing the order of two activities offered a promising solution.

Our next patient experiences a much greater change in his ability to participate.

**What happens when the clinical environment—and the examination itself—adds demands that the patient cannot communicate or respond to?**

### Case 2 — The consultation that changed course

David attends a neurology appointment with his wife. He has a history of functional seizures, chronic migraine and significant sensitivity to light and sound. The appointment is intended to address his migraine, which has affected him continuously for several years.

They spend at least fifteen minutes in a brightly lit waiting room where several conversations are taking place. David wears specialized tinted glasses, earplugs and a wide-brimmed hat that shades his eyes. These are accommodations he regularly uses when leaving home.

When they're called into the consultation room, David adjusts his earplugs so he can follow the conversation.

The neurologist begins discussing his migraine and FND history. She acknowledges the name *functional neurological disorder* but describes his functional seizures as psychological rather than neurological, using language associated with the older diagnosis of conversion disorder.

David and his wife continue discussing his symptoms.

**Your turn — First clinical decision**

You're conducting David's consultation.

Given his migraine, sensory sensitivities and established functional seizures, **what would you change, if anything, about the environment or how you conduct the appointment?**

What would you want to know about his usual seizure pattern and support needs before proceeding?

#### An unexpected interruption

During the conversation, the neurologist walks to a printer, raises its lid and lets it fall shut. The closing lid makes a loud noise.

Immediately afterward, David's head turns to the right and his eyes close. He slumps to one side, his arms begin showing involuntary posturing, and he stops responding verbally.

His wife tells the neurologist that this resembles his usual functional seizures and they can last an hour or more.

The episode continues. David experiences intermittent abnormal posturing, approximately thirty seconds of generalized shaking, and repeated arching of his back and neck. The neurologist examines his eyes, noting upward movement behind his closed eyelids.

**Your turn — Second clinical decision**

David's wife recognizes the event as familiar, but he remains unable to communicate.

**What immediate assessment and care would you provide?** How would you use the spouse's account when deciding whether further investigation or urgent intervention was necessary?

#### The demands of care

After some time, David remains unable to respond or move purposefully.

The neurologist asks his wife to retrieve his wheelchair from their car, which is parked on the street outside. His wife leaves the consultation room.

While she is away, the neurologist continues examining David. She lifts one of his arms and releases it toward his face, applies firm pressure with her knuckles against his sternum, and examines his eyes again.

David does not respond verbally or make a purposeful movement.

The neurologist records a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 3/15 (E1, V1, M1), indicating no observable response during the assessment.

The neurologist completes these maneuvers before his wife returns. She then continues working at her computer while David remains in the room.

Gradually, over the next hour, David regains some voluntary movement, although he is still drowsy and unable to communicate normally. His wife helps him transfer into the wheelchair and takes him from the office.

The migraine consultation does not resume.

**Your turn — Third clinical decision**

Consider the examination maneuvers performed while David was unresponsive.

**What clinical information could each maneuver provide, and how would you determine whether that information justified the procedure?**

How would you assess the decision to have David's wife leave, the support available during his recovery, and his condition before departure?

#### Information learned afterward

David later explains that he remained aware of portions of the episode despite being unable to move or respond.

He remembers his wife leaving the room. He knew the neurologist was examining him but could not communicate what he was experiencing.

The pressure against his sternum was extremely painful, and the pain continued afterward. He also experienced considerable anxiety while his wife was away, knowing that he could neither move nor ask for her to return.

David describes another part of the appointment that had been distressing. Hearing the neurologist characterize his FND as psychological made him feel that his neurological condition was not being taken seriously. He had come seeking help for migraine and found himself struggling with how the neurologist understood his existing diagnosis.

His wife confirms that David had not recovered to his usual level of functioning when she transferred him into the wheelchair.

The neurologist's correspondence describes a prolonged event involving head deviation, eye closure, abnormal arm posturing, generalized shaking, repeated arching of the back and neck, and reduced responsiveness lasting close to an hour. It also records the wife's statement that the event was typical of David's previously recognized functional seizures.

David left without completing the migraine assessment.

**Your turn — Final clinical assessment**

You now have David's later account alongside the examination record and his wife's observations.

**Does this information change how you evaluate the examination, recovery arrangements or decisions made during the appointment?**

Consider the information obtained, the patient's experience of pain and anxiety, and whether alternatives were available.

Finally, what would you arrange to address the migraine concern that brought David to the consultation?

### Clinical reflection — What does good practice tell us?

Rather than judging each action for the reader, we can examine the clinical principles that help evaluate the decisions.

#### Recognizing and assessing functional seizures

The American Academy of Neurology's functional-seizure guideline emphasizes obtaining a careful history, considering the characteristics of observed events, and using information from patients and witnesses when identifying functional seizures. ([5](#citation-5))

David's established diagnosis and his wife's familiarity with his episodes are clinically useful. They help establish whether the observed event resembles his usual pattern and inform decisions about assessment and immediate care.

The guideline also supports appropriate evaluation when a person has distinct event types or possible coexisting epilepsy. A familiar diagnosis informs clinical judgment without making further assessment unnecessary when circumstances warrant it.

#### Available capacity includes the demands of care

FND occupational-therapy consensus encourages clinicians to examine the activities, environments and circumstances affecting everyday functioning. ([1](#citation-1))

David's appointment illustrates why this matters in healthcare itself.

He entered a bright, noisy environment with significant sensory sensitivities and several established accommodations. During the consultation, he also encountered an explanation of his diagnosis that he later described as distressing.

The sudden noise immediately preceded his functional seizure. The timing is relevant to understanding the sequence, although it doesn't establish that the noise alone caused the episode.

Once David became unable to respond, the demands did not stop. Examination, touch, movement, painful stimulation and separation from his wife became part of what he experienced.

**A clinician's actions can add to the demands a patient is already managing, even when the patient cannot demonstrate those effects through speech or movement.**

David's later report of pain and anxiety provides important information about the consequences of those actions.

#### Examination, awareness and clinical judgment

Apparent unresponsiveness does not necessarily establish an absence of awareness or sensation.

This distinction is particularly important when considering examination maneuvers intended to assess responsiveness.

Their value depends on the clinical question, the information they can reliably provide, and whether that information is needed to guide care. Clinicians must also consider the potential pain, distress or injury associated with the examination and whether less burdensome alternatives are available.

David's later account changes what is known about the examination's effects. The absence of a visible response did not mean he experienced no pain or distress.

These considerations support evaluating the necessity and proportionality of clinical procedures without requiring the reader to reach a predetermined judgment about the fictional neurologist.

#### Recovery and continuity of care

Individualized functional-seizure care includes attention to the person's usual recovery pattern, safety, communication needs and appropriate assistance after an event. New or concerning findings require assessment according to the clinical circumstances. ([5](#citation-5))

A person's ability to be transferred into a wheelchair is not, by itself, a complete assessment of recovery.

David's original reason for attending also remains important. Even when an acute event prevents a consultation from continuing, the underlying medical concern still requires an appropriate plan.

**Managing the seizure and completing the patient's medical assessment are distinct responsibilities.**

### Returning to your decisions

Consider whether the additional information changed your clinical judgments.

You began with a patient attending for migraine assessment who had known sensory sensitivities and functional seizures. You subsequently learned about a prolonged episode, examination procedures, incomplete recovery, retained awareness, pain and anxiety.

Some findings were visible during the consultation. Others only became available when David could communicate again.

The purpose of this exercise is not to identify a perfect response to every event. It is to consider **what each clinical decision contributed, what demands it imposed, and whether the patient's healthcare needs were addressed**.

---

### Bringing the cases together

Morgan and David experienced very different clinical encounters, but both cases demonstrate that healthcare activities place demands on a patient's available capacity. Morgan completed the walk while struggling to retain information; David became unable to participate during a consultation, yet continued experiencing the demands of examination and recovery.

In both cases, **the way care was delivered influenced what the patient could manage and whether their needs were met**. Recognizing these interactions allows clinicians to evaluate their own decisions, reduce unnecessary demands and support meaningful access to healthcare.
