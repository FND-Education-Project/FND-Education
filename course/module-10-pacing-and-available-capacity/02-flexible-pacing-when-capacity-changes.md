## For the Person With FND

### What would you decide?

In our last lesson, we learned to spot demands that might be hiding in ordinary activities. This time, we're going to make things a little harder.

Our characters must make decisions **before they know how things will turn out**.

> **Pacing:** Adjusting activities and opportunities for recovery to suit what you can realistically manage. Pacing can change as your circumstances and abilities change.

As you follow each story, look for what the character knows when making a choice. What might they have overlooked? What could they reasonably predict?

And when something unexpected happens, does that mean their earlier decision was wrong?

Remember, we're looking for clues, not someone to blame.

Your own symptoms may make some of these decisions easier or more difficult. Notice how your experiences might change the choices you would make.

### Story 1 — Priya's afternoon out

Priya has been missing her friends. She still remembers seeing a photograph of them together, with an empty chair where she would usually sit.

So when a friend invites her to meet for coffee at the library café, she wants to say yes.

It's been a difficult week. Her movement hasn't been very reliable, and she's spent more time at home than she'd like.

Last night wasn't particularly restful either. Noise from another apartment kept waking her, and she eventually got up feeling as though she hadn't slept enough.

But this morning is different. Her movement is more reliable than it has been all week, and she gets ready without needing as many breaks. Her headache is still bothering her a little, but she feels better than she expected.

She's already planned to finish some laundry and sort through a pile of mail. Neither is urgent.

Priya looks at her friend's invitation again. She's missed them, and the idea of sitting together over coffee makes her smile.

**Your turn:** You know how Priya has been feeling, how she slept, what she'd planned for today and how much she wants to see her friends. Would you accept the invitation? What would influence your decision?

Priya decides to go.

She leaves the laundry and mail for another day, arranges a ride that will drop her near the library entrance, and asks her friend whether they can sit somewhere quiet.

Before leaving, she prepares something easy for dinner. Even if the afternoon goes well, she doesn't particularly want to cook afterward.

At the café, her friends welcome her warmly. They order drinks and begin catching up.

For the first twenty minutes, Priya is having a lovely time. She's laughing at a story about an unfortunate haircut when a large group arrives at the next table.

The café gets noisier. Priya starts missing parts of the conversation, and she shifts in her chair several times, trying to get comfortable.

One friend notices and suggests moving to a quieter table near the entrance.

That helps. Priya can follow the conversation again, although she's beginning to feel tired. Her legs also feel less steady when she gets up to move.

A little later, the others begin talking about visiting a nearby bookshop.

"Come with us," one says. "We haven't seen you in ages!"

Priya loves browsing books, and she's enjoying being with everyone. But she'd arranged her ride home for shortly after coffee, and the bookshop would mean more walking and a change of plans.

**Your turn again:** Priya is happy to be with her friends, but her movement has become less reliable and she's growing tired. The quieter table helped, and she hasn't seen her friends in ages. Would you go to the bookshop, or would you head home? What could make either choice reasonable?

Priya considers the invitation for a moment.

"I think I'll save the bookshop for another day. I've had a really nice afternoon, though."

Her friend smiles. "Then we should do this again."

When Priya gets home, she puts her things down, eats the dinner she prepared and spends the evening quietly. She's tired but pleased with herself.

The next morning is different.

Her movement is less reliable, and she's having difficulty concentrating. Even deciding what to make for breakfast seems to take longer than usual.

She looks at the laundry she still hasn't finished.

"Well, that's helpful," she mutters.

Priya remembers feeling better yesterday and wonders whether going out was a mistake.

But she's had similar outings without feeling worse afterward. She's also had difficult mornings when she barely left the house.

She can't tell what made today different.

Her phone lights up with a photograph from the café. Everyone is smiling, including her.

She looks at it for a while, then replies.

"That was lovely. We should do it again."

### Answer key — What did you discover?

**Was accepting the invitation a reasonable choice?**

Priya knew she'd had a difficult week and a poor night's sleep. She also knew she was moving more reliably that morning and wanted to see her friends.

She couldn't know how the afternoon would turn out.

Notice what she arranged before leaving. She postponed the laundry, organized transport, requested a quieter table and prepared dinner.

Those decisions gave her options and reduced some demands. But they couldn't guarantee a particular outcome.

**What changed at the café?**

Priya was enjoying herself until the room became noisier. She had more difficulty following the conversation, and her movement became less reliable.

Moving tables helped with the noise, but it didn't make her feel completely better.

> **Salience:** The importance the nervous system gives to information, helping decide what deserves attention.

> **Selective attention:** Concentrating on particular information while other things compete for our attention.

The additional conversations around Priya may have made it harder to concentrate on her friends. We can't tell exactly how much the noise contributed to her difficulties, but we can recognize the competing demands.

**What about the bookshop?**

Priya had already enjoyed some time with her friends, but the additional outing meant walking farther, changing her transport arrangements and continuing while she was growing tired.

She chose to go home.

Another person might reasonably have made a different choice. Even Priya might choose differently on another day.

Pacing involves considering what's important alongside what seems manageable. It isn't a rule that tells everyone to make the same decision.

**Did the difficult morning mean she made a mistake?**

Not necessarily.

Priya knew she'd slept poorly before the outing. She also knew her symptoms could vary without an obvious explanation.

We can't determine whether the outing contributed to the following day's difficulties. Nor can we establish that staying home would have prevented them.

That's a difficult part of pacing: sometimes you make a thoughtful choice and still have a difficult day.

**And what about her feelings?**

Priya enjoyed the café visit but was disappointed by how she felt the next morning.

> **Emotional regulation:** How we notice, understand and respond to feelings as they change.

Both feelings belonged to the same experience. She didn't need to decide the outing was entirely successful or entirely a mistake.

And did you remember the empty chair from the beginning?

Priya wasn't simply deciding how to spend her energy. She was deciding whether to take an opportunity to be part of something she had been missing.

That matters when we consider what a good pacing decision might look like.

### Story 2 — Daniel's garden

Daniel loves his garden. He's been working on making it easier to enjoy despite his functional walking difficulties. He has a sturdy chair, raised planters and a place to keep the tools he uses most often.

One Thursday morning, he spends about fifteen minutes trimming herbs and checking his plants. He works mostly while seated and doesn't notice anything particularly unusual afterward. He's pleased with how much he managed.

At his next physiotherapy appointment, Daniel mentions that he'd worked in the garden without much difficulty.

"That's encouraging," his physiotherapist says.

**Your turn:** Daniel managed fifteen minutes of gardening and felt reasonably comfortable at the time. Would you consider spending longer in the garden next time? Is there anything else you'd want to know before deciding?

They discuss whether he might eventually spend a little longer doing the activities he enjoys. Daniel likes that idea. He's hoping to grow more vegetables next season.

But there's something he hasn't mentioned.

The morning after gardening, he'd woken feeling much worse. His whole body felt unusually heavy, he had a headache, and even concentrating on a television programme seemed difficult.

The worsening lasted several days.

At the time, Daniel assumed he'd simply had another difficult spell. He hadn't thought much about whether the gardening and the following days were connected.

A few weeks later, something similar happens after an afternoon spent helping a neighbour with a computer problem.

Daniel mostly sits during the visit. He's pleased to help, although working through the problem requires quite a lot of concentration. He feels reasonably comfortable when his neighbour leaves.

The following day, however, he's unwell again. His exhaustion feels much greater than he'd expect from the activity, and it takes several days before he's back to his usual level.

**Your turn again:** Daniel has now felt unusually unwell after two very different activities. Both times, the difficulties appeared later and lasted several days. What connections can you find? Would this change your earlier decision about spending longer in the garden?

Daniel begins wondering whether the timing matters.

He remembers another occasion when he felt unusually unwell after a short trip outside, although he isn't certain whether it was the same experience.

He thinks back to the physiotherapy appointment.

When he'd said gardening went well, he was thinking about what happened **while he was gardening**. He hadn't connected the difficult days afterward with that activity.

He writes a few notes about the episodes, including when he was active, when the worsening began and how long it lasted.

At his next medical appointment, Daniel mentions the pattern.

His doctor asks more questions. Had it happened before? Was the worsening immediate or delayed? Had anything else changed with his health? How long did it take to recover?

They agree that the pattern needs further assessment rather than assuming it is simply part of his FND.

Daniel also raises it with his physiotherapist before making any increase to his activity plan.

At home, he looks out at the planters. Some of the herbs need watering, and he's frustrated that something he loves has become so complicated.

His neighbour has offered to help with the watering for now, and Daniel accepts.

He still wants to enjoy his garden. He's simply realized there may be something important about his recovery that he doesn't yet understand.

### Answer key — What did you notice?

**The most important clue appeared after the activity.**

Daniel's gardening seemed manageable while he was doing it. That was the information he shared with his physiotherapist.

But the next morning, he experienced much greater difficulties, and they lasted for several days.

The same pattern appeared after helping his neighbour.

Being able to complete an activity doesn't necessarily tell us how someone will feel afterward.

**Did you notice that the activities were different?**

Gardening involved movement, even though Daniel was seated for much of it. Helping with a computer problem involved concentration and problem-solving.

Yet he noticed a somewhat similar pattern after both.

That's a useful clue because different activities involve different kinds of effort.

It doesn't establish that either activity caused his symptoms. But it gives his clinician something more specific to investigate than the statement, "Sometimes I get exhausted."

**Could this be something other than ordinary fatigue?**

Possibly.

> **Post-exertional malaise:** A worsening of symptoms following physical, mental or other exertion that is disproportionate to the activity. It may be delayed and can last for days or longer. It is a characteristic feature of conditions such as myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS).

Not every difficult day following activity is post-exertional malaise. Daniel's story doesn't tell us whether he has this condition or why his symptoms worsened.

But the repeated, delayed and prolonged worsening is a reason for appropriate medical assessment. FND does not explain every symptom a person experiences.

And if a condition involving post-exertional malaise is present, activity recommendations may need to be quite different from those used in some rehabilitation programmes.

**What was missing from the physiotherapy discussion?**

When Daniel reported that gardening went well, he was describing his performance during the activity.

His physiotherapist didn't yet have the information about the days afterward.

This matters because a treatment or activity plan may look manageable during a session but have effects that only become apparent later.

Remember what we learned about neuroplasticity and rehabilitation in Module 9: practice can support learning, but **more practice is not automatically better for every person or every health condition**.

Understanding what happens afterward can be important when deciding whether a plan should change.

**Was Daniel's earlier plan a mistake?**

We don't have enough information to say that.

He had chosen adaptations for his garden, worked for a limited time and was trying to pursue something meaningful.

The later pattern gave him information he hadn't previously recognized. He brought that information to his clinicians rather than assuming that he should simply increase activity or give up gardening.

**Did you spot the small change at the end?**

Daniel accepted his neighbour's offer to help water the plants.

That didn't settle the medical question. It also didn't mean he'd abandoned his goal.

It allowed him to keep his garden while he and his healthcare team worked on understanding the delayed worsening.

Sometimes a useful pacing decision isn't about choosing the perfect amount of activity. It's about recognizing when new information means a previous plan deserves another look.

---

### Bringing the stories together

Priya made decisions as her circumstances changed. Daniel discovered that he needed more information before deciding what to do next. Neither could know everything in advance. **Flexible pacing means making reasonable choices with what we know, then reconsidering them when we learn something new.** A difficult day doesn't always mean we made a poor decision, and a successful activity doesn't always tell us the whole story.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### When a better day is not a blank cheque

After several difficult days, the person you care about feels well enough to catch up. They may want to visit a friend, do laundry, shop and finally enjoy a favourite activity.

You may worry that it will be too much. Yet telling them “You're not allowed” can make you feel like their supervisor and them feel like a child.

### Plan together instead of policing

**Pacing** involves adjusting activities and recovery around what is safe and sustainable. Ask the person what matters today and which demand could be moved, shortened, shared or done differently. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

You may be able to suggest, “Would you rather I handle the shopping so there's room for your visit?” Offer a choice, not a verdict.

Sometimes a flare happens despite careful planning. Do not decide afterward that the person “brought it on themselves.” A pattern of worsening may be influenced by several conditions and may not be preventable.

### A very important professional boundary

If activity causes **delayed, disproportionate or prolonged worsening**, ask the clinical team to consider post-exertional malaise and relevant conditions such as ME/CFS. Do not impose a standard weekly exercise increase, and do not treat a good morning as medical clearance for greater exertion. **PT, OT and the clinician managing any coexisting illness** need to coordinate the plan. ME/CFS guidance is condition-specific and should not be applied indiscriminately as an FND diagnosis either.

Supporters shouldn't be the ones measuring, provoking or enforcing exertion. It is reasonable to request professional guidance when practical activity decisions have become unsafe, confusing or a source of conflict.

> “I’m her carer and we are fortunate to be able to plan our days around how she feels although she is often bedridden.”

**Something to discuss together:** “If tomorrow is difficult, which part of the plan could change without losing what's important to you?”

**One small thing:** Agree on one flexible option for a demanding day, including what assistance you can realistically offer.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### Establish the time course before prescribing progression

A boom-and-bust description may identify alternating periods of activity and worsening, but it does not establish the cause of either phase. Ask what activity involved—physical, cognitive, emotional, sensory or upright demands—whether worsening was immediate or delayed, its duration, whether the person returned to baseline and how often the pattern occurs. Consider sleep, pain, migraine, medications, anemia, endocrine conditions, dysautonomia, depression and relevant other illness rather than assuming deconditioning alone.

**Physiotherapy and occupational therapy** should coordinate activity plans so therapy sessions, transfers, household tasks and travel do not unknowingly compete for the same practical tolerance. **Psychology** may address guilt, uncertainty, fear and adaptation in a consent-based way, rather than explain prolonged physical worsening as avoidance or lack of motivation. **Primary care or relevant specialists** assess possible ME/CFS or another contributor where delayed and disproportionate post-exertional symptom exacerbation suggests it.

### Pacing is not one standard exercise dose

The goal is to match activity to medical safety, tolerability and the person's priorities. Options include changing the environment, distributing effort, choosing supports, shortening the task, using planned rest or selecting an alternative method. Progression—if appropriate at all—is an individualized clinical decision.

When **post-exertional malaise (PEM)** is reported, especially if ME/CFS is suspected or established, assess the delayed, disproportionate and potentially prolonged nature of worsening and respect condition-specific energy-management guidance. Do not prescribe automatic fixed increments, use a successful single clinic session as clearance for increased activity or require deliberate provocation of a crash. NICE guidance on ME/CFS does not, by itself, invalidate individualized rehabilitation in FND without PEM; the two clinical questions must be kept separate. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

### A case for a joint team plan

Someone with assessed functional weakness also reports exertion-related headache, light sensitivity and next-day exhaustion. PT could tailor movement to a safe function; OT could reduce task and sensory demands; the clinician responsible for migraine and fatigue could reassess other treatable causes. The patient chooses which activities deserve priority. The team watches whether the plan makes ordinary life more sustainable—not merely whether therapy attendance increased.

**Evidence boundary:** Pacing for FND relies heavily on individualized multidisciplinary and OT consensus, not a validated flare-prevention dose [1](#citation-1). NICE ME/CFS recommendations apply specifically to that disease [2](#citation-2).
