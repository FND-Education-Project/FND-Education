## For the Person With FND

### What are we making room for?

We've spent the last two lessons discovering how everyday activities can place different demands on us, and how difficult it can be to make decisions when our capacity changes.

Now we're going to look at another challenge: **what happens when something that's helping us also makes other parts of life more difficult?**

You already know that rehabilitation can support learning, that adaptations can help us participate, and that improvement doesn't always mean becoming symptom-free.

In these stories, look for what is improving, what the improvement requires, and what the person is trying to achieve. There may be more than one reasonable choice.

Your own symptoms and circumstances might make some decisions easier or more difficult. Think about what you'd choose, and what might change your mind.

### Story 1 — Natalie's appointment

Natalie has been attending physiotherapy once a week.

Getting there takes some planning. She arranges accessible transport, packs her earplugs and sunglasses, and keeps the appointment information in her phone. Her persistent migraine makes bright or noisy places particularly uncomfortable.

This morning, the car arrives late.

By the time Natalie reaches the clinic, the waiting room is busy. Someone is speaking loudly on a phone, and a television is playing near the reception desk.

Natalie finds a quieter place to wait. She closes her eyes for a while, then looks at the note she's brought.

*Ask about walking at home.*

Once she's in the treatment room, things improve. The physiotherapist knows about her difficulties with light and sound and makes the room more comfortable.

They've been working together on her movement difficulties. Today, with the therapist nearby for safety, Natalie manages to walk farther across the room than she did during her earlier appointments.

She tries again and manages a similar distance.

Natalie grins.

"Well, that's something!"

Her physiotherapist agrees. They've noticed encouraging changes during their sessions, although walking around her apartment remains less reliable.

As they finish, the physiotherapist mentions that another weekly appointment has become available.

"If you'd like, we could discuss whether seeing you more often might help us build on what you're learning."

Natalie likes the idea of making progress. She's spent a long time hoping that ordinary movement might become easier.

But she also knows how much trouble it took to get here this morning.

**Your turn:** Natalie has made encouraging progress during treatment, and another appointment may be possible. Would you consider attending twice a week? What would you want to know before deciding?

Natalie thinks for a moment.

"Could we talk about that next time? I'd like to think about how the rest of my week is going."

Her physiotherapist agrees.

The journey home is uncomfortable. Sunlight comes through the car window, and Natalie's migraine becomes more troublesome.

When she finally gets inside her apartment, she leaves her bag by the door and settles into her favourite chair.

There's a grocery order she meant to finish, but looking at the screen is difficult. She decides to deal with it tomorrow.

The following day, Natalie has more trouble moving around her apartment. She also finds it difficult to concentrate long enough to complete the grocery order.

She manages a simple meal and answers an important message, but most of what she'd planned remains unfinished.

By Friday, she's feeling somewhat better.

Leah visits with something from the bakery. She usually comes once or twice a week, and Natalie looks forward to their time together.

Today, though, Natalie is struggling to follow their conversation. She keeps losing the thread of what Leah is saying.

"We don't have to talk about anything complicated," Leah says. "I can just sit here and admire your excellent curtains."

Natalie laughs.

"They're particularly impressive when they're closed."

They enjoy a shorter visit than usual.

Later, Natalie thinks about the week. She's pleased with what happened in physiotherapy, but she hasn't walked any farther around her apartment. She also missed several things she wanted to do.

She's not certain which difficulties were connected to the appointment and which were part of her usual symptom changes.

At her next session, the physiotherapist asks how the week went.

Natalie begins with the walking.

"I was really pleased with what I did here. But I'd like to tell you what the rest of the week looked like."

She describes the transport, waiting room, migraine, groceries and shortened visit with Leah.

Her physiotherapist listens and asks what Natalie most wants the treatment to help her accomplish.

"Being able to move around my home more reliably would be wonderful," Natalie says. "But I'd also like enough room in my week to enjoy seeing Leah."

They discuss the appointment arrangements, how to judge whether the improvements are carrying over into daily life, and whether the current schedule needs adjusting.

**Your turn again:** You now know more about Natalie's week. Would you keep the same plan, change the appointments, or ask for more information? What would count as meaningful progress for Natalie?

Natalie decides not to add the second weekly appointment for now.

She and her physiotherapist agree to explore ways of making the existing appointments more manageable and to review how she's functioning between sessions.

As she prepares to leave, Natalie looks toward the far side of the treatment room.

She remembers how pleased she felt when she walked that distance.

She still feels pleased.

She just wants the progress to fit into the rest of her life.

### Answer key — What did you notice?

**The improvement in physiotherapy was real, but it wasn't the whole picture.**

Natalie walked farther during treatment. That's an encouraging observation.

However, she hadn't yet noticed the same improvement when moving around her apartment.

> **Transfer:** Using something learned in one situation in a different activity or setting.

We learned about transfer in Module 9. Progress during a supported therapy session may be valuable even before it becomes reliable elsewhere.

But if Natalie's goal is to move around her home more easily, what happens at home matters too.

**Did you notice everything the appointment required?**

The physiotherapy wasn't the only demanding part of Natalie's day.

She needed to organize transport, tolerate the journey, wait in a busy room, concentrate during treatment and travel home afterward.

Her migraine, movement difficulties and sensitivity to sound made some of those demands especially challenging.

The treatment room was adapted to her needs, but the whole journey wasn't.

Changing those surrounding demands might make the appointment more manageable without changing the rehabilitation itself.

**What did Natalie lose during the week?**

She postponed her groceries, struggled with some everyday activities and had a shorter visit with Leah.

These details matter because they tell us about the life she's trying to manage alongside rehabilitation.

> **Participation:** Being involved in everyday activities and parts of life that matter to us, such as caring for ourselves, working, learning or spending time with other people.

Natalie wants better movement. She also wants groceries in the kitchen and time with her friend.

One goal doesn't make the others less important.

**Could we conclude that physiotherapy made her symptoms worse?**

Not from this story.

Natalie's symptoms already vary, and she has a persistent migraine. We don't know how much the appointment or travel contributed to her difficult days.

But the difficulties are still worth discussing. Her care team needs to understand whether the overall plan is manageable, regardless of whether every change can be explained.

**Was it wrong to offer another appointment?**

Not necessarily.

The physiotherapist saw encouraging progress and offered Natalie an opportunity to discuss further treatment. Natalie then provided information that hadn't been part of the original decision.

Both were contributing to a better-informed plan.

The important discovery is that **treatment can be helpful without its current schedule being the best fit**.

### Before our next story

Natalie was trying to make rehabilitation fit into her week.

Now imagine someone who is improving at a particular skill but still can't do the everyday activity that made them want to improve in the first place.

**Would you keep practising the skill, change the activity, or try to make room for both?**

Keep that question in mind as you read about Amira.

### Story 2 — Amira's drawings

Amira enjoys drawing birds.

She particularly likes the little ones that gather near her window, arguing over the bird feeder as though they own the place.

Before her functional arm movements became troublesome, she often filled a sketchbook with drawings of them.

These days, her arm sometimes jerks when she tries to make a careful movement. Holding a pencil steady can be difficult, especially when she's trying to draw something small.

She's been attending occupational therapy to work on everyday activities involving her hands and arms.

During recent appointments, she's become more successful at some of the tasks they've practised. Her therapist is pleased, and Amira can see the difference herself.

At home, though, the results are less predictable.

She keeps a lidded cup beside her sketchbook because her arm movements can make ordinary drinking cups awkward. The adaptation works well, and she barely thinks about it anymore.

One afternoon, Amira receives a message from a friend who helps organize an art group at the local community centre.

They're putting together a display of drawings and paintings about the natural world.

"Would you like to contribute something?" her friend asks.

Amira looks at her sketchbook.

She'd love to contribute a bird drawing.

She's been trying to draw a small robin for several days. The body and wings are coming along, but she keeps struggling with the fine lines around its eyes and beak.

She spends another twenty minutes trying to make them look right. Each time her hand moves unexpectedly, she has to decide whether to correct the line or start again.

Eventually, she puts the pencil down.

The following morning, Amira sits at her table with the unfinished robin.

There's time to work on the careful hand movements she's been practising in therapy. She could also try making the drawing differently, using some of the larger art materials she already owns.

The community centre has asked for the artwork by the end of the week.

**Your turn:** Amira's hand control has improved during some therapy tasks, but detailed drawing remains difficult. Would you spend the morning practising those movements, change how you make the artwork, or do something else? What matters most to your decision?

Amira opens a cupboard and takes out a box of coloured paper, wide markers and paint.

She begins making a picture with larger shapes instead of tiny pencil lines.

The robin gradually takes form.

It's not quite the drawing she had imagined. Its feathers are made from overlapping pieces of coloured paper, and its feet are rather larger than a real robin's.

Amira looks at them.

"Well, at least he'll be stable in a strong wind."

She laughs and keeps going.

Later, while talking to her friend on the phone, she reaches across the table to point at part of the picture. The movement happens more smoothly than some of the careful movements she was attempting earlier.

She notices, but doesn't stop the conversation to test whether she can do it again.

By the afternoon, the robin is nearly finished. She adds a branch and a few leaves, then decides she'll leave the background for tomorrow.

At her next occupational therapy appointment, Amira brings the picture.

Her therapist admires it.

"That's lovely. How did you find making it?"

Amira explains what happened with the pencil and how she eventually used different materials.

She also mentions something that has been bothering her.

"I'm getting better at some things here. But I started coming because I wanted to do things like this again."

She taps the picture.

"I've spent so much time worrying about whether I can draw properly that I nearly forgot to make anything."

Her therapist asks which activities Amira would most like to work toward, and they talk about her original goals.

Amira still wants to improve her hand control. There are practical reasons for that, including everyday tasks that remain difficult.

But she's also discovered that she doesn't have to wait until her hand movements improve before enjoying art again.

**Your turn again:** What would you count as progress for Amira? Does the finished picture count, even though she used different materials? What might still be worth working on in rehabilitation?

Amira and her therapist decide to review the activities they're practising and how those activities relate to what she wants to do at home.

They don't abandon the work she's been doing. They also agree that making art is an important goal in its own right.

At the end of the week, Amira takes her robin to the community centre.

Her friend holds it up beside the other pictures.

"I love the feathers," she says.

Amira looks at the little bird.

She likes them too.

### Answer key — What did you find?

**Amira was making progress in one place but having difficulty using it somewhere else.**

Her hand control had improved during some occupational therapy activities. That was meaningful progress.

But the detailed pencil work she wanted to do at home remained difficult.

This takes us back to transfer. Learning or improving a particular task doesn't automatically mean every related activity becomes easier.

Amira and her therapist may need to consider which activities are improving and whether those changes are helping her outside treatment.

**Did you notice what happened when she concentrated on her hand?**

Amira spent a long time carefully watching and correcting her pencil movements. Later, she reached across the table while talking on the phone, and the movement appeared smoother.

> **Automatic movement:** Movement that can happen with little conscious attention to each part, rather than requiring us to direct every step.

We've learned that attention can sometimes influence movement in FND.

The difference between those moments is interesting, but it doesn't prove why Amira's movements changed. We don't know whether attention, the kind of movement, the surroundings or something else made the difference.

It's a clue worth recognizing, not a diagnosis or a treatment instruction.

**Was changing her art materials giving up?**

Amira used larger shapes and different tools to make her picture.

> **Adaptation:** Changing a task, tool or environment to make an activity more accessible or manageable.

She didn't need to complete every movement in the way she once did to participate in something she enjoyed.

Her art wasn't evidence that her functional arm symptoms had disappeared. It showed that she could still create something meaningful while managing those symptoms.

**What else did Amira achieve?**

She finished a picture, contributed to a community display and enjoyed making art again.

Those outcomes weren't the same as improving precise hand movements.

Both kinds of progress could matter.

Amira might continue practising a skill because she wants to use it in everyday life. She might also use adaptations because she wants to participate today, rather than wait for an uncertain future improvement.

The two approaches don't have to compete.

**Did you notice how Amira's goal became clearer?**

At first, she was focused on drawing accurately. Her attention gradually shifted toward making her hand perform correctly.

By the end, she'd recognized something important: her larger goal was to create art and enjoy doing it.

That didn't make precise movement unimportant. It helped her and her therapist see how rehabilitation might serve the activities she actually valued.

Sometimes the most useful question isn't simply, "Am I getting better at this exercise?"

It's also, "Is what we're doing helping me live the life I'm working toward?"

---

### Bringing the stories together

Natalie and Amira were both making progress in rehabilitation, but they needed to consider how that progress fitted into everyday life. Natalie wanted treatment that left room for her other needs. Amira wanted to improve her movement while continuing to enjoy art. **Rehabilitation, adaptations, rest and meaningful activities can all have a place in the same life.** The challenge is finding an approach that supports what matters to the person, rather than making everything else revolve around treatment.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters

### When helping becomes another full-time job

A rehabilitation plan might be sensible in a clinic. But then comes travel, cooking, appointments, family responsibilities, symptoms and the next day. A supporter may find themselves arranging everything and trying to keep the entire plan going.

It's possible to care deeply about someone and still be exhausted by what they're asking of you—or what the system has left you to manage.

### Preserve the relationship as well as the plan

Ask together: Which activities are genuinely helpful? Which feel unsustainable? Is therapy leaving enough room for meals, meaningful connection and rest? Is an accommodation helping right now, even when symptoms haven't improved?

You can offer support without taking responsibility for recovery. The person remains the authority on their priorities; your ability to help has limits worth respecting too.

One useful way to talk is: “I want this plan to work for both of us. I can help with transport twice this month, but not every day. Can we ask the team what other support is available?”

### Hand the clinical work back where it belongs

If a home routine is too difficult, unsafe or worsening symptoms, request review by the **physiotherapist, OT, SLP, psychologist or other treating clinician** who recommended it. Ask about the goal, dose, safety instructions, review criteria and whether another route is available. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [3](#citation-3))

For complex needs, **primary care or a designated care coordinator** may need to clarify responsibilities and referrals. If supporter exhaustion threatens safe care, ask about additional practical services or respite options where available. Those services depend on locality, eligibility and consent; they cannot be assumed.

Persistent or markedly changed medical symptoms still belong to the appropriate medical team, and emergencies require emergency services—not a renegotiated family pacing schedule.

### A shared review, not a scorecard

Together, choose one part of the current plan to **keep, modify, pause or ask the team about**. Include what the person gains and what each person's contribution costs. Continuing aids, written reminders, sensory accommodations or assistance can remain necessary alongside rehabilitation.

> “I don't blame him for any of this, I just need help!”

*Figure 1. Community experience.* A spouse describes caregiver strain without blaming the person with FND. [Original public source](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1takhkp/convincing_someone_they_have_fnd/). (*citations* [4](#citation-4))

**Something to discuss together:** “What part of our plan makes life better, and what part would we like professionals to help us rethink?”

**One small thing:** Prepare one question for the treating team about a task or support that has become difficult to sustain. You don't need to solve it alone.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team

### From understanding capacity to choosing treatment

Throughout this module, we've examined how different demands affect functioning, why abilities can fluctuate and how later symptom changes may alter rehabilitation decisions.

Those principles inform clinical judgment, but they do not establish which intervention is appropriate for a particular FND presentation.

**FND is a diagnosis with multiple symptom presentations, not a single rehabilitation protocol.** Treatment selection requires consideration of the specific symptoms, relevant positive diagnostic findings, coexisting conditions, available evidence and the person's functional goals.

The next task is to connect what you've learned about available capacity with the guidance appropriate to the symptom being treated.

### Different symptoms, different treatment evidence

Functional motor symptoms, functional seizures, communication difficulties and functional visual symptoms do not share an interchangeable treatment programme.

For example, physiotherapy approaches for functional gait disorder may emphasize movement retraining, external focus and functional practice. These recommendations have a different evidence base from psychological interventions studied for functional seizures or speech-language interventions for functional voice disorders.

The distinction matters when interpreting treatment research. A trial demonstrating improvement in functional motor symptoms does not establish that the same intervention is effective for functional seizures. Likewise, evidence supporting treatment of functional dysphonia cannot automatically be extended to functional language difficulties.

Even within a symptom category, the specific presentation matters. An intervention appropriate for intermittent functional tremor may be unsuitable for fixed functional posturing or profound weakness.

Clinical recommendations should therefore distinguish between interventions supported by comparative research, those based primarily on professional consensus and approaches supported only by preliminary evidence. Evidence for a complete rehabilitation programme should not be presented as proof that every component works independently.

### Where to find presentation-specific guidance

Our Reference Library brings together several resources for selecting and evaluating care:

- **[Diagnostic Techniques](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/README.md)** — positive diagnostic findings, differential assessment and limitations for individual symptom presentations.
- **[Recovery and Management Technique Index](../../reference/recovery-techniques/technique-index.md)** — rehabilitation and management approaches organized by symptom, with evidence classifications and links to detailed pages.
- **[Recovery Techniques Collection Guide](../../reference/recovery-techniques/collection-guide.md)** — how to interpret evidence labels, assess treatment relevance and recognize the limits of individual techniques.
- **[Common Co-occurring Conditions](../../reference/co-occurring-conditions/README.md)** — information on migraine, pain, fatigue, sleep, autonomic symptoms and other conditions that may require parallel assessment and treatment.

These resources provide an organized starting point, not a substitute for professional guidance or specialist assessment. The Reference Library's individual technique pages remain working drafts undergoing clinical review. Clinicians should consult the linked original studies and guidelines when making treatment decisions.

### Applying the evidence to an individual

The evidence supporting an intervention and its suitability for a particular patient are related but separate questions.

A biopsychosocial approach helps clinicians understand how different influences interact in an individual's functioning and rehabilitation. It provides a framework for considering the whole clinical picture without assuming that every influence has equal importance or that one explanation accounts for all symptoms.

> **Biopsychosocial approach:** A framework for understanding health through the interaction of biological factors (such as neurological conditions and physical health), psychological factors (such as attention, emotions and learned responses), and social factors (such as relationships, environment and access to care). It does not mean that FND symptoms are imagined or necessarily caused by psychological factors.

For instance, motor retraining may be appropriate for an assessed functional movement presentation, yet persistent pain, joint disease, significant sensory sensitivity or suspected post-exertional malaise may influence how rehabilitation can safely proceed.

Similarly, treatment directed toward functional seizures does not replace appropriate evaluation and treatment of coexisting epilepsy. Communication rehabilitation must account for the specific speech, voice or language presentation, while visual rehabilitation requires appropriate ophthalmological assessment and consideration of other visual disorders.

These distinctions influence professional responsibilities. Neurology, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech-language pathology, psychology and other specialties contribute different expertise. When findings extend beyond the treating clinician's scope, appropriate medical assessment or referral becomes part of the rehabilitation plan.

The objective is not to combine every available intervention. It is to select a clinically justified approach, establish meaningful outcomes, consider the demands treatment places on the patient and review whether the intervention remains appropriate.

### Bringing the module together

Available capacity helps explain why an activity or treatment may be manageable under one set of circumstances but difficult under another. Symptom-specific evidence helps determine what treatment should aim to accomplish and which methods are appropriate.

Neither provides every answer independently.

The clinician's responsibility is to bring these sources of information together: the diagnosed presentation, treatment evidence, coexisting conditions, the person's circumstances and their own priorities.

**A sound rehabilitation decision is not simply choosing an intervention that has helped people with FND. It is choosing an intervention supported for the relevant presentation, making it accessible and appropriate for this patient, and evaluating whether it produces a benefit that matters to them.**

That is where the principles of this module become practical clinical care.
