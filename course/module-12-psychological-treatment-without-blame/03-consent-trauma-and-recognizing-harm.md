# Consent, Trauma, and Recognizing Harm

## For the Person With FND
{: #for-the-person-with-fnd .shareable }

### When treatment needs to change

Callum is seventeen and has been attending cognitive behavioural therapy, or CBT, for several weeks.

He has FND, which affects his walking and sometimes makes speaking difficult or impossible. His speech can change without any warning he recognizes. Sometimes he struggles to get sounds out properly. Other times, he knows exactly what he wants to say but can't speak at all.

When speaking isn't available, Callum uses his phone. Typing can be slow, and people don't always wait. His CBT isn't meant to explain what caused his speech difficulties. He wants help with **the worry and frustration of taking part when his speech is unreliable**.

So far, the appointments have been useful. But today his psychologist suggests an exercise that doesn't feel right.

### What Callum has learned about CBT

In their first appointments, Callum learned how CBT works: thoughts, feelings and actions can affect one another. What we believe about a situation can change how we feel and what we do next.

For example, Callum remembers a group discussion at school. His speech stopped working, and he began typing an answer on his phone. Before he finished, his classmates moved on to something else.

Callum felt hurt and embarrassed. He started thinking that nobody wanted to wait for him. The following week, he contributed less to the discussion because he expected the same thing to happen.

His psychologist helped him separate what happened from what he'd concluded. His classmates really had moved on. But did that mean none of them wanted to hear him?

"Probably not. We're all on our phones half the time anyway. They can't always tell if I'm typing an answer or just looking something up. Some might wait if I asked. But some people really don't."

Callum enjoyed working with his friends and wanted to contribute. CBT wasn't about telling him his feelings were wrong. It helped him notice his thoughts and feelings, consider other possibilities and decide whether to respond differently. Now they could practise.

### A suggestion that doesn't quite fit

At his next appointment, the psychologist asks whether Callum would like to work on speaking up during group discussions.

"Yeah. I don't want to stop contributing just because someone might not wait for me."

She suggests practising a short conversation, then pauses.

"I've noticed you sometimes reach for your phone before attempting an answer. I'm wondering whether worry about your speech might sometimes make you turn to typing earlier than you need to."

Callum thinks about that.

"Maybe sometimes. But there are other times when I just can't talk."

"Would you be willing to try answering a few questions before using your phone, to see what happens?"

Callum hesitates. His speech is working today, but it can change without warning. The phone isn't simply something he uses because he's nervous.

*If my speech stops, how would I tell her I need to stop?*

Now he's worrying about how to disagree without disappointing her. That takes some of his attention away from what he wants to say. But he recognizes the difference: this worry is happening *now*. His speech can fail even when he's calm.

"I don't think that would work for me," he says.

"Can you tell me what concerns you?"

"If my speech stops, I need the phone. Otherwise I can't explain what's happening. Sometimes I can't even ask someone to wait."

The psychologist thinks for a moment. She knows his speech can stop. But when he reaches for his phone *before* trying to speak, she'd wondered whether expecting difficulty sometimes leads him to avoid speaking. She hadn't checked that idea.

"Can I check something?" she asks. "When you reach for your phone, is that because you can't speak, or because you're afraid you won't be able to?"

"Both can happen. Sometimes the sounds come out wrong, and sometimes nothing comes out. I can't always tell when it will happen."

"So you need the phone available even on a good day?"

"Exactly. How else would I tell you if it stopped?"

The psychologist nods. "I was looking at whether worry changes when you try to speak. I hadn't asked enough about what the phone does for you. We can explore that worry without removing it."

"So I can keep using it?"

"Of course. Let's change the exercise."

Callum feels relieved. He hadn't wanted to refuse treatment; he wanted it to make sense.

### Changing the exercise together

The psychologist returns to the school discussion.

"Instead of practising without your phone, could we work on what you might do when other people don't give you enough time to answer?"

"Yeah. That's what I want help with."

They return to his thought: *If I can't answer quickly, people won't want me involved.* Some classmates have been impatient; others have been happy to wait.

"Maybe I could tell them I need more time before they move on," he suggests.

"How would you like to do that?"

Typing can take too long. Some phones and communication apps let people save phrases to show on the screen or speak aloud.

Callum could save something like:

*Please give me a moment. I have something to say.*

He could also agree on a gesture with friends: one raised finger could mean, *I need more time.* It wouldn't work everywhere, but he likes having options.

The psychologist suggests they practice a short conversation, with his phone available throughout. Callum agrees.

### Trying a different approach

The psychologist pretends to be a classmate.

"Which part of the presentation would you like to do?"

Callum's speech is working, but they're practising how to ask for time. He shows her the saved phrase. She waits, and he answers aloud: "I'd like to help with the research."

"That worked here. How do you think it might go at school?"

"Depends who's asking."

They smile. Practising in a quiet office doesn't show how his classmates will behave. If someone moves on without waiting, he might ask a friend to bring the discussion back to him or ask his teacher for help.

Some classmates may still be impatient. CBT can't change their behaviour, and Callum shouldn't have to solve every communication barrier himself. But he now has a way to ask for time without giving up his phone.

### Why changing the plan mattered

Callum had agreed to CBT, but the first suggested exercise didn't fit his needs.

> **Consent:** Freely agreeing to something after understanding what is involved.
>
> **Informed consent:** Making that choice after receiving understandable information about what is being proposed, its possible benefits and difficulties, and other available options.

Agreeing to begin psychological treatment doesn't mean agreeing to every exercise a therapist suggests.

His psychologist explained her idea, listened to what didn't fit and changed the exercise with him. She hadn't meant to leave him without a way to communicate, but her suggestion hadn't accounted for that need. Callum could disagree with an activity without rejecting CBT itself.

### When communicating takes too much effort

A phone can be extremely helpful when speech isn't available, but it doesn't solve every communication problem. Typing may be slow, tiring or difficult, particularly when other people are waiting.

One community member described their experience of speech loss:

> “Loosing your speech is incredibly lonely. You can be in a room full of people and feel completely invisible.”

The same person later explained how difficult it can be to keep up with conversations by typing:

> “You end up just giving up trying to explain as it’s just too much hassle and the moment has often passed by the time you are done typing.”

— [A community member describing their experiences](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1vpsiic/recently_diagnosed_fnd_complete_speech_loss/)

Having a phone isn't always enough; other people also need to wait. Saved phrases and gestures won't restore speech, but they may help Callum stay involved.

### When should treatment be reconsidered?

Callum still felt awkward asking someone to wait. But he understood why they were practising, had helped choose the task, could communicate and knew he could stop.

**An exercise being difficult doesn't automatically mean it's helping.** What matters is whether the activity has a useful purpose, the person understands and agrees to it, and the results are reviewed together.

Therapy may involve upsetting conversations or unfamiliar practice. That can be worthwhile with a clear goal and suitable support. But a therapist repeatedly ignoring concerns, pressuring someone to discuss trauma, treating disagreement as resistance or continuing a plan that's making things worse are reasons to question it.

Someone may need to pause, request changes or seek another opinion. New or changing medical symptoms still need medical attention. Treatment should be something people can discuss and adjust together.

### Having a say in your treatment

Nadia wanted to understand what treatment might help with. Graham explored what seizure-focused CBT might involve and how to recognize useful progress. Callum has discovered something else: **even after treatment begins, you can help shape how it happens**.

You might agree on an overall goal but find that one exercise is too tiring or doesn't fit your needs. You can discuss changing the plan. Sometimes another approach or professional may be needed.

Callum's speech remains unreliable, and one practice conversation doesn't prove CBT will help. But he and his psychologist can keep working toward a goal he values without taking away his way to communicate.

**Agreeing to psychological treatment is the beginning of working together, not the end of having a choice.**

### Something to think about

Have you ever agreed with a goal but been unsure about the suggested way to reach it? What would you want to understand before deciding whether to try?

### How might that feel?

If you needed to tell someone helping you that something wasn't working, what might make that conversation easier or harder?

### One small step

Try a little of what Callum learned about CBT.

Think of one small, safe everyday situation that was difficult. In your thoughts or on paper, finish:

**"I thought ___."**

**"I felt ___."**

**"I did ___."**

Now ask yourself: **"Could there be another way to understand what happened, or one small change that might help next time?"**

If it's safe and manageable, you might try that change and notice what happens. You can also leave this as a thought exercise. This isn't about testing your FND symptoms or making them disappear.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters
{: #for-family-friends-and-other-supporters .shareable }

Do not search for a hidden cause, question the person's memory or press for session details. Believe their report of a boundary or harmful interaction. Ask what support they want before contacting a provider.

Therapy can bring temporary discomfort, but that phrase must not excuse coercion, retraumatization, unsafe deterioration or loss of consent. Help the person reach independent clinical, safeguarding or crisis support when needed.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team
{: #for-clinicians-and-the-care-team .shareable }

### When new information changes treatment

FND rehabilitation often begins with a working explanation of what may be affecting someone's symptoms or participation. A clinician might consider attention, expectations, emotional responses or learned patterns and select an activity to explore whether changing one of them helps.

A useful hypothesis remains open to correction. **The patient's response to a proposed exercise can reveal something important about the problem, rather than merely whether the patient is willing to cooperate.**

This matters because treatment can be helpful or harmful depending on how clinicians interpret what they observe. Mcloughlin and colleagues' review of iatrogenic harm in FND examines difficulties that healthcare interactions themselves can create. The British Psychological Society's guidance likewise supports individualized psychological care rather than a single assumed explanation of FND. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))

Callum's experience provides a practical example: an exercise intended to examine anticipatory worry overlooks the function of a communication aid.

### What does Callum's phone tell us?

Callum is seventeen and has FND affecting his walking and speech. Sometimes speech becomes difficult or unavailable without warning, so he uses his phone to communicate. His CBT focuses on the worry and frustration of participating in conversations when speaking is unreliable—not on discovering the cause of his speech symptoms.

At school, classmates once moved on while he was typing an answer. He felt excluded and began expecting that others would not wait for him.

During therapy, his psychologist notices that Callum sometimes reaches for his phone before attempting to speak. She wonders whether anticipating difficulty occasionally leads him to avoid speaking, and proposes a short conversation in which he answers before using the phone.

Callum raises a concern: if his speech stops, he may need the phone to explain what is happening, request time or say he wants to stop. Even on a day when he can speak, he cannot count on that ability remaining available.

The fuller account appears in [For the Person With FND](#for-the-person-with-fnd).

The psychologist now has information that changes how she can interpret his behaviour. **Reaching for the phone may sometimes reflect worry; it may also preserve communication when speech fails. Both can be true.**

### What would the proposed exercise actually test?

If Callum speaks successfully without his phone, that shows he could speak during that task. It does not establish that his speech is reliably available or that he generally uses the phone to avoid speaking.

If his speech stops, the exercise may leave him unable to communicate his needs. That would not tell the psychologist whether his earlier phone use was driven by worry.

The proposed activity therefore changes two things at once: Callum's opportunity to attempt speech and his access to communication. It becomes difficult to interpret the result in relation to the original hypothesis.

This is more than a theoretical distinction. Speech-language therapy consensus guidance has raised concerns about aids potentially maintaining functional mutism, while broader communication guidance supports individualized access to alternative communication. Neither position, by itself, establishes that removing Callum's phone would improve his speech. [Speech-language guidance](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2021-326767) and [communication-access guidance](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/professional-issues/augmentative-and-alternative-communication/) address different parts of the decision.

The useful question becomes: **Can the psychologist explore Callum's expectations while keeping his ability to communicate available?**

### Keep the goal; revise the exercise

Callum's agreed goal remains relevant. He wants to contribute to school discussions without withdrawing because he expects classmates to move on.

Rather than asking him to delay using his phone, the psychologist can help him distinguish what happened from what he concluded. His classmates did move on before he finished typing. That is a real participation barrier. He also concluded that they did not want to hear from him—a possibility worth exploring, not an established fact about every classmate.

They could practise ways to ask for time, perhaps using a saved phone message or a gesture agreed with classmates. Callum keeps the phone available throughout. They can then explore what he expects, what others actually do and which arrangements help him participate.

This approach doesn't ask Callum to deny his speech difficulties or assume that every classmate will respond well. Some social barriers need changes from other people, not just changes in Callum's thinking.

The **treatment goal stays the same**, but the revised method now explores the intended difficulty without compromising his communication. That is an improvement in clinical reasoning, not a retreat from therapy.

### Consent can improve the formulation

Callum agreed to CBT, not to every possible exercise. His objection offered information the psychologist had not considered. Listening allowed them to improve the plan together.

Meaningful consent therefore contributes to the quality of treatment. A person who can ask questions, disagree and communicate changing needs can help the clinician distinguish an unhelpful assumption from a useful therapeutic target.

Callum's age makes the applicable consent and confidentiality framework relevant, but his ability to express his preferences remains central to this clinical discussion.

The same principle applies when psychological care explores difficult emotions or trauma. Ludwig and colleagues' research identifies group-level associations between adversity and FND; it does not establish that trauma caused an individual's condition or that trauma-focused work is necessarily the appropriate treatment. (*citation* [1](#citation-1))

A formulation remains a proposal to examine collaboratively. New information—including disagreement—can make it more accurate.

### What this changes clinically

Callum's case suggests four practical lessons:

- **Ask what a behaviour accomplishes.** Anticipation, practical necessity and adaptation can coexist. An observation alone may not distinguish them.
- **Examine what an exercise can establish.** A successful task, a difficult task or a patient's reluctance may each have several explanations.
- **Separate the goal from the method.** Callum still wants to participate in school discussions; the therapist can change the exercise while preserving that goal.
- **Use the patient's experience to revise treatment.** Understanding why something does not fit may produce a more useful plan than insisting on the original one.

Across FND presentations, this reasoning is relevant whenever an activity, support or symptom response has more than one possible explanation. The exact technique will differ, but the clinical habit remains valuable: ask, test what the evidence can support, listen and revise.

**A useful treatment plan becomes more individualized as clinician and patient learn together.**
