# Why Psychological Treatment Does Not Mean Imaginary

> **Working draft:** This page was automatically generated and is looking for [contributors and reviewers](https://fnd-education-project.github.io/FND-Education/).

Psychological treatment is one possible part of care. Being offered it should never mean that symptoms are imagined, chosen or “only stress.”

## For the Person With FND
{: #for-the-person-with-fnd .shareable }

### What is psychological treatment meant to help with?

We've learned that FND symptoms are real and involuntary. A diagnosis rests on recognizable clinical features, not proof of stress or trauma. What caused someone's FND and what might help them now are different questions.

Nadia is about to discover how easily those questions can become confused.

### Before the appointment

Nadia has an appointment with a psychologist next week. At her kitchen table, she opens her laptop beside a mug of tea and Dr. Chen's referral letter.

You may remember that Nadia has movement difficulties and functional seizures, alongside migraines that have recently worsened. She originally visited Dr. Chen for migraine care. They agreed that the psychological assessment would explore a different question, while he continued helping her with migraine treatment.

Nadia wants to understand what that different question might be. She looks up psychological treatment for FND and finds that it can serve several purposes. Some approaches work with particular functional symptoms. Others help someone manage fear of episodes, cope with changes to everyday life or address depression, anxiety or trauma-related difficulties that deserve care in their own right.

None of those possibilities requires a therapist to discover the hidden event that caused FND.

That gives Nadia some hope. She could imagine discussing how unpredictable symptoms have narrowed her life, or whether therapy could help her feel less overwhelmed by the disruption. She's not sure which approach would suit her, but that's why she's going to the appointment.

She writes three questions on a small page:

*What could treatment help me with?*

*What would we work on together?*

*How would we know whether it's useful?*

Then she remembers why she's been uneasy about going.

Years earlier, a psychologist diagnosed her with *conversion disorder*, another name used for FND. Neurological examinations had supported that diagnosis. But the psychologist seemed certain that unresolved emotions caused her symptoms, and Nadia felt that questioning the explanation was mistaken for rejecting the diagnosis itself.

She eventually learned she could accept having FND without accepting every explanation for its cause. She hopes this appointment will be different.

### A difficult beginning

The new psychologist greets Nadia warmly. The room is quiet, and the overhead lights are off. Nadia appreciates that; her migraine has made the journey uncomfortable.

"I understand you've been living with conversion disorder for several years," the psychologist says. "I've helped people with this before. When your symptoms begin or worsen, have you noticed what you're feeling? Have you found any connection?"

Nadia feels her shoulders tighten. They're barely seated, and the conversation already sounds familiar.

"I've thought about things that affect my symptoms," she replies. "But FND doesn't necessarily come from unresolved emotions. It's a neurological condition."

"Conversion disorder is included in the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*," the psychologist replies. "The name may have changed, but I think you're in the right place for treatment."

"I know it's in the DSM. But the criteria doesn't require a psychological cause anymore."

"Do you think its place in that manual might still tell us something?"

Nadia is irritated. She came to explore treatment, not defend her diagnosis. Still, she tries once more.

"Functional MRI studies have found differences in how brain networks work in people with FND."

"Would you be surprised to learn that researchers also found brain differences in people with depression and anxiety? What do you think? Would imaging alone tell us whether something is psychological or neurological?"

"No, but that's not—"

Nadia stops. She knows that brain imaging isn't a diagnostic test for an individual with FND. She was challenging the assumption that her symptoms must express hidden emotions, not denying that psychiatric conditions involve the brain.

*I didn't come here to teach someone about FND,* she thinks. The appointment has barely begun, and she already feels defensive.

"All right," she says, settling back. She'll listen for now.

### Questions that seem to have an answer already

The psychologist asks about Nadia's symptoms and daily life. Nadia describes days when movement is manageable and others when simple tasks exhaust her. Then the questions turn toward her past.

"Tell me about when the symptoms first appeared. Were you under unusual stress?"

"There were difficult things happening. I was having other health problems too."

"Do you think there were feelings you couldn't deal with at the time?"

"I'm not sure there were."

"Could some have been difficult to recognize?"

"Possibly."

The psychologist makes a note. Nadia wonders what her answer will mean on paper. She said *possible*, not *true*.

As they continue, Nadia notices a pattern. Mentioning stress brings more questions about emotions. Describing an unpredictable functional seizure soon leads back to how she'd felt beforehand. She's willing to discuss her feelings, but she can't shake the sense that the questions are steering toward a cause.

"Do you think your migraines might be connected to unresolved stress?" the psychologist asks.

"Dr. Chen and I are looking at treatments for those separately. Migraine is neurological, but biological, psychological and social factors can all affect it."

"So perhaps over the coming days you could write down what you're feeling before a migraine worsens?"

A record like that might sometimes be useful. But Nadia had tried to explain that something can *influence* an illness without having *caused* it. The distinction seems to have gone unheard.

Her frustration grows. She wants to say that her migraine isn't a puzzle she's failed to solve. Instead, she gives a small nod. Her prepared questions are still in her bag, but reaching for them feels like starting the argument over again.

### When Nadia stops trying to sort it out

At first, Nadia compared each question with what she'd learned about FND. Now every exchange demands another decision: *Is this a useful possibility, or is she assuming it's the answer? Do I need to challenge that?*

Her migraine is worsening, and trying to filter the conversation has become exhausting. She listens less closely. Her annoyance stays hidden behind short replies and a tired smile.

"Do you find it difficult to accept that your emotional well-being might contribute to your symptoms?" the psychologist asks.

*I never said it couldn't,* Nadia thinks. But explaining that again would return them to the argument at the beginning.

"I'm not sure."

The psychologist suggests more appointments to explore Nadia's emotional experiences and their possible connection to her symptoms.

"Would we also work on the difficulties I'm having now?" Nadia asks.

"That may be possible. But wouldn't understanding what's underneath those symptoms be useful first?"

Perhaps. But Nadia still doesn't know what they would actually work toward. She doesn't want to agree to a series of appointments simply because she has run out of energy to ask questions.

"Could I think about it?"

"Certainly. You don't need to decide today."

Nadia thanks her and leaves, frustrated but relieved. The psychologist had been courteous, and some questions were reasonable. Yet the appointment seemed focused on explaining why she became ill, rather than finding out what help she wanted now.

### Two different questions

Nadia arrived hoping to discuss the possible *purpose* of psychological treatment. The psychologist spent much of the visit exploring a possible *cause* of her FND. Both questions might be worth asking, but they aren't interchangeable.

The psychologist was correct that conversion disorder appears in a psychiatric diagnostic manual, and that brain-imaging differences also occur in psychiatric conditions. Neither fact establishes that unresolved emotions caused Nadia's FND. Asking about difficult experiences may be appropriate; treating a possible connection as the answer is another matter.

> **Treatment goal:** Something a person and clinician agree they want care to help with. It might involve symptoms, everyday activities, distress or a separate mental-health condition.

Psychological treatment doesn't have to prove why FND began to have a useful goal. For example, someone might work on fear of functional seizures, ways to participate in life or a coexisting anxiety disorder. Approaches and evidence differ, so a helpful assessment should make the proposed aim clear enough for the person to consider.

Nadia's quiet answers weren't agreement with every assumption. She was tired and frustrated. Her silence may have left misunderstandings unresolved, but it didn't mean she had accepted a particular explanation or consented to treatment.

### What Nadia takes home

Back at home, Nadia pulls the page of questions from her bag. She didn't ask most of them. Still, she has learned something important about the psychologist's approach, and she can decide whether it suits her. Her migraine care with Dr. Chen remains separate.

For now, she sets the page aside and rests. She doesn't need to decide today.

### Something to think about

Think about a difficulty you might want help with. Does knowing why it began tell you everything about what could help you now?

### How might that feel?

When you think about getting help, what hope or concern would you most want a therapist to understand?

### One small step

Complete this sentence, even if only in your thoughts:

**"I'd like help with ___ because being able to ___ matters to me."**

You don't need to be considering psychological treatment to do this.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters
{: #for-family-friends-and-other-supporters .shareable }

Do not treat therapy as a confession or ask what hidden event “really caused” FND. The person can find therapy useful without accepting that story, and can decline it without rejecting their diagnosis.

Support access if wanted: transport, privacy, technology, childcare or quiet recovery time. Do not ask for details of sessions unless the person offers them.

### Explain target, rationale and limits

State whether the referral concerns a specific FND presentation, adjustment to illness, cognitive or emotional processes relevant to rehabilitation, or a comorbid psychiatric condition. Do not use the referral as retrospective proof of causation.

Evidence varies by modality, phenotype and outcome. Discuss mixed trial findings, alternatives, burden and patient preference. Psychological care should sit within continuing neurological and general healthcare, with reassessment of new or changed symptoms. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))
