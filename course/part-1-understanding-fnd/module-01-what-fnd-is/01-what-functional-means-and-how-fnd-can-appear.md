# What “Functional” Means and How FND Can Appear

> **Working draft:** This page is a newly generated interim draft (October 12, 2026), replacing human-authored text that is preserved in an internal editorial archive. It is looking for [contributors and reviewers](https://fndeducationproject.org/contact/).

Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) describes genuine neurological symptoms involving how the nervous system functions. It can affect movement, sensation, awareness, speech, vision and other abilities. The word *functional* does not mean the symptoms are imaginary, intentionally produced or unimportant. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

## For the Person With FND
{: #for-the-person-with-fnd .shareable }

### What does “functional” mean?

Your brain, spinal cord, nerves and body continuously exchange information. These processes help you move, feel, speak and pay attention. With FND, aspects of this functioning may become disrupted in ways that produce real symptoms. This description does not establish one exact biological mechanism for every person. Researchers are exploring several interacting processes, and important questions remain unanswered. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [3](#citation-3))

A person can experience major difficulty even when a scan does not show an injury that explains that particular problem. At the same time, a person with FND can also have an injury, a neurological disease or another medical condition. **Functional and structural problems are not mutually exclusive.** A normal scan by itself does not diagnose FND.

### How FND can appear

FND does not have one required symptom. Examples include:

- **Movement difficulties:** weakness, paralysis, shaking, jerks, changes in walking, or unusual fixed postures.
- **Episodes:** functional seizures or other temporary changes in awareness, movement or responsiveness.
- **Sensory and communication difficulties:** altered feeling, some visual symptoms, or problems speaking or using the voice.
- **Thinking and everyday function:** difficulties with concentration, remembering, finding words or carrying out activities. These need careful assessment because several conditions may contribute.

Not all these problems automatically mean a person has FND. Similar symptoms can come from other health conditions. A clinician considers the individual history and appropriate examination findings.

### Why symptoms can vary

Some functional symptoms change with the activity, the situation or the way a movement happens. For example, a clinician may notice that a particular movement is easier in one context than another. This can offer evidence about how the nervous system is functioning. It **does not** mean the person is choosing the symptom or can simply force it to disappear. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

Symptoms may be constant, intermittent or unpredictable. A brief successful movement in a clinic does not tell the whole story about fatigue, safety, the help someone needs or what happens afterwards.

### What a diagnosis should explain

A useful diagnostic conversation describes the positive findings that support FND, any other conditions being considered, and what further review might be necessary. A diagnosis should not depend only on tests being normal, or on a person's history of anxiety, stress or trauma. Psychological stress may matter to some individuals but is not required to diagnose FND. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2))

### Questions to reflect on

What part of the word *functional* remains confusing? Which everyday limitations would you want a clinician to understand beyond what happened during an examination?

### One small step

If useful, write down one symptom, when it happens, and how it affects an activity you value. Include any delayed recovery cost. You do not need to test yourself repeatedly or push through unsafe symptoms to prove anything.

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters
{: #for-family-friends-and-other-supporters .shareable }

FND symptoms are real and involuntary. Changes in symptoms from one moment to another do not show whether someone is trying hard enough. Avoid asking the person to repeat an examination movement at home as a test of credibility. Ask what support would make an important activity safer or more manageable.

Help the person record questions for a clinician if they wish, and take new or significantly changed symptoms seriously rather than assuming every symptom is due to FND.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team
{: #for-clinicians-and-the-care-team .shareable }

Explain the positive evidence supporting the diagnosis in terms suited to the symptom phenotype, and identify important differential and coexisting conditions. Distinguish observed task performance from sustained functional capacity, participation and quality of life. An examination finding may support diagnosis without measuring the person's total disability.

Be clear about the difference between established clinical observations, proposed mechanisms and individual formulation. Tailor safety-netting and referrals to changes in symptoms and individual risk. Discuss the diagnosis collaboratively, without equating symptom variability with intentional control. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3))
