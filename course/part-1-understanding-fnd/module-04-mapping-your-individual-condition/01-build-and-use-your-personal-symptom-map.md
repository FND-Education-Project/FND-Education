# Making Sense of Your Symptoms and Questions

> **Human-edited working draft:** The person-with-FND section reflects the project lead's edited material. Supporter, clinician and research sections need the usual editorial and clinical audits.

An FND diagnosis may be one part of a bigger health story. This lesson helps sort what has been diagnosed, what still needs assessment, and how timing may help explain an experience.

## For the Person With FND
{: #for-the-person-with-fnd .shareable }

### When your health story gets complicated

Imagine sitting down with a new doctor.

They ask how you've been doing.

Well, where do you begin?

Maybe your walking has been difficult. You've had migraine attacks, too. And there's that new symptom you've been meaning to ask about.

Some days have been better than others. A few details are important, but they're hard to remember on the spot.

Before you know it, your thoughts are playing hide-and-seek.

There's a way to make these conversations a little easier.

It's called a **personal symptom map**.

Think of it as a short guide to your health. It helps you keep track of what has been diagnosed, what you're still trying to understand and what makes everyday life more manageable.

You can use it when meeting a new clinician, talking with someone who supports you or deciding what to bring up at your next appointment.

It's also useful when memory and concentration are difficult.

One person in the FND community described their experience this way:

> “I can’t really tell the difference between whether something happened two weeks ago or four months ago.”

[Read their community post](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1mnw4fd/dae_have_functional_cognitive_disorder/).

Imagine how helpful a short written record could be when dates and memories get mixed together.

And here's the nice part: your map can be just one page.

You can add to it when something important changes. The aim is to make remembering easier, while leaving you free to get on with living your life.

A personal symptom map is a way of organizing your health information that we've introduced in this course. It can support conversations and decisions about care. Your healthcare team still provides the assessment needed to understand and diagnose symptoms. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3), [6](#citation-6))

### Giving everything its own place

Let's imagine someone whose leg weakness has been diagnosed as functional.

They also have migraine.

Recently, they've started experiencing dizziness that feels different from anything they've had before.

All three experiences matter. But they tell us different things.

Their leg weakness has been identified as part of their FND.

If their migraine has been diagnosed, it belongs with their other health conditions. If they're still being assessed for migraine, that question can stay open.

Doctors sometimes call conditions that occur alongside one another **coexisting conditions** or **comorbidities**.

And the new dizziness?

That belongs in a space marked **still to be assessed**.

There's nothing wrong with an unanswered question. Writing it down helps make sure it receives attention.

The same approach works for many different experiences.

You might live with functional seizures, movement changes, speech difficulties, sensory symptoms or problems with thinking and memory. Perhaps you experience several of these, or something entirely different.

Your map is about *your* particular combination.

It can also have room for medication changes, side effects or symptoms that may be linked to another condition.

Each piece gets its own place until you and your care team know more. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4))

### What happened before, during and after?

Sometimes understanding the order of events can be useful.

Imagine someone who experiences episodes of functional symptoms.

One evening, they sleep poorly.

The following afternoon, they notice a familiar change in their speech. Shortly afterwards, one of their usual episodes begins.

When it's over, they're exhausted.

There are three different things happening here.

The poor sleep came **before** the episode. It might be a *trigger* — something that increases the chance of symptoms under certain circumstances.

The speech change appeared as the episode was beginning. It might be a *warning sign*, showing that something familiar is already happening.

The exhaustion came **afterwards**. That's a *consequence* — something experienced as a result of the episode.

These words help describe when things happen. They also help us ask better questions.

Perhaps poor sleep is connected with more difficult days for this person. We'd need more information to understand that connection.

For some people, episodes may occur without a recognizable trigger or warning. Other symptoms may be present for much of the time, rather than appearing as distinct episodes, although their intensity can still vary.

Your experiences may follow a different pattern, and your map can reflect that.

You may also hear clinicians mention a **risk factor**, which can make a condition more likely, or a **maintaining factor**, which may contribute to symptoms continuing.

We explored these terms in [Lesson 7](../module-03-causes-mechanisms-and-honest-uncertainty/01-causes-risk-factors-triggers-and-maintaining-factors.md). Here, we're simply giving useful observations a place to live.

### A small place to start

What would you most want a clinician to know about your health?

Perhaps it's a symptom already diagnosed as FND, another health condition or a question that's still open.

You can write just one line about it. That's a useful beginning.

In [Lesson 11](02-building-and-using-your-personal-symptom-map.md), we'll look at what helps, what remains possible and how to build those notes into a simple map.

**Your health story is bigger than a page. The map helps you keep the important pieces within reach.**

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters
{: #for-family-friends-and-other-supporters .shareable }

### Knowing someone well is a starting point

You may know someone with FND so well that you can tell when they're having a difficult day before they've said a word.

You know what their usual symptoms look like. You may even recognize when something seems different.

But imagine a new symptom appears. Is this part of their FND, another health condition, or something that still needs checking?

Familiarity helps you notice changes. **The right medical words help you talk about what you've noticed.** And a clinician can help determine which explanation fits.

Let's explore a few of those words together.

### A diagnosis tells us what has been identified

Imagine someone whose leg weakness has been diagnosed as functional. They also live with migraine, and recently they've begun feeling dizzy in a new way.

It could be tempting to put everything under one label: *“That's their FND acting up again.”*

There is a more useful way to understand the situation.

The **functional leg weakness** has been identified as part of their FND. The clinician made that diagnosis using **positive signs**: examination findings that support FND, rather than simply finding nothing wrong on a scan.

Depending on the symptom, these signs may include differences in how a movement works during different parts of an examination. Those changes are involuntary. They help a trained clinician recognize a functional symptom; they're not home tests or a measure of someone's effort. (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

The **migraine** is a separate neurological condition if it has also been diagnosed. When two conditions occur in the same person, clinicians call them **coexisting conditions** or **comorbidities**.

A person can have both FND and migraine. Their symptoms may also affect one another in everyday life, while each condition still deserves its own care.

And the **new dizziness**?

For now, it's an **unresolved symptom** — something still awaiting assessment. It's perfectly reasonable to say, *“We're still finding out what this is.”*

Those three descriptions give everyone a clearer picture. They also leave room for new information. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4))

### When symptoms overlap

Some experiences, such as fatigue, pain or problems concentrating, can occur alongside FND. They may also relate to another illness, a medication effect, sleep difficulties or more than one influence.

This is where the term **associated symptom** can be helpful. It describes something that occurs alongside a condition without automatically deciding what caused it.

Think of it like hearing several instruments playing at once. You can recognize the music while still working out which instrument is making a particular sound.

The important question becomes, *“What do we already know about this symptom, and what still needs assessment?”*

If a clinician has explained which symptoms are part of the person's FND and which belong to another diagnosis, that explanation gives you a firmer starting point. New information may change the picture over time.

### Before, during and after: words that make a difference

Now imagine someone who experiences familiar episodes of functional symptoms.

They sleep poorly one night. The next day, they notice a speech change before an episode. Afterwards, they're exhausted.

You might say, *“It all happened around the seizure.”* That's understandable, but there are more precise ways to describe what happened.

The poor sleep could be a **possible trigger**: something that may increase the chance of an episode.

The speech change could be a **warning sign**: something that appears as the familiar episode is beginning.

The exhaustion afterwards could be a **consequence**: an effect of what happened.

The timing helps us describe an experience; it doesn't prove that poor sleep caused the episode, or that every speech change is functional.

Some people experience no recognizable warning or trigger. Other FND symptoms may be present much of the time, with their intensity changing rather than starting and stopping in separate episodes.

You may also hear **risk factor**, meaning something that makes a condition more likely, or **maintaining factor**, meaning something that may contribute to a symptom continuing. We introduced these ideas in Lesson 7.

With these words, you can say *“We noticed this happened before the episode”* instead of having to guess *“This must have caused it.”*

### Better questions lead to clearer answers

Suppose you're sitting together at an appointment, trying to understand which symptoms belong where.

A useful conversation might sound like this:

*“Which symptoms have been diagnosed as FND, and what positive signs support that diagnosis?”*

Then perhaps:

*“Which other conditions have been diagnosed, and which symptoms are still being investigated?”*

And finally:

*“If something changes, what should we watch for, and when should we seek medical assessment?”*

These questions invite the care team to explain its reasoning in words everyone can use.

Recognizing a familiar pattern can be reassuring. At the same time, a **new, severe or substantially different symptom** deserves appropriate medical assessment, even when the person already has an FND diagnosis.

You don't need a medical degree to offer valuable support. Sometimes what helps most is knowing the difference between *“We know,” “We suspect,”* and *“We need to find out.”*

In Lesson 11, we'll use that understanding to help organize the person's health information and make appointments easier.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team
{: #for-clinicians-and-the-care-team .shareable }

### Help keep clinical categories accurate

Identify phenomena supported by positive clinical evidence for FND. Keep comorbid diagnoses, associated symptoms, medication effects, unresolved presentations and possible contributors distinct. Describe what supports the current formulation and what might warrant reassessment. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4))

As Bennett et al. note:

> “Comorbidities are common in FND.”

*Research quotation retained for verification during the evidence audit.* (*citations* [1](#citation-1))

A map can organize the conversation while keeping the patient's subjective experience central. It is not a diagnostic instrument and cannot classify new symptoms without appropriate evaluation.

### Make the timeline meaningful

Clarify the timing of relevant events and symptoms when it helps answer a clinical question. Vulnerabilities, possible triggers, warning features, maintaining factors and consequences are distinct concepts. Temporal proximity or one repeated association does not independently establish causation.

Patients may have symptoms without identifiable triggers, including persistent or variably intense symptoms. The purpose of documenting timing is clinical understanding rather than assigning responsibility for symptoms.

Explain relevant red flags and plans for reassessment, including new or substantially altered symptoms. In the following lesson, the map becomes a practical communication and care-planning aid.

## Research and Sources

### What this evidence can and cannot establish

Clinical reviews and survey evidence support separating FND phenomena, coexisting conditions and concerns requiring assessment. These sources help frame medical reasoning but do not establish causation from the timing of symptoms or validate this course's symptom map as a diagnostic tool. The community quotation is an individual's experience, not diagnostic evidence. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [3](#citation-3), [4](#citation-4), [6](#citation-6))
