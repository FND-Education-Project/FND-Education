# Making Sense of Your Symptoms and Questions

> **Working draft:** The person-with-FND section was revised with the project lead. Other audience sections and research need the normal editorial and clinical audits.

An FND diagnosis may be one part of a bigger health story. This lesson helps sort what has been diagnosed, what still needs assessment, and how timing may help explain an experience.

## For the Person With FND
{: #for-the-person-with-fnd .shareable }

### When your health story gets complicated

Imagine sitting down with a new doctor.

They ask how you've been doing.

Well, where do you begin?

Maybe your walking has been difficult. You've had migraine attacks, too. And there's that new symptom you've been meaning to ask about.

Some days have been better than others. A few details are important, but they're hard to remember on the spot.

Before you know it, your thoughts are playing hide-and-seek.

There's a way to make these conversations a little easier.

It's called a **personal symptom map**.

Think of it as a short guide to your health. It helps you keep track of what has been diagnosed, what you're still trying to understand and what makes everyday life more manageable.

You can use it when meeting a new clinician, talking with someone who supports you or deciding what to bring up at your next appointment.

It's also useful when memory and concentration are difficult.

One person in the FND community described their experience this way:

> “I can’t really tell the difference between whether something happened two weeks ago or four months ago.”

[Read their community post](https://www.reddit.com/r/FND/comments/1mnw4fd/dae_have_functional_cognitive_disorder/).

Imagine how helpful a short written record could be when dates and memories get mixed together.

And here's the nice part: your map can be just one page.

You can add to it when something important changes. The aim is to make remembering easier, while leaving you free to get on with living your life.

A personal symptom map is a way of organizing your health information that we've introduced in this course. It can support conversations and decisions about care. Your healthcare team still provides the assessment needed to understand and diagnose symptoms. (*citations* [2](#citation-2), [3](#citation-3), [6](#citation-6))

### Giving everything its own place

Let's imagine someone whose leg weakness has been diagnosed as functional.

They also have migraine.

Recently, they've started experiencing dizziness that feels different from anything they've had before.

All three experiences matter. But they tell us different things.

Their leg weakness has been identified as part of their FND.

If their migraine has been diagnosed, it belongs with their other health conditions. If they're still being assessed for migraine, that question can stay open.

Doctors sometimes call conditions that occur alongside one another **coexisting conditions** or **comorbidities**.

And the new dizziness?

That belongs in a space marked **still to be assessed**.

There's nothing wrong with an unanswered question. Writing it down helps make sure it receives attention.

The same approach works for many different experiences.

You might live with functional seizures, movement changes, speech difficulties, sensory symptoms or problems with thinking and memory. Perhaps you experience several of these, or something entirely different.

Your map is about *your* particular combination.

It can also have room for medication changes, side effects or symptoms that may be linked to another condition.

Each piece gets its own place until you and your care team know more. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4))

### What happened before, during and after?

Sometimes understanding the order of events can be useful.

Imagine someone who experiences episodes of functional symptoms.

One evening, they sleep poorly.

The following afternoon, they notice a familiar change in their speech. Shortly afterwards, one of their usual episodes begins.

When it's over, they're exhausted.

There are three different things happening here.

The poor sleep came **before** the episode. It might be a *trigger* — something that increases the chance of symptoms under certain circumstances.

The speech change appeared as the episode was beginning. It might be a *warning sign*, showing that something familiar is already happening.

The exhaustion came **afterwards**. That's a *consequence* — something experienced as a result of the episode.

These words help describe when things happen. They also help us ask better questions.

Perhaps poor sleep is connected with more difficult days for this person. We'd need more information to understand that connection.

For some people, episodes may occur without a recognizable trigger or warning. Other symptoms may be present for much of the time, rather than appearing as distinct episodes, although their intensity can still vary.

Your experiences may follow a different pattern, and your map can reflect that.

You may also hear clinicians mention a **risk factor**, which can make a condition more likely, or a **maintaining factor**, which may contribute to symptoms continuing.

We explored these terms in [Lesson 7](../module-03-causes-mechanisms-and-honest-uncertainty/01-causes-risk-factors-triggers-and-maintaining-factors.md). Here, we're simply giving useful observations a place to live.

### A small place to start

What would you most want a clinician to know about your health?

Perhaps it's a symptom already diagnosed as FND, another health condition or a question that's still open.

You can write just one line about it. That's a useful beginning.

In [Lesson 11](02-building-and-using-your-personal-symptom-map.md), we'll look at what helps, what remains possible and how to build those notes into a simple map.

**Your health story is bigger than a page. The map helps you keep the important pieces within reach.**

## For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters
{: #for-family-friends-and-other-supporters .shareable }

### Two people can know different parts of the same story

Living alongside someone with FND can teach you a great deal about their usual symptoms. You might recognize a familiar episode, notice when something has changed, or remember details from a difficult day.

The person living with FND knows something equally important: **what the experience feels like from the inside**. They may recognize a change in pain, sensation, thinking or awareness that nobody else can see.

Those two perspectives can work together. The person describes their experience and what matters to them. A supporter, when invited, can add what they observed and help communicate it. **A clinician's role is to assess and diagnose**, using medical history, examination and other tests when needed.

Each role brings something different to the conversation.

And many people with FND manage their care **without a supporter**. These same questions are for them, too. They can explain what they experience, ask clinicians for clear answers and request accessible ways to communicate.

### Having the right words helps

In Lesson 10, we've been separating things that are known from things that are still being investigated. A few terms make those conversations more precise.

A symptom **diagnosed as FND** has been identified by a clinician, using *positive signs* when applicable: examination findings that support a functional diagnosis.

A **coexisting condition**, also called a *comorbidity*, is another condition the person has been diagnosed with. Migraine, for example, can exist alongside FND.

An **associated symptom** occurs alongside the condition but may have more than one possible explanation. An **unresolved symptom** still needs to be understood or assessed.

These are useful distinctions because a familiar FND diagnosis tells us something important about *some* symptoms. It doesn't settle every question about the person's health. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4))

Suppose the person says, *“This feels different from my usual symptoms.”* A supporter might say, *“I've noticed a change, too.”*

Neither person has to work out the diagnosis. Together, they've given the clinician a useful starting point.

### Sharing observations without deciding the cause

Supporters may see things a person has difficulty remembering, particularly around an episode. With permission, they can describe what happened before, during and after.

This is where familiar words from Lesson 7 help.

Something that may make an episode more likely is a **possible trigger**. A change that appears as an episode begins may be a **warning sign**. Something that happens afterwards is a **consequence**.

You might also hear about a **risk factor** or a **maintaining factor**. These describe possible influences, rather than something a supporter is expected to identify or prove.

An observation such as *“Their speech changed before the episode”* tells the care team what happened. Whether the speech change was part of that episode is a different question.

The person might add, *“I felt confused before I could speak.”* Both accounts can be valuable, even when they describe different parts of the experience.

And sometimes symptoms occur without a recognizable trigger or warning. That's useful information as well.

### When is a medical visit worth the cost?

For some people with FND, getting medical care is exhausting. The journey, bright lights, noise, waiting, pain or interruptions to rest can worsen symptoms. An emergency department can be particularly difficult.

That creates a real balancing act. A visit might answer an important question, but getting there can take a heavy toll. And because FND itself can cause such striking symptoms, it isn't always obvious when something else needs attention.

Imagine someone who has lived with familiar functional episodes for years. After one episode, they experience a new kind of severe pain. The FND is already well known, so it becomes the focus of the conversation. Only later is a separate physical injury recognized.

This example is adapted from a lived experience, with personal details left out. It illustrates a risk called **diagnostic overshadowing**: when one familiar diagnosis draws attention away from another possible explanation.

Think of a folder labelled *FND*. The label tells us something important about the pages already inside. But it can't tell us what's written on every new page.

A new medical problem does not make the person's established FND diagnosis any less real. It means that more than one condition may be present at the same time. In the same way, an FND diagnosis does not automatically explain every new pain, symptom or change. A new or different symptom may still need its own assessment.

So how does someone decide whether a medical visit is worth the effort?

Often the answer lies in **what has changed, how serious it seems, and how quickly an answer is needed**. A familiar fluctuation may already be covered by the person's care plan. A mild, non-urgent concern might be discussed by phone or at a planned appointment. Other changes—such as a serious injury, sudden stroke-like symptoms, concerning chest pain or difficulty breathing—can make prompt or emergency assessment important despite the burden of getting there.

These differences are worth talking through with a clinician *before* the next difficult day. Understanding which changes can wait, which call for advice and which need urgent care can reduce some of the guesswork.

### Making both perspectives count

The person with FND may be the first to notice, *“This feels different.”* A supporter, if one is involved, might remember how the person's usual episodes look or notice something new afterwards.

Together, those observations can make a useful distinction: *“The episode seemed familiar, but this part is new.”*

That simple sentence may help a clinician consider both the established FND and another possible cause. It's a way of making room for more than one explanation, rather than trying to decide the diagnosis at home.

Medical care can also be made more manageable. Sometimes a phone appointment, a written account, quieter surroundings or less repeated questioning can help the person's concerns reach a clinician without so much added strain.

## For Clinicians and the Care Team
{: #for-clinicians-and-the-care-team .shareable }

### What does this diagnosis establish?

An FND diagnosis is meaningful because it can be supported by **positive clinical evidence**. The strength of that evidence depends on the presenting symptom, the examination finding, its interpretation and the wider clinical picture. Bennett et al. (2021) and Espay et al. (2018) emphasize a positive, phenotype-specific approach rather than diagnosis by exclusion. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [7](#citation-7))

That principle becomes especially important when a patient presents with several difficulties.

Consider someone with positively diagnosed functional leg weakness, an independently diagnosed migraine disorder and newly reported dizziness.

The evidence supporting the leg weakness diagnosis remains relevant. It says something specific about that presentation.

The migraine diagnosis offers another established explanation for some experiences.

The dizziness is a separate clinical question until the history, examination and any indicated assessment clarify it.

**Evidence supporting one diagnosis is not, by itself, evidence explaining every symptom.** Keeping these levels of certainty distinct allows clinicians to retain confidence in an established FND diagnosis while considering other causes. Coexisting neurological or medical illness is well recognized in FND; the literature does not support treating FND and other disease as mutually exclusive. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4), [8](#citation-8))

### What would change the explanation?

A clinical formulation becomes more useful when its reasoning can be followed.

For each important presentation, the explanation can make clear what was observed, what diagnosis those findings support, what else remains plausible and what new evidence would lead to reassessment.

The distinction between **observation** and **inference** matters here.

A patient may report that episodes are more frequent after poor sleep. That is an observation about timing. Poor sleep as a causal trigger remains a possibility to explore, rather than a proven mechanism for that individual.

Likewise, a warning feature, a maintaining factor and a consequence describe different relationships. Some episodes have no recognizable warning or trigger; other FND symptoms are persistent or variable rather than episodic.

This measured approach keeps the patient's experience useful without expecting them to establish causation. It also helps avoid letting an appealing explanation become more certain than the findings allow.

### When an established diagnosis meets a new problem

Imagine a person with a well-characterized functional episode. Afterwards, they report severe new pain following a fall.

The episode may closely match their documented FND presentation. At the same time, the new pain and the possibility of injury raise a separate question.

If attention stays entirely on the established diagnosis, an additional physical problem could be missed. This is one way **diagnostic overshadowing** can occur.

The important reasoning is that two propositions can both be true: the familiar episode may be functional, and the subsequent pain may reflect a new injury. Assessing the second proposition on its own merits does not weaken the evidence for the first.

Reviews of FND in emergency settings emphasize attention to coexisting disease and to changes in presentation, alongside accurate recognition of established functional symptoms. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [8](#citation-8))

### Weighing the value and burden of assessment

There is also a risk on the other side of the balance.

Repeated investigations and emergency interventions may add little when a presentation is already well characterized. Some can cause harm, while the travel, sensory environment, waiting and recovery demands may substantially reduce the patient's functioning.

Mcloughlin et al. (2025) examine **iatrogenic harm**—harm related to medical care—in FND. This makes the burden of assessment part of the clinical reasoning, rather than an inconvenience outside it. (*citation* [9](#citation-9))

The value of another investigation therefore depends on the question it might answer.

A familiar episode covered by a suitable existing plan presents a different decision from sudden focal neurological symptoms, a significant injury or a materially changed event. The potential seriousness and time sensitivity of a new problem can outweigh the burdens of urgent assessment.

Between those situations, there may be room for telephone advice, planned review or targeted investigations. The reasoning should account for current risk, remaining uncertainty and whether further information would alter care. (*citations* [8](#citation-8), [9](#citation-9))

This is **proportionate assessment**: remaining alert to consequential change while avoiding investigations that are unlikely to help.

### Making the reasoning understandable

Patients and supporters can contribute crucial observations, yet the clinician carries responsibility for diagnostic interpretation.

An explanation might therefore connect three statements:

*“These examination findings support FND for your familiar weakness.”*

*“The pain after your fall is a separate concern, so we need to consider injury.”*

*“Here is why this level of assessment is appropriate, and what would change our plan.”*

That sequence shows how the evidence supports the decision. It leaves space for the patient's experience, including when they have no supporter, and makes uncertainty discussable without dismissing symptoms.

## Research and Sources

### What this evidence can and cannot establish

Bennett and Espay describe an approach to FND diagnosis based on positive clinical evidence, interpreted in the context of the individual presentation. Butler's survey documents associated symptoms and comorbidity in a selected respondent population; it cannot determine the cause of a particular person's new symptom. Finkelstein's emergency-department review discusses coexisting conditions and changed presentations, while Mcloughlin's review examines harm associated with medical interventions. Together these sources support careful, proportionate clinical reasoning—not a fixed testing schedule or a validated triage rule. The course's symptom map remains an educational communication aid. (*citations* [1](#citation-1), [4](#citation-4), [7](#citation-7), [8](#citation-8), [9](#citation-9))
