# Module 7 — Prediction, Body Signals, and Agency

**Part Two: Understanding Attention, Action, and Body Signals**<br>
**Status:** Approved syllabus direction; source review completed October 26, 2026; lesson authoring and independent clinical review pending.<br>
**Planned lessons:** 18–21 (future numbers, not yet live).

## Purpose

Help readers understand the brain's use of predictions, bodily and external sensory information, salience, arousal, and the sense of agency. Teach reasoning toward personal adaptations without offering a supposed universal formula for FND.

## Lesson 18 — The Predictive Brain
- How expectations prepare actions and help interpret changing information.
- The missing-stair example revisited: predictions and incoming evidence can differ.
- Basic predictive-processing ideas; why models, correlations and therapeutic analogies do not establish an individual's cause.
- Everyday applications without prescribing symptom control.

## Lesson 19 — Interoception, Proprioception, and Outside Senses
- **Interoception:** sensing and interpreting internal bodily conditions (for example breathing, hunger or heartbeat), much of which happens outside awareness. Awareness, confidence, accuracy and recorded brain responses are different measures.
- **Proprioception:** body position and movement sense.
- **Exteroception:** sensory information about the external world.
- Signal, attention, interpretation and cause are distinct; not every difficult body sensation has a functional explanation.
- Interoception, proprioception and outside senses interact; these are useful distinctions, not sealed systems. Research on heartbeat processing does not establish the state of every body-sensing process.
- Respect sensory accommodations; no forced exposure, breath-holding or unsupervised respiratory drills.

## Lesson 20 — Salience, Arousal, and Feedback
- **Salience:** what the brain regards as important enough to prioritize.
- **Arousal:** physiological and cognitive readiness, not equivalent to anxiety.
- Examples of interactions involving pain, exhaustion, attention, context, expectations and automatic responses.
- A feedback loop is not blame or proof that a person can turn symptoms off.
- Autonomic or systemic complaints also deserve medical evaluation when indicated.

## Lesson 21 — Sense of Agency, Learning, and Personal Adaptations
- Agency as the *experience* of initiating or controlling an action, distinct from intent, responsibility and actual capacity.
- Learning and expectations can shape later experiences; involuntary symptoms are not learned fakery.
- Integrate concepts through **understand → notice → consider → adapt → review**.
- Practice reasoning about low-risk, reversible adaptations; judge by access, ease, safety and quality of life, not cure claims.

## Teaching and review instructions

Use ordinary-language stories, carefully explained concepts, gentle humour, accurate illustrations and ample pacing. Clearly mark (a) established general nervous-system function, (b) FND group observations, (c) proposed mechanism, and (d) person-specific unknowns. Do not claim that prediction, emotional distress or interoception is abnormal in every person with FND.

**Supporters:** Respect autonomy and sensory preferences; never enforce an explanation or strategy.

**Clinicians:** Evaluate evidence and limits surrounding predictive processing, precision weighting, salience, interoception, agency, network interactions and dissociation. Distinguish theory, clinical observations and individualized formulation.

## Outcome

Learner can distinguish attention, prediction, bodily sensing, salience, arousal and agency and use the vocabulary to consider adaptations without self-diagnosis.
