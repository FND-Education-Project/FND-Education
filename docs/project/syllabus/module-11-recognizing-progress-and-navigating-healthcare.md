## Purpose

Help people recognize useful learning before symptom recovery, communicate needs and disagreements constructively in healthcare, and judge how real-world barriers affect whether an assessment may lead to appropriate accessible care. The lessons do not promise that understanding FND, speaking up, or making another referral will fix symptoms or create services that are unavailable.

## Approved lesson sequence

### [Lesson 33 — Recognizing How Far You've Come](../../../course/module-11-building-an-individual-treatment-team/01-who-may-be-on-the-treatment-team.md)

- **Person:** Callum uses what he has learned to understand a familiar speaking difficulty, communicate using his phone, and share observations with his father and teacher. Symptom improvement is not required for his understanding to matter.
- **Supporter:** Callum's father learns alongside him, respects ordinary family time and allows familiar symptoms to remain unexamined when Callum wants that.
- **Clinician:** A family doctor considers both immediate access to a school presentation and individualized assessment for functional communication rehabilitation; an accommodation is not automatically a barrier to recovery.

### [Lesson 34 — Working With Your Healthcare Professionals](../../../course/module-11-building-an-individual-treatment-team/02-coordination-shared-goals-and-care-without-local-specialists.md)

- **Person:** Nadia and Dr. Chen distinguish the purpose of a psychological referral from her unmet migraine treatment question; diagnostic overshadowing, ambiguity in clinical language and respectful disagreement matter.
- **Supporter:** Nadia's friend learns not to substitute her own opinions or speech for Nadia's, particularly when Nadia needs time and a written cue to recover a thought.
- **Clinician:** Dr. Chen revisits the clinical question, assesses worsening migraine on its own terms, and identifies a consent-based possible purpose for psychological support without assuming psychological causation.
- **Historical-source policy:** Where a historical examination paper contains stigmatizing language, the modern understanding of genuine and involuntary FND symptoms comes first. The shortest owner-approved historical quotation is retained with original-abstract wording verification; original full-methods access was unavailable; original-paper links are kept in internal notes, with unlinked plain-text public bibliography and stable ID.

### [Lesson 35 — Finding Your Way Through Healthcare Barriers](../../../course/module-11-building-an-individual-treatment-team/03-care-when-no-local-fnd-specialist-is-available.md)

- **Person:** Graham evaluates the financial, travel and practical burden of a distant specialist assessment and asks whether recommendations could lead to attainable treatment.
- **Supporter:** Lena considers offering one journey while being clear she cannot commit to frequent distant treatment visits.
- **Clinician:** Dr. Singh and the local physiotherapist assess whether specialist advice can be competently implemented locally, without assuming a written report equals supervised FND rehabilitation.

## Audience conventions and preservation

The **Person** sections retain the standard three final reflections, **Something to think about**, **How might that feel?**, and **One small step**. The project lead explicitly chose **not** to revise Lesson 34's existing `One small step` or Lesson 33's existing `How might that feel?` during Audit 1; keep those approved sentences. Person sections retain accessible conversation, realistic uncertainty, definitions where needed, and humour appropriate to the situation.

The **Supporter** sections preserve autonomy, consent and practical limits without making informal supporters responsible for clinical decisions or unavailable services. The **Clinician** sections preserve evidence-aware, case-specific clinical decisions; the clinical examples are fiction and do not establish treatment outcomes.
