# Module 6 — Attention and Automatic Control

**Part Two: Understanding How Symptoms Can Happen**  
**Status:** Approved syllabus direction; content, source checks, independent clinical review pending.  
**Planned lessons:** 15–17 (future numbers, not yet live).

## Purpose

Give readers a working understanding of attention and automatic control, and the ability to consider safe adaptations without assuming they have identified the cause of a symptom. Continue the introduction in Module 3 without simply repeating it.

## Lesson 15 — Attention: What the Brain Notices
- Selective, divided, sustained and internally versus externally directed attention.
- What changes when an experience moves into focus; distinguishing changes in attention from changes in the underlying body state.
- Finite cognitive resources, competing demands, fatigue, pain and sensory load.
- Everyday demonstrations (for example, noticing discomfort after a film) are *analogies*, not FND diagnostic tests.
- Reflection: what can be made easier by reducing competing demands?

## Lesson 16 — Automatic Control and Conscious Effort
- The brain's automatic coordination of familiar actions and the difference from deliberately guiding each step.
- Why close monitoring can interfere with fluency in some circumstances.
- Why movement briefly observed once may not be reliably available on demand, and variable ability is not evidence of deliberate production.
- Reflection: how can a task or environment support useful automatic patterns?

## Lesson 17 — Distraction, External Focus, and Context
- Distraction, attention redirection, task focus and rhythm: what differs and what overlaps.
- How these can sometimes alter movement, performance and subjective experience, with important limits across presentations.
- Why a shift with attention is not proof of FND or a home test.
- Explore one safe, reversible adaptation, with professional input when necessary.

## Teaching and review instructions

Each Person section: recognizable scene → concept → cautious FND relevance → one question → optional low-burden application. Age-accessible, humane tone; no rapid lists or pressure to improve. Use appropriate sources, distinguish observed effects from proposed mechanisms, and keep disability access available even when practicing rehabilitation.

**Supporters:** Offer only invited cues; do not conduct strength or distraction tests, monitor constantly or interpret symptom variability as choice.

**Clinicians:** Differentiate positive examination features from mechanism theory; assess safety, uncertainty, coexisting conditions and realistic task-based changes.

## Outcome

Learner can explain how attention and automatic action relate and can propose an adaptation while knowing it may not help, does not prove a diagnosis, and is not a test of effort.
