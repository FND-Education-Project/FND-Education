**Part Five: Living With FND**

## Purpose

Address the personal and relational consequences of FND without implying that positivity cures illness.

## Editorial handoff from Module 7 — conflict when symptoms interrupt communication

**Current curriculum position:** This legacy-numbered syllabus file describes the topic now placed at **Module 14**. Module 7 now includes a cognitive-difficulties lesson (Lesson 21) and an emotional-regulation lesson (Lesson 22); that lesson explains the normal processes, research uncertainty and why an involuntary symptom appearing during conflict is **not evidence of a convenient, deliberate escape**. Do not repeat the full neuroscience explanation here.

**Additional handoff from Module 7 Lesson 21 (November 3, 2026) — memory, trust and autonomy:** The approved Lesson 21 Supporter section explains that genuine cognitive difficulty can make someone depend heavily on others' confident recollections. It encourages supporters to welcome uncertainty, original records and an independent trusted perspective, and affirms the person's equal right to question accounts, disagree and decide for themselves. **Module 14 is responsible for the more detailed relational and safeguarding implications**, including respectful disagreement about shared history; unequal access to memory or records; maintaining decision-making autonomy; recognizing coercion, manipulation or gaslighting without assuming that all memory disagreements are abusive; independent sources of support where relationships may be unsafe; and consent-based, revisable shared records. Avoid implying that the person with FND is always mistaken, that the supporter is always correct, or that genuine coercion is simply a symptom. Explain how to handle disputes fairly without turning the partner into a memory adjudicator or the person into a credibility test. The earlier [shared-history editorial note](../../../reference/_internal/course/module-10-shared-history-memory-and-trust-placement-note.md) provides additional source context. No Module 14 reader-facing prose is approved by this handoff.

**Additional Module 7 Lesson 22 handoff — emotional changes, relationship safety and suicide risk (November 3, 2026):** The approved Lesson 22 Supporter prose uses a mother and adult daughter to explain that emotional intensity and regulation may be more difficult for some people with FND, without implying a universal symptom or causal mechanism. It acknowledges that understanding an emotion-related difficulty never requires a supporter to accept abuse or intimidation. It also briefly, carefully explains that the Nightscales et al. (2020) estimate of **20% relates to deaths among people with functional seizures who died before age 50 in the study**, not to an individual’s chance of dying by suicide, and encourages taking self-harm statements seriously and seeking urgent help when immediate danger exists. In Module 14, develop the **deeper** work of trust, mutual dignity, consent, safe boundaries, coercion and access to independent safeguarding support; do not treat emotional symptoms as proof of abuse or excuse abusive conduct. Coordinate any detailed suicide-risk explanation with the clinical/functional-seizure materials and mental-health crisis guidance rather than using a statistic to predict individual risk. Module 14 remains a working draft: this is an editorial handoff, not approval of its reader-facing sections.

**Module 14's practical teaching responsibility:** In a disagreement, cognitive difficulties, loss of words/speech, dissociation or functional seizures may interrupt the person's ability to continue before the issue is resolved. These are examples, **not** a required sequence or proof that conflict caused an episode. Both the neurological difficulty and the supporter's concerns must remain credible; the issue does not disappear, and the timing does not establish manipulation.

Build the Person and Supporter sections around a **shared negotiation of access to the conversation**, not only a pause-and-return script: discuss parts of a larger issue over several manageable occasions; make room for both people's needs, shared latitude, concessions and realistic boundaries; consider written or alternate communication by agreement; preserve safety and never pressure someone to demonstrate speech or cognition during symptoms. Equal consideration need not mean equal moment-to-moment capacity. Supporters, including spouses, parents and friends, have limits; people without a supporter still deserve communication accommodations and professional access.

**Clinical scope:** Consider symptom-related access barriers separately from emotional regulation or relationship conflict; evaluate relevant cognitive, speech, awareness and medical changes as indicated. Involve counselling/couples or family support when useful and consented to, without treating partner conflict as a diagnosis, a cause of FND, or proof of intent. Keep coercion, abuse and safeguarding concerns distinct from ordinary collaborative compromise.

## Essential knowledge

- Why losing abilities, plans, work, roles, or independence can produce real grief.
- How FND may change responsibilities between partners, relatives, friends, and caregivers.
- Why both the person with FND and the supporter may have fear, anger, sadness, or resentment.
- How honest communication and agreed boundaries may prevent unspoken strain from growing.
- Why intimacy and sexuality may need practical adaptation and open discussion.
- How parenting, family life, and friendship may continue in changed forms.
- Ways to respond to disbelief or insensitive comments without having to explain everything to everyone.
- Why isolation can worsen quality of life even when leaving home is difficult.
- How interests, pleasure, spirituality, creativity, and purpose may still matter without being presented as cures.
- When counselling, relationship help, safeguarding, or other support may be useful.

## For health professionals and readers wanting more detail

This section should focus on how clinicians can help the patient understand the subject and their own presentation, identify what may help, support symptom or functional improvement, and remain helpful when improvement is limited or not yet possible. It should include safety, accessibility, quality of life, and appropriate reassessment rather than treating symptom reduction as the only worthwhile outcome.

- Illness-related grief, ambiguous loss, identity disruption, role change, stigma, and social isolation.
- Dyadic adjustment, caregiver burden, family systems, expressed emotion, overprotection, invalidation, and autonomy.
- Intimacy, sexuality, parenting, dependent relationships, and culturally shaped family roles.
- Effects of disbelief, contested diagnosis, unemployment, inaccessible environments, and healthcare experiences on relationships.
- Communication interventions, peer support, couples or family therapy, and caregiver support with accurate evidence labelling.
- Distinguishing helpful family involvement from making relatives responsible for treatment delivery or symptom surveillance.
- Safeguarding, coercion, financial dependence, privacy, and supported decision-making.
- Outcomes involving connection, role participation, relationship quality, identity, and meaning rather than symptom reduction alone.

## Practical work

- Identify one relationship need, one boundary, and one valued role that can be preserved or adapted.

## Support-person focus

- Remaining a spouse, relative, or friend—not only a caregiver.
- Making time in which FND is not the central subject.

## Expected takeaway

The reader can acknowledge loss while protecting relationships, dignity, identity, and meaningful participation.
