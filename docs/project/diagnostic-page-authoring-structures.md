## How these structures differ

Symptom pages retain the person, supporter, clinician and research sections. Individual diagnostic-technique pages are clinician-focused and readable by other audiences, with topic navigation instead of repeated person/supporter sections. This is the explicit exception agreed in the September 23 discussion; recovery-page structure is unchanged.

## Symptom-page structure

1. Title, breadcrumb and accurate authorship/review banner.
2. **Quick Reference:** a very short semi-clinical symptom description, scope boundaries and links to relevant techniques with a short “when used” description. Use readable bullets when a table would crowd the page. Label signs, diagnostic criteria, observations, investigations and cautions accurately. Keep the full catalogue in the diagnostic index where appropriate.
3. **For the Person With FND:** what the symptom feels/looks like, its actual time course, what is understood or uncertain about it, what else can produce similar symptoms, what relevant tests answer, and what positive FND findings contribute. Introduce ideas before advice. Give a concise recovery link rather than another treatment lesson.
4. **For Family, Friends, and Other Supporters:** useful preparation, consensual observations, communication/access needs, support during the examination, understanding the explanation and follow-up. Keep this about assessment rather than reproducing recovery instructions.
5. **For Clinicians and the Care Team:** phenotype, symptom-specific differential and coexistence, selection of examinations/investigations, interpretation and uncertainty, documentation and explanation. Link detailed manoeuvres.
6. **Research and Sources:** claim-linked evidence notes and a standalone numbered citation table; distinguish research from lived experience.
7. Repeated audience links before each audience section and after sources. Finish with a correct next-page destination, related links and global navigation.

Keep a brief emergency/reassessment boundary where appropriate. A shared safety discussion does not replace symptom-specific safety information.

## Individual technique-page structure

1. Title, breadcrumb, draft/review status and a short purpose/scope statement.
2. Topic menu, repeated before each major section and after sources, each link on a separate line.
3. **Purpose and when it is used:** exact symptom appearance, clinical question and assessment type. Explain when it is unsuitable.
4. **Anatomy and physiology:** structures and normal function relevant to the manoeuvre; distinguish observation from a proposed FND mechanism.
5. **Preparation, consent and safety:** clinician role, positioning/equipment, pain and other confounders, stopping criteria and accessibility needs.
6. **How the clinician performs the assessment:** source-faithful sequence and precisely described comparisons. No home-testing invitations or hazardous demonstrations.
7. **Interpreting the findings:** positive, negative and indeterminate findings; alternative explanations; relation to the full assessment.
8. **Explaining the result:** short everyday-language example, avoiding implications about honesty, motivation or voluntary control.
9. **Evidence and limitations:** study design, sample and relevant subgroup, comparator, blinding, accuracy estimates where supported, reproducibility and generalizability. Identify gaps without inventing evidence.
10. **Media and accessibility:** only when useful; illustration/video scope, alt text, captions/transcript, consent and anatomical accuracy.
11. **Research and Sources:** numbered citations, full source table and stable central IDs.
12. Final topic menu; next technique or collection destination, symptom page, related recovery link where appropriate, and global navigation.

There are **no separate person-with-FND or supporter sections on technique pages**. The symptom page owns that context. Keep terminology accessible throughout, not only in the example explanation.

### Definition convention

Place a short blockquote definition immediately after the first necessary medical term. Use a plain label, not quotation marks suggesting a sourced quotation:

> **Term:** Brief everyday-language definition, limited to what the reader needs here.

Definitions are authored explanatory text, not patient or research quotations. They do not acquire a figure number merely by being blockquotes. Actual sourced quotations still follow the research/citation policy.

## Shared concept-page structure

Use a question-led introduction, enough explanation to stand alone, relevant examples across symptoms, and links back to symptom applications. Retain the existing audience-and-research format where useful for the different audiences. Avoid a shared page becoming a second recovery manual or duplicating the course verbatim.

## Clinician awareness and everyday impact

Apply the [shared project principles](core-principles.md#write-with-the-whole-clinical-team-in-mind) during planning and review. Identify likely professional users and include familiar language and decision points while keeping the page useful to the whole team. Do not add a separate section for every profession or reinstate person/supporter sections on individual technique pages.

On **symptom pages**, connect the clinical presentation to relevant daily function, quality of life and mental health, with sources and the person's perspective. Describe what brief or standard assessments may leave unanswered about variability, repeated activity, cognitive/sensory demands, recovery time and support needs.

On **technique pages**, state what the particular finding can establish and what it cannot establish about severity, endurance, safety, independence or participation. Include a concise relevant implication or follow-up question, then link to the symptom page for fuller life-impact context. A positive diagnostic finding is not a disability scale.

On **shared concept pages**, own the recurring distinctions between diagnosis, observed performance and everyday disability. Retain enough context on each symptom/technique page for safe interpretation. Research quality of life and mental health at the level where those claims belong, rather than duplicating unrelated studies in every manoeuvre page.

## Editorial safeguards

- Preserve human wording, lived-experience attribution and qualifications. Track relocation in the ownership map and pull request.
- Never infer a diagnosis or mechanism solely from improvement with a rehabilitation technique.
- Keep diagnostic observations, criteria, investigations and unsupported/historical cautions distinct. An inventory row is not automatically a validated test or a future page.
- Explain terms before using them to tell the reader what to do.
- Keep source IDs stable. Every published page has its own citations; update the central source-use record.
- Add only real destinations to navigation and site maps. Planned filenames remain code-formatted text in project documents until content is published.
- Check local links/anchors, actual rendered headings/menus, source-table continuity and next-page chains before review.
