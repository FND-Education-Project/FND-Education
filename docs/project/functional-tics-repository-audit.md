> **Planning report — September 17, 2026.** This audit identifies changes needed to give functional motor and vocal/phonic tic-like symptoms clear, accurate coverage across the repository. It does not diagnose an individual symptom or establish a treatment protocol. Clinical, lived-experience, accessibility and stigma review remain necessary before reader-facing publication.

## Implementation status — September 17, 2026

The paired [diagnostic draft](../../reference/diagnostic-signs/17-functional-tics-and-tic-like-symptoms.md) and [recovery overview](../../reference/recovery-techniques/17-functional-tics-and-tic-like-symptoms.md), course and access additions, glossary, citation records, indexes and illustration are now prepared for review. Detailed technique pages and printable resources remain deferred as recommended. Community quotations are explicitly unverified gaps; specialist, lived-experience and accessibility reviews remain pending. The recommendations below preserve the original audit plan.

## Recommendation

Add **Functional Tics and Tic-Like Symptoms** as the seventeenth paired symptom category in the diagnostic-sign and recovery collections. Cover motor and vocal/phonic symptoms together, including simple sounds and involuntary words or phrases. Keep functional tics distinct from functional jerks, speech or voice impairment, cough and primary tic disorders, while adding links where readers may reasonably enter through those topics.

The work should begin with one diagnostic page and one recovery overview. A detailed recovery subcollection should follow only after the overview list and its evidence labels have been reviewed. The present evidence does not support creating a large set of apparently independent techniques merely to match the page counts of other symptom collections.

This addition changes the current collection from 16 to 17 presentations. Statements about the **sixteen original recovery lists** remain historically correct and should be preserved as such. Functional tics had no separate baseline list when that historical set was created; its first reviewed recovery list should be recorded as a new post-baseline addition.

## What the audit found

Current coverage is too slight and is routed through neighboring symptoms:

- Module 1 lists “functional tics” without explaining motor or vocal/phonic forms.
- Module 7 mentions tic-like movement once in a clinician note, but gives readers no usable account of tic symptoms, assessment or recovery.
- The functional-jerks recovery overview includes “functional tic-like” among alternate descriptions. That can imply that jerks and tics are interchangeable.
- The speech and voice collection does not explain where involuntary sounds, words or phrases belong.
- Cough, facial-symptom and dystonia pages mention tic disorders only as parts of a differential diagnosis.
- There is no dedicated diagnostic page, recovery overview, glossary entry, index entry, recovery history record or searchable route for vocal, phonic or “verbal” tics.
- No current page explains coexistence with Tourette syndrome or another primary tic disorder.

The gap is material. Someone whose movements have become vocal may look under speech, voice or cough and never find the one existing mention under movement. They may also infer that a new vocal symptom proves or disproves a functional diagnosis. The repository should instead explain that vocal symptoms occur in both functional tic-like presentations and primary tic disorders, and that evolution from motor to vocal symptoms is not a self-diagnostic distinction.

## Scope and terminology

Use **Functional Tics and Tic-Like Symptoms** as the reader-facing title. In research discussion, retain the authors’ term **functional tic-like behaviours (FTLBs)** where needed, while explaining that “behaviours” is a clinical research term and does not mean deliberate action. Symptoms are experienced as involuntary and are real.

The core page should cover:

- **Motor symptoms:** simple or complex movements, including facial, head, limb, whole-body and self-injurious movements.
- **Vocal or phonic symptoms:** non-word sounds, throat clearing, changes in vocal output, words, phrases and complex vocalizations.
- **Search language:** “verbal tic” should lead readers to the page, although **vocal tic**, **phonic tic** or **tic-like vocalization** should be the preferred explanatory language.
- **Copro-, echo- and pali-phenomena:** define these without reproducing stigmatizing examples. Explain that coprolalia means involuntary socially inappropriate or taboo words or phrases; it is neither required for Tourette syndrome nor unique to it.
- **Tic attacks or clusters:** describe prolonged or intense episodes without automatically classifying every episode as a functional seizure.

Do not present functional tics as another name for functional myoclonus, functional speech disorder, functional cough, Tourette syndrome or intentional speech. A person may have more than one condition, including both a primary tic disorder and functional tic-like symptoms.

## Diagnostic page requirements

Create `reference/diagnostic-signs/17-functional-tics-and-tic-like-symptoms.md`. Append number 17 to preserve every current numbered URL, but place the link near jerks in reader-facing symptom lists.

The page should make these points explicit:

1. **Diagnosis is clinical and specialist-led.** It draws on history, developmental course, direct or video observation, the overall symptom pattern, examination where relevant, and consideration of coexisting conditions. There is no single laboratory test, video feature or online checklist that confirms the diagnosis.
2. **Use a constellation, not a stereotype.** Abrupt onset, rapid escalation, age, sex or gender, complex symptoms, copro-phenomena, a reported premonitory urge, suppressibility and social-media exposure have all been discussed in the literature. None should be presented alone as proof.
3. **Acknowledge disputed criteria.** The 2023 European consensus criteria were created by expert Delphi consensus and had not been prospectively tested for sensitivity or specificity. A 2024 critical review raised concerns about circular reasoning, weak clinical benchmarks and the difficulty of recognizing coexisting functional and neurodevelopmental tics. The page must not present the consensus criteria as a validated gold standard.
4. **Cover the full differential.** Discuss Tourette syndrome; persistent motor or vocal tic disorder; provisional tic disorder; stereotypies; compulsions; myoclonus; dystonia; seizures; medication or substance effects; and other neurological or medical conditions according to the presentation.
5. **Explain coexistence.** Functional symptoms can occur in someone with a childhood tic history or current primary tic disorder. The assessment may need to formulate more than one symptom process.
6. **Avoid causal shortcuts.** Social or video exposure may be relevant context for some people, but it is not proof of diagnosis or a sole cause. Stress, trauma, anxiety, autism, ADHD or other diagnoses must not be made prerequisites.
7. **Set a reassessment boundary.** A familiar tic pattern usually follows an individual plan. Sudden new symptoms with injury, breathing or swallowing risk, acute neurological change, altered mental state, or a possible medication reaction require appropriate medical assessment.

The diagnostic page should include a short “what this page cannot tell you” box and should never invite readers to reproduce or suppress symptoms to test themselves.

## Recovery overview requirements

Create `reference/recovery-techniques/17-functional-tics-and-tic-like-symptoms.md`. The page should state plainly that controlled treatment trials specific to functional tics were not available in the 2022 expert review. The published I-CBiT report describes eight young people in an uncontrolled case series; it is promising preliminary work, not proof of efficacy and not evidence that the same approach suits every age or presentation. Evidence for CBIT in primary tic disorders must not be silently generalized to functional tic-like symptoms.

The first recovery list should use evidence and purpose labels rather than treating every item as a separately proven technique:

| Proposed entry | Purpose | Evidence framing |
|---|---|---|
| Positive explanation and shared formulation | Validate symptoms, explain the diagnosis and agree what may be modifiable | Expert guidance; individual formulation required |
| Individual pattern, urge, context and response mapping | Understand the person’s symptom sequence and functional impact without constant monitoring or blame | Assessment and treatment-planning component |
| Clinician-led, individually formulated behavioral or psychological treatment | Select and adapt treatment; discuss I-CBiT where appropriate | Emerging evidence: uncontrolled series of eight young people |
| Participation, routine and valued-activity restoration | Reduce life restriction and measure function as well as symptom frequency | Rehabilitation principle; individual goals |
| Coexisting-condition and medication review | Address primary tic disorder, ADHD, OCD, anxiety, depression, other FND and medication effects when present | Coordinated care; not evidence that medication treats the functional mechanism |
| Motor and vocal symptom safety and communication plan | Reduce injury and preserve communication access | Care planning, not a distinct treatment |
| Family, school and workplace response plan | Prevent punishment, shame and forced public suppression; agree practical accommodations | Support and access planning |
| Tic-attack, cluster and flare plan | Prepare for intense or prolonged episodes and define reassessment thresholds | Safety and relapse planning |

Medication language must say that anti-tic medication may be indicated for a coexisting primary tic disorder or another condition; readers should not start, stop or change medication without the prescriber. Recovery content should not prescribe forced suppression, exposure or response prevention as a self-help exercise.

Do not create a detailed recovery folder until specialist and lived-experience reviewers have decided whether these entries should remain on one overview or be expanded. If detailed pages are later warranted, record which pages are treatment components, which are coordinated care and which are safety or access guidance. Record the reviewed overview as the first-version baseline so later page counts can be audited honestly.

## Files that do not need symptom-content changes

The static contact landing page in `docs/index.html`, puzzle files, general asset instructions and course modules with no diagnostic, treatment, communication, school/work or changing-symptom role do not need functional-tic additions. They still require routine link checks if shared navigation is generated or edited. This keeps the project from turning one missing symptom into unrelated repository-wide wording changes.

## Safety, dignity and editorial rules

- Never imply that a vocalization is chosen because it forms a recognizable word or phrase.
- Do not reproduce offensive words for vividness. Describe the type of symptom and its social impact.
- Do not frame a family member’s attention, a peer group or social media as blame. Interactions and exposures can be discussed as possible context within an individual formulation.
- Do not use sex, gender, age, autism, anxiety, trauma, a lack of childhood tics, copro-phenomena, absent urge or non-suppressibility as a shortcut to diagnosis.
- Do not demand suppression as a condition of school, work, care or public access. Any clinician-led behavioral work needs consent, a formulation and monitoring for distress or harm.
- Include adults. Much of the rapid-onset literature concerns adolescents and young adults and cannot be generalized to everyone.
- Discuss self-injury, falls, neck movements, driving, swallowing, breathing and object-throwing through individualized safety plans rather than sensational examples.
- Measure participation, distress, injury and access as well as tic frequency. Symptom reduction is not the only meaningful outcome.
- Separate evidence for primary tic disorders from evidence for functional tic-like symptoms, and state clearly when guidance rests on expert opinion or case reports.

## Review required before publication

The reader-facing pages should be reviewed by:

- a movement-disorder neurologist or clinician with tic expertise;
- an FND specialist, with both pediatric and adult perspectives represented;
- a psychologist or behavioral clinician familiar with tic disorders and FND;
- a speech-language pathologist for vocal symptoms and communication access;
- an occupational, education or workplace-access reviewer;
- people with functional motor and vocal tic-like symptoms;
- people with Tourette syndrome or another primary tic disorder, including people with both diagnoses;
- supporters or family members; and
- an accessibility and stigma reviewer.

Reviewers should be asked specifically whether the material makes symptoms feel involuntary and credible, distinguishes diagnosis from stereotypes, protects access to communication, and avoids blaming the person or people around them.

1. Draft and review the diagnostic page, recovery overview and glossary language together.
2. Add citation-index records and verify every diagnostic and treatment claim against its source.
3. Correct the jerks, speech/voice, cough and facial scope boundaries.
4. Expand Module 7 and update its illustration, navigation and syllabus references.
5. Add access guidance in Modules 9, 17, 20 and, if useful, 21.

7. Add verified lived-experience material after the clinical scope is stable.
8. Decide whether detailed recovery pages are warranted. If so, map each one to the reviewed first-version list and label treatment, care, safety and access pages separately.

## Acceptance checks

Implementation is complete only when:

- searching for `functional tic`, `tic-like`, `motor tic`, `vocal tic`, `phonic tic` and `verbal tic` leads to an appropriate dedicated route;
- no page treats tics as a synonym for jerks, speech disorder, cough or intentional behavior;
- motor and vocal/phonic symptoms receive equal core coverage;
- primary tic disorders and functional tic-like symptoms are distinguished without claiming that one feature proves the difference;
- coexistence is visible in diagnostic and recovery content;
- recovery claims identify case-series, consensus and care-planning evidence honestly;
- every current collection total says 17, while the provenance audit still identifies the original historical set of 16;
- new pages appear in all relevant indexes, navigation chains and the site map;
- citation records, page-use mappings, internal links and the revised SVG render correctly; and
- specialist, lived-experience, accessibility and stigma review status is visible.
