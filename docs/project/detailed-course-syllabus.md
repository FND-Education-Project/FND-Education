# Detailed Course Syllabus

## Living With FND: Knowledge, Skills, and Support

This document is the working outline for the full FND Education course. It identifies what each module is intended to cover; it is not itself finished educational content.

The course will be self-paced. Readers may follow it in order or enter through a symptom-specific pathway. Every module will include material for the person with FND and the people who support them.

## Course-wide outcomes

By the end of the course, a person with FND should be better able to:

- explain what research and clinical guidance say FND is—and what remains uncertain;
- understand how clinicians make a positive diagnosis;
- distinguish primary FND symptoms, associated symptoms, separate diagnoses, and meaningful medical changes;
- identify research-supported, guideline-based, and lived-experience-informed management ideas;
- participate in treatment and rehabilitation decisions;
- communicate needs, boundaries, and safety plans;
- build a personal reference for episodes, appointments, setbacks, and daily life.

A support person should be better able to:

- understand that functional symptoms are described as genuine and involuntary;
- respond calmly and safely without blame or overprotection;
- support independence, rehabilitation, communication, and medical reassessment;
- recognize the limits of the support-person role;
- protect their own health and the underlying relationship.

# Part One: Understanding FND

## Module 1 — What FND Is

### Purpose

Give readers a clear, evidence-traceable starting explanation without reducing FND to structural damage, “normal tests,” stress, trauma, or imagination.

### Main subjects

- meanings and limits of the word **functional**;
- current diagnostic terminology and historical terms;
- motor, seizure, sensory, cognitive, speech, and other symptom families;
- symptoms as genuine and involuntary;
- nervous-system function versus visible structural damage;
- why normal tests alone do not establish FND;
- common misconceptions, stigma, and misleading mind-versus-body language;
- variation in severity, duration, disability, and recovery.

### Practical work

- Prepare a short personal explanation of FND for family, friends, or service providers.
- Identify terms from the reader’s medical records that need clarification.

### Support-person focus

- Believing the person without pretending to know the cause.
- Validating symptoms without predicting recovery or reinforcing fear.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can describe FND in plain language while clearly identifying what comes from research and what remains uncertain.

## Module 2 — How FND Is Diagnosed

### Purpose

Explain positive diagnosis, appropriate investigation, and uncertainty without suggesting that every person has the same signs or needs the same tests.

### Main subjects

- clinical history and neurological examination;
- positive clinical signs and what they demonstrate;
- internal inconsistency, variability, distractibility, and preserved abilities;
- why inconsistency does not mean deliberate behaviour;
- roles of video, EEG, imaging, laboratory tests, and specialist assessment;
- functional seizures, epilepsy, and diagnostic overlap;
- FND alongside structural neurological or other medical disease;
- when a second opinion or reassessment may be reasonable;
- limitations, misdiagnosis, and diagnostic communication.

### Practical work

- Create a “How was my diagnosis established?” question sheet.
- Record which positive signs or test findings the clinician explained.

### Support-person focus

- Understanding positive signs without repeatedly testing the person.
- Helping record the clinician’s explanation and unresolved questions.

### Expected takeaway

The reader understands that FND should be diagnosed through positive evidence and appropriate clinical reasoning—not merely because testing was unrevealing.

## Module 3 — Causes, Mechanisms, and Honest Uncertainty

### Purpose

Present leading scientific models without turning any one model into a universal or proven cause.

### Main subjects

- the difference between cause, risk factor, trigger, mechanism, and perpetuating factor;
- predisposing, precipitating, and perpetuating frameworks and their limitations;
- brain-network research;
- predictive processing, attention, expectation, agency, and automatic control;
- symptom learning and neuroplasticity;
- physical illness, injury, migraine, pain, medication, fatigue, and sleep disruption as possible contributors;
- psychological stress, psychiatric conditions, trauma, and dissociation when relevant;
- why trauma or stress is neither required nor irrelevant to every person;
- limits of autonomic and polyvagal explanations;
- recognizing scientific uncertainty and exaggerated mechanism claims.

### Practical work

- Sort personal possibilities into known facts, plausible contributors, unanswered questions, and unsupported assumptions.

### Support-person focus

- Avoiding blame and unwanted searches for hidden trauma.
- Listening to the person’s understanding without enforcing a preferred theory.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can consider possible contributors without mistaking association or a useful therapeutic metaphor for established causation.

## Module 4 — Mapping Your Individual Condition

### Purpose

Help the reader organize a complicated condition into a useful personal map without self-diagnosing.

### Main subjects

- confirmed FND symptoms;
- associated or commonly co-occurring symptoms;
- separate confirmed diagnoses;
- symptoms still requiring assessment;
- medication, substance, sleep, pain, and environmental effects;
- triggers versus early warning signs;
- helpful strategies and preserved abilities;
- daily, weekly, and situational variation;
- avoiding excessive monitoring and false certainty.

### Practical work

- Complete a one-page symptom and comorbidity map.
- Select a small number of patterns worth tracking.

### Support-person focus

- Contributing observations without overruling the person’s account.
- Helping identify patterns without turning daily life into surveillance.

### Expected takeaway

The reader has an organized, updateable picture of what is known, suspected, changing, and helpful.

# Part Two: Safety and Symptom Knowledge

## Module 5 — Medical Safety and New Symptoms

### Purpose

Reduce both diagnostic overshadowing and unnecessary emergency responses by teaching individualized safety planning.

### Main subjects

- why FND does not remove ordinary medical risk;
- established personal patterns versus new or substantially changed symptoms;
- injury, breathing problems, prolonged unresponsiveness, and sudden neurological change;
- red flags and the limits of generic online lists;
- when an emergency plan should override general advice;
- communicating with emergency personnel;
- risks of both over-treatment and dismissal;
- keeping emergency information accurate and concise.

### Practical work

- Draft an emergency information sheet with clinician-reviewed criteria where available.

### Support-person focus

- Following the agreed plan while watching for unusual features.
- Describing observations calmly and avoiding diagnostic arguments during an emergency.

### Expected takeaway

The reader understands that safety depends on the person’s established pattern, current circumstances, and professional plan—not on the FND label alone.

## Module 6 — Functional Seizures and Episodic Symptoms

### Purpose

Explain functional seizures and related episodes while supporting safe, individualized responses.

### Main subjects

- functional seizures, dissociative seizures, PNES, and terminology;
- diagnosis using history, witness information, video, and video-EEG where appropriate;
- differences from epilepsy and the possibility of having both;
- common episode forms and wide individual variation;
- warnings, triggers, loss of awareness, movement, immobility, and recovery;
- injury reduction and environmental safety;
- when emergency help may be needed;
- documenting useful observations without constant surveillance;
- evidence for treatments and major research gaps.

### Practical work

- Create an individualized episode plan and recovery preferences sheet.

### Support-person focus

- Protecting from injury, timing and observing, avoiding restraint, and never placing objects in the mouth.
- Reducing crowds, repeated questioning, and panic during recovery.

### Expected takeaway

The person and supporter have a shared episode response that reflects the individual’s diagnosis and medical guidance.

## Module 7 — Functional Movement, Weakness, and Gait Symptoms

### Purpose

Explain functional motor symptoms and introduce evidence-informed rehabilitation principles.

### Main subjects

- functional weakness and paralysis;
- tremor, jerks, spasms, dystonia, and gait disturbance;
- falls and injury risk;
- positive motor signs;
- automatic versus consciously controlled movement;
- redirected attention, rhythm, imagery, and meaningful tasks;
- roles of FND-informed physiotherapy and occupational therapy;
- deconditioning, pain, fatigue, and mobility aids;
- graded practice without force or shame.

### Practical work

- Identify preserved or more automatic movements to discuss with a therapist.
- Create a safe practice and fall-response plan.

### Support-person focus

- Using only agreed cues.
- Supporting safe independence without pulling, forcing, or repeatedly testing strength.

### Expected takeaway

The reader understands why motor retraining often works with automatic movement and redirected attention rather than simply trying harder.

## Module 8 — Sensory, Visual, Balance, and Dizziness Symptoms

### Purpose

Organize several frequently overlapping symptom areas and clarify when adaptations, rehabilitation, or further assessment may be appropriate.

### Main subjects

- numbness, altered sensation, pain, and hypersensitivity;
- functional visual symptoms and appropriate eye assessment;
- dizziness, balance problems, and persistent postural-perceptual dizziness;
- vestibular and migraine overlap;
- sensory overload, photophobia, phonophobia, and environmental strain;
- accommodations versus total avoidance;
- gradual exposure and its limits;
- fall prevention and mobility safety;
- research gaps in severe sensory disability.

### Practical work

- Make a sensory-load and adaptation map.
- Identify one reversible environmental change and one question for a clinician.

### Support-person focus

- Reducing avoidable strain without abruptly exposing the person to triggers.
- Helping build gradual plans that respect severity and consent.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can separate immediate accessibility needs, possible rehabilitation, and reasons for specialist assessment.

## Module 9 — Speech, Voice, Swallowing, and Breathing Symptoms

### Purpose

Explain communication and upper-airway symptoms while giving safety issues appropriate weight.

### Main subjects

- functional speech, voice, language-access, and fluency symptoms;
- mutism, dysphonia, stuttering, accents, and word blocking;
- functional swallowing symptoms and globus;
- cough, breathing-pattern symptoms, and upper-airway presentations;
- automatic speech, voice, rhythm, and distraction strategies;
- roles of speech-language pathology and respiratory or swallowing assessment;
- choking, aspiration, respiratory distress, and other red flags;
- communication access during severe symptoms.

### Practical work

- Prepare a communication card or alternative communication plan.
- Record swallowing or breathing questions for appropriate clinicians.

### Support-person focus

- Allowing time and using agreed alternatives without automatically speaking for the person.
- Responding to breathing or swallowing danger rather than assuming it is functional.

### Expected takeaway

The reader knows that functional communication symptoms may be treatable while swallowing and breathing safety still require careful individualized assessment.

# Part Three: Common Non-Motor Difficulties

## Module 10 — Cognition, Memory, and Dissociation

### Purpose

Explain cognitive symptoms without treating every memory difficulty as either neurodegeneration or “just FND.”

### Main subjects

- attention, working memory, processing speed, and executive function;
- encoding, storage, and retrieval;
- episodic-memory complaints, semantic retrieval, and word finding;
- functional cognitive symptoms and Functional Cognitive Disorder;
- dissociation, altered awareness, depersonalization, and derealization;
- effects of migraine, pain, sleep, fatigue, medication, mood, and sensory overload;
- formal cognitive assessment and its limitations;
- meaningful cognitive change and reasons for reassessment;
- external memory supports and reduced cognitive load.

### Practical work

- Build a small external-memory system using consistent locations, written summaries, alarms, and one-step prompts.

### Support-person focus

- Cueing without testing, shaming, or removing autonomy.
- Giving one subject at a time and allowing additional processing time.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can use practical cognitive supports while preserving appropriate investigation of other contributors.

## Module 11 — Pain, Migraine, Fatigue, and Sleep

### Purpose

Show how these disabling problems may accompany, trigger, maintain, or remain distinct from FND.

### Main subjects

- chronic pain and central sensitization concepts;
- migraine as a separate neurological diagnosis and possible FND trigger;
- fatigue as a symptom versus a separate condition;
- post-activity worsening and uncertainty around pacing evidence;
- insomnia, fragmented sleep, circadian problems, and sleep disorders;
- medication and substance effects;
- interactions among pain, attention, cognition, movement, and seizures;
- treatable contributors and appropriate referrals;
- limits of “push through” and “avoid everything” approaches.

### Practical work

- Map interactions among sleep, pain, migraine, fatigue, activity, and FND symptoms.
- Select one measurable question for a clinician rather than tracking everything.

### Support-person focus

- Respecting pain and fatigue without assuming permanent incapacity.
- Helping plan within available capacity without policing activity.

### Expected takeaway

The reader understands why these problems deserve care in their own right and should not automatically be absorbed into the FND diagnosis.

## Module 12 — Autonomic and Whole-Body Symptoms

### Purpose

Explain common whole-body complaints while avoiding unsupported claims that all are caused by FND or autonomic “dysregulation.”

### Main subjects

- sweating, temperature changes, nausea, bowel symptoms, dizziness, and palpitations;
- ordinary autonomic physiology;
- effects of pain, migraine, sleep, medication, dehydration, and illness;
- overlap with recognized autonomic disorders;
- what FND research has and has not established;
- objective measurements and their limitations;
- symptom tracking without obsessive monitoring;
- questions that may guide primary-care or specialist assessment.

### Practical work

- Create a brief pattern record including context, duration, medication changes, and objective data only where useful.

### Support-person focus

- Helping document concerning patterns without dismissing them as anxiety or FND.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can discuss autonomic symptoms more precisely while remaining open to several possible explanations.

# Part Four: Treatment and Rehabilitation

## Module 13 — Building an Individual Treatment Team

### Purpose

Help readers understand professional roles and assemble realistic care when specialist FND services are limited.

### Main subjects

- neurologist and primary-care responsibilities;
- FND-informed physiotherapy and occupational therapy;
- speech-language therapy;
- psychology, psychiatry, and neuropsychology;
- rehabilitation, pain, migraine, sleep, and other specialists;
- care coordination and responsibility gaps;
- recognizing relevant FND competence;
- telehealth, local clinicians, and sharing professional resources;
- treatment goals and deciding who handles which problem.

### Practical work

- Build a care-team map showing confirmed responsibilities, gaps, and next questions.

### Support-person focus

- Attending when invited as a witness and memory aid.
- Supporting coordination without taking control from the person.

### Expected takeaway

The reader has a clearer plan for which professional may address each need and where coordination is missing.

## Module 14 — Rehabilitation and Neuroplastic Change

### Purpose

Explain rehabilitation learning while correcting claims that neuroplasticity guarantees recovery.

### Main subjects

- practice, repetition, attention, expectation, and nervous-system learning;
- automatic and goal-directed movement;
- meaningful tasks and external focus;
- graded rehabilitation and baseline selection;
- feedback, confidence, and reducing threat;
- variability, plateaus, and setbacks;
- outcome evidence and its limitations;
- why improvement does not prove voluntary control;
- cure claims and oversimplified “rewiring” language.

### Practical work

- Choose one functional goal and define a small, repeatable practice with stop and review criteria.

### Support-person focus

- Encouraging practice without becoming the therapist.
- Praising effort, strategy, and participation rather than demanding normal performance.

### Expected takeaway

The reader understands rehabilitation as individualized learning and practice, not a test of willpower or a guaranteed cure.

## Module 15 — Pacing, Activity, and the Boom-and-Bust Cycle

### Purpose

Help readers plan activity amid variable symptoms while being honest about limited FND-specific pacing research.

### Main subjects

- baseline activity and variability;
- planned exertion, ordinary activity, and rehabilitation practice;
- physical, cognitive, emotional, and sensory load;
- restorative rest versus isolation or avoidance;
- flexible ranges rather than rigid quotas;
- post-activity worsening and delayed effects;
- adapting after a flare;
- gradually building capacity where possible;
- avoiding moral judgments about rest and productivity.

### Practical work

- Create a flexible activity range and a simple plan for high-, medium-, and low-capacity days.

### Support-person focus

- Helping plan without policing.
- Noticing agreed early overload signs without making assumptions.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can test a flexible approach that respects current capacity while preserving meaningful activity and rehabilitation goals.

## Module 16 — Psychological Treatment Without Blame

### Purpose

Explain why psychological treatment can be relevant without defining FND as imaginary or universally psychological.

### Main subjects

- what psychological therapy can and cannot establish about causation;
- cognitive-behavioural approaches and functional seizures evidence;
- symptom fear, attention, avoidance, mood, and coping;
- acceptance-based and mindfulness approaches;
- treatment of depression, anxiety, PTSD, or other conditions when present;
- trauma-focused treatment only when indicated and acceptable;
- choosing goals and measuring benefit;
- consent and the right to decline an unsuitable therapy;
- warning signs of coercive or unsupported hidden-cause models.

### Practical work

- Prepare questions for evaluating a therapist’s FND knowledge, approach, goals, and safety.

### Support-person focus

- Supporting treatment choices without treating therapy as confession or proof of a psychological cause.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can consider psychological support on its actual evidence and goals, without blame or forced explanations.

# Part Five: Living With FND

## Module 17 — Daily Living, Accessibility, and Equipment

### Purpose

Support present safety and participation while leaving room for reassessment and rehabilitation.

### Main subjects

- personal care, cooking, cleaning, and household activities;
- energy, cognitive, and sensory adaptations;
- canes, walkers, wheelchairs, and other mobility aids;
- seating, positioning, fall, and seizure safety;
- home modifications and communication aids;
- evidence gaps and conflicting opinions about equipment;
- balancing current access with long-term goals;
- professional assessment, funding, and periodic review;
- dignity, privacy, and choice.

### Practical work

- Conduct a task-and-environment review: barrier, risk, current workaround, possible adaptation, and professional input needed.

### Support-person focus

- Asking before helping and avoiding automatic takeover.
- Recognizing when a task is unsafe for either person.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can evaluate an aid by safety, access, independence, effort, personal goals, and planned reassessment—not ideology.

## Module 18 — Relationships, Identity, and Grief

### Purpose

Address the personal and relational consequences of FND without implying that positivity cures illness.

### Main subjects

- grief for abilities, roles, plans, and identity;
- relationships altered by disability and caregiving;
- intimacy, sexuality, and communication;
- parenting and family responsibilities;
- friendship, disbelief, and social isolation;
- boundaries and responding to insensitive comments;
- interests, pleasure, spirituality, and meaning;
- identity beyond productivity and symptoms;
- relationship or mental-health help when needed.

### Practical work

- Identify one relationship need, one boundary, and one valued role that can be preserved or adapted.

### Support-person focus

- Remaining a spouse, relative, or friend—not only a caregiver.
- Making time in which FND is not the central subject.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can acknowledge loss while protecting relationships, dignity, identity, and meaningful participation.

## Module 19 — Healthcare Communication and Self-Advocacy

### Purpose

Make appointments and medical records more accurate, efficient, and usable.

### Main subjects

- concise medical histories and problem lists;
- separating confirmed diagnoses, clinician opinions, and personal hypotheses;
- describing patterns, duration, impact, and change;
- preparing priorities and questions;
- requesting written instructions and accessible communication;
- correcting inaccurate records;
- seeking another opinion respectfully;
- responding to stigma and diagnostic overshadowing;
- support-person roles and consent;
- limits of online information and self-advocacy.

### Practical work

- Create a one-page appointment summary and after-appointment record.

### Support-person focus

- Serving as witness, note-taker, and memory aid when invited without speaking over the person.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can present complex information clearly and leave appointments with decisions, responsibilities, and unanswered questions recorded.

## Module 20 — Work, Disability, and Community Participation

### Purpose

Address meaningful participation across a wide range of ability without making employment the sole measure of recovery.

### Main subjects

- return-to-work or education planning where appropriate;
- accommodations and variable capacity;
- disability applications and functional evidence;
- transportation and mobility barriers;
- financial and administrative burden;
- volunteering, hobbies, advocacy, and community roles;
- severe or long-term disability;
- balancing present limitations with future possibilities;
- dignity and contribution outside paid work.

### Practical work

- Map desired roles, barriers, accommodations, supports, and realistic next steps.

### Support-person focus

- Respecting both rehabilitation hopes and current disability.
- Avoiding employment or productivity as a measure of worth.

### Expected takeaway

The reader can define participation and contribution according to personal values, access, safety, and capacity.

# Part Six: Long-Term Management

## Module 21 — Setbacks, Relapse, and Changing Symptoms

### Purpose

Prepare readers to respond to fluctuation without assuming either disaster or “just FND.”

### Main subjects

- common patterns of fluctuation;
- temporary setback versus sustained change;
- possible accumulating physical, cognitive, emotional, and environmental pressures;
- returning to previously useful strategies;
- temporarily adjusting activity or rehabilitation;
- injury and medical reassessment;
- updating the episode and safety plan;
- emotional effects of relapse;
- avoiding catastrophic conclusions after one difficult period.

### Practical work

- Write a tiered setback plan: early signs, immediate response, reassessment criteria, and recovery review.

### Support-person focus

- Using the written plan instead of improvising during distress.
- Offering reminders without claiming to know why symptoms worsened.

### Expected takeaway

The reader has a plan that supports calm adaptation while preserving medical vigilance.

## Module 22 — Building Your Personal FND Handbook

### Purpose

Combine the course’s practical work into one accessible personal reference.

### Handbook sections

- how the diagnosis was established;
- confirmed medical conditions and current uncertainties;
- primary symptoms and established patterns;
- warning signs and common triggers;
- helpful and unhelpful responses;
- communication, sensory, cognitive, and mobility needs;
- functional-seizure or episode plan;
- emergency criteria;
- medication and treatment record;
- care-team roles and contact information;
- current rehabilitation goals;
- supporter instructions and consent boundaries;
- appointment preparation pages;
- setback and relapse plan;
- review dates and questions for future care.

### Practical work

- Assemble, test, and revise the handbook in print and/or accessible digital form.

### Support-person focus

- Learning where the handbook is, how to use it, and what remains the person’s decision.

### Expected takeaway

The reader has a concise reference that reduces dependence on memory during symptoms, appointments, and emergencies.

## Module 23 — Reviewing Progress

### Purpose

Evaluate the course by knowledge, safety, participation, confidence, and support—not only symptom counts.

### Main subjects

- knowledge and ability to explain the condition;
- confidence responding to symptoms and change;
- safety planning;
- independence, accessibility, and participation;
- communication with clinicians;
- use of practical strategies;
- symptom frequency, severity, or recovery where meaningful;
- support-person confidence, burden, and boundaries;
- what did not help or caused difficulty;
- goals and questions for the next three months.

### Practical work

- Complete a course review and update the Personal FND Handbook.

### Support-person focus

- Review what support is useful, unwanted, unavailable, or unsustainable.

### Expected takeaway

The person and supporter can recognize progress, unmet needs, and next steps without treating recovery as a pass-or-fail outcome.

# Symptom-Specific Pathways

Pathways will gather relevant course sections into shorter routes for readers who need information about one problem first.

Planned pathways:

- functional seizures, dissociative seizures, PNES, and altered-awareness episodes;
- functional movement disorder, tremor, jerks, and dystonia;
- functional weakness, paralysis, gait disorder, and falls;
- speech, voice, swallowing, breathing, and communication symptoms;
- sensory, visual, dizziness, and balance symptoms;
- cognition, memory, brain fog, and Functional Cognitive Disorder;
- dissociation;
- chronic pain;
- migraine;
- fatigue;
- sleep problems;
- severe or long-term disability;
- children and adolescents with FND;
- family members, caregivers, and other support persons.

# Support-Person Mini-Course

## Lesson 1 — Believe, Listen, and Learn

- evidence and guidance concerning genuine, involuntary symptoms;
- listening to the person’s account;
- avoiding interrogation, blame, and unsolicited causal theories;
- validation without unrealistic promises.

## Lesson 2 — Safe Help During Episodes

- individualized episode plans;
- injury reduction and observation;
- emergency criteria;
- calm recovery support;
- actions to avoid.

## Lesson 3 — Supporting Rehabilitation

- independence and consent;
- agreed cues and strategies;
- encouraging without pressuring;
- helping without becoming the therapist;
- adapting when capacity changes.

## Lesson 4 — Communication and Cognitive Support

- written reminders and summaries;
- one subject or instruction at a time;
- additional processing time;
- alternative communication;
- supporting decisions without removing autonomy.

## Lesson 5 — Boundaries and Caregiver Health

- recognizing overload and burnout;
- sharing responsibilities;
- realistic limits and respite;
- safety for both people;
- preserving the underlying relationship.

## Lesson 6 — Planning Together

- episode and emergency plans;
- appointment roles;
- daily support preferences;
- privacy and consent;
- setback plans;
- regular review and revision.

# Standard Components of Each Module

Each completed module will follow the project’s standard educational-page structure and will also provide:

- a short person-with-FND learning goal;
- a short support-person learning goal;
- one practical worksheet, plan, or exercise;
- practical at-home ideas separated by evidence source;
- safety and reassessment considerations;
- optional deeper research notes and citations;
- a plain-language summary suitable for printing;
- links to relevant pathways, glossary terms, and resources.
