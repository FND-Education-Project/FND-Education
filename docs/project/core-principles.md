# Core Principles

These principles guide how FND Education will research, write, and present material.

## Claims should come from identifiable sources

Important claims should be traceable to research, professional guidance, or another clearly named source. Readers should not have to accept a statement simply because FND Education says it.

## Certainty should match the evidence

Research findings, clinical consensus, hypotheses, individual clinical models, and lived experience are different kinds of information. They should not be written as though they carry equal evidentiary weight.

## Symptoms are genuine and involuntary

Research and clinical guidance describe functional symptoms as genuine and involuntary. Educational pages should show readers the evidence and clinical reasoning behind this rather than asking them to accept it on authority.

## Diagnosis should be explained through positive evidence

Modern clinical literature emphasizes positive diagnostic features of FND rather than treating it simply as what remains after normal tests. The project will explain the evidence for this approach and its limitations.

## No single theory explains every person

FND is heterogeneous. Proposed mechanisms and explanatory models should be presented with competing models, limitations, and unanswered questions.

## Trauma and psychological stress should not be assumed

Research may find trauma, stress, psychiatric symptoms, or adverse experiences relevant in some groups or individuals. That does not establish them as necessary causes for every person. Pages should distinguish association, risk factor, trigger, perpetuating factor, and cause.

## FND should not automatically explain every symptom

People with FND may also have other neurological and medical conditions. Educational pages should guard against diagnostic overshadowing and identify situations that warrant medical reassessment.

## Treatment information should preserve uncertainty and individual differences

Treatment studies show what helped groups under particular conditions. They cannot guarantee what will help an individual. Pages should explain both the supporting evidence and its limitations.

## Practical information matters

Explaining a symptom without helping the reader identify safe next steps leaves an important need unmet. Symptom and management pages will include practical at-home ideas, their source, their evidence level, relevant cautions, and reasons to seek professional help.

## Support should preserve autonomy

Information for support persons should improve safety, understanding, confidence, and independence. It should not turn a family member into a clinician, therapist, or supervisor.
