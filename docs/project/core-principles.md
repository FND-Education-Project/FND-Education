# Core Principles

These principles guide how FND Education will research, write, and present material.

## Claims should come from identifiable sources

Important claims should be traceable to research, professional guidance, or another clearly named source. Readers should not have to accept a statement simply because FND Education says it.

## Certainty should match the evidence

Research findings, clinical consensus, hypotheses, individual clinical models, and lived experience are different kinds of information. They should not be written as though they carry equal evidentiary weight.

## Symptoms are genuine and involuntary

Research and clinical guidance describe functional symptoms as genuine and involuntary. Educational pages should show readers the evidence and clinical reasoning behind this rather than asking them to accept it on authority.

## Diagnosis should be explained through positive evidence

Modern clinical literature emphasizes positive diagnostic features of FND rather than treating it simply as what remains after normal tests. The project will explain the evidence for this approach and its limitations.

## No single theory explains every person

FND is heterogeneous. Proposed mechanisms and explanatory models should be presented with competing models, limitations, and unanswered questions.

## Trauma and psychological stress should not be assumed

Research may find trauma, stress, psychiatric symptoms, or adverse experiences relevant in some groups or individuals. That does not establish them as necessary causes for every person. Pages should distinguish association, risk factor, trigger, perpetuating factor, and cause.

## FND should not automatically explain every symptom

People with FND may also have other neurological and medical conditions. Educational pages should guard against diagnostic overshadowing and identify situations that warrant medical reassessment.

## Treatment information should preserve uncertainty and individual differences

Treatment studies show what helped groups under particular conditions. They cannot guarantee what will help an individual. Pages should explain both the supporting evidence and its limitations.

## Practical information matters

Explaining a symptom without helping the reader identify safe next steps leaves an important need unmet. Symptom and management pages will include practical at-home ideas, their source, their evidence level, relevant cautions, and reasons to seek professional help.

## Support should preserve autonomy

Information for support persons should improve safety, understanding, confidence, and independence. It should not turn a family member into a clinician, therapist, or supervisor.

## Clinical information should remain useful to the patient

Material for clinicians should be organized around how the clinician can help the patient understand the condition and their own presentation, identify what may help, and support realistic improvement. When a symptom persists or is unlikely to improve, useful care may still include validation, symptom relief, safety planning, rehabilitation where appropriate, accessibility, participation, communication, quality of life, supporter needs, and agreed reassessment. Limited recovery must not be treated as a failure of effort or a reason to withdraw appropriate care.

## Write with the whole clinical team in mind

Apply these principles when creating diagnostic pages, editing recovery pages, and revising other clinical educational content. All clinical content should remain useful across professions. During planning, identify who is most likely to use the particular page and the decisions they need to make. Include terminology, examples and practical questions familiar to those readers, defining specialist terms for everyone else. This is a writing aid, not a division of the content into professional silos or a requirement for a separate section for every profession.

| Professional perspective | Familiar questions and language to consider when relevant |
|---|---|
| Neurology | Symptom phenotype, positive findings, differential diagnosis, coexistence, interpretation and explanation |
| General practice / family medicine | Longitudinal course, coordination, medication and coexisting-condition review, reassessment and everyday impact |
| Occupational therapy | Occupational performance, daily activities, routines, cognitive/sensory demands, environmental fit, assistance and participation |
| Physiotherapy | Movement, mobility, balance, task performance, endurance, safety, variability and the cost of practice |
| Psychology / psychotherapy and other therapists | Distress, coping, adjustment, mental health, relationships, stigma and the person's goals, without assuming a psychological cause |
| Other relevant professions | Add the perspective the page needs, such as speech-language communication access, nursing care or social support; make the role clear |

Use overlapping perspectives to improve shared understanding. The table is illustrative, not a scope-of-practice rule. Psychological care is one part of comprehensive care; its inclusion must not imply that FND is primarily psychological or that mental illness is required.

## Connect assessment findings with everyday life

Keep **diagnostic evidence**, **performance during an assessment**, and **disability in daily life** distinct. A brief successful task or a normal standard-test score must not, by itself, be presented as evidence of mild disability, sustained independence, or low support needs. Describe what a measure assesses, the circumstances in which it was used, and what remains unmeasured. Do not dismiss standard assessments wholesale; explain their specific uses and limits with supporting evidence.

Where relevant to the symptom and supported by the evidence, consider variability, episodic symptoms, endurance, cognitive and sensory load, environmental demands, safety, assistance, delayed worsening and recovery time. Do not assume every person experiences all of these. Distinguish completing a task once from doing it reliably, repeatedly and safely at an acceptable cost in ordinary life.

Connect an observation to a practical question rather than inventing an individual's limitations. For example, successful performance in a quiet assessment may leave open how the same activity works amid interruptions, how much help is needed, or what recovery time follows. Seek the person's account and relevant assessment evidence. Do not use symptom provocation or exhausting tests merely to make disability visible.

## Research outcomes that matter to people

Research coverage should include quality of life and mental health alongside symptoms and examination findings. Where relevant, look for daily function, participation, work or education, relationships, independence and assistance, safety, stigma, caregiver burden and patient-reported outcomes. Explain what the observed change may mean for a person's life and what the study did not measure.

Keep mental-health burden, a coexisting mental-health condition, and a proposed causal mechanism separate. Association does not establish causation. Symptom reduction, better test performance and improved quality of life are different outcomes; do not assume one guarantees another. Equally, useful support or participation gains can matter while symptoms persist. Describe variation and evidence gaps without extrapolating one person's experience to everyone.

*Guidance adopted from the clinician-awareness discussion in “Sitemap URL guidance,” September 24, 2026. These are editorial requirements; clinical claims made when applying them still require appropriate sources.*
