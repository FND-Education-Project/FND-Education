# FND Education

**Free, evidence-informed education for people living with Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) and the people who support them.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This project is in early development. It is an educational resource, not medical advice, diagnosis, or a replacement for care from qualified healthcare professionals.

## Why this project exists

Functional Neurological Disorder can be difficult to understand, difficult to explain, and difficult to live with. Useful information is scattered across research papers, clinical guidelines, specialist services, patient organizations, and the experiences of people living with FND.

This project has two primary goals:

1. **Help people with FND understand the research around their condition well enough to cope, communicate, make informed decisions, and participate meaningfully in treatment and rehabilitation.**
2. **Give family members, caregivers, friends, and other support persons understandable research-based information and practical ideas that may help them support the person without blame, fear, overprotection, or loss of autonomy.**

The long-term aim is a free, highly searchable FND education course and reference library that can grow to include worksheets, surveys, community discussion, and other support resources.

---

# Our role: explain the evidence, not become the authority

FND Education is not intended to speak as an authority on FND.

The authority should remain with the evidence itself: published research, clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, consensus documents, and clearly identified clinical expertise.

Our job is to find that information, examine it critically, explain it in ordinary language, connect it to practical questions people with FND actually have, and make it easy to trace back to its sources.

Where the evidence is uncertain, conflicting, preliminary, or absent, the wording should make that uncertainty visible.

Whenever possible, the tone should therefore be:

- **“Research has found…”**
- **“Several studies suggest…”**
- **“A systematic review reported…”**
- **“Clinical guidelines recommend…”**
- **“Researchers do not yet know…”**
- **“There is disagreement about…”**
- **“This is a proposed model rather than an established explanation…”**

rather than:

- “FND is caused by…”
- “This happens because…”
- “You should…”
- “The correct treatment is…”

unless the underlying evidence genuinely justifies that degree of certainty.

Readers should be able to tell **where an idea came from, how strong the evidence is, and where our knowledge ends.**

# Core principles

These principles guide how material will be researched, written, and presented.

### Claims should come from identifiable sources

Important claims should be traceable to research, professional guidance, or another clearly named source. Readers should not have to accept a statement simply because FND Education says it.

### Certainty should match the evidence

Research findings, clinical consensus, hypotheses, individual clinical models, and lived experience are not the same thing. They should not be written as though they carry equal evidentiary weight.

### Functional symptoms are described in research and clinical guidance as genuine and involuntary

Pages discussing this principle should show readers the evidence and clinical reasoning behind it rather than asking them to accept the statement on authority.

### Diagnosis should be explained through positive evidence

Modern clinical literature emphasizes positive diagnostic features of FND rather than treating it simply as what remains after tests are normal. The diagnosis section of this project will explain the evidence supporting that change in approach and its limitations.

### No single theory explains every person

FND is heterogeneous. Proposed mechanisms and explanatory models should be presented as the research presents them, including competing models and unanswered questions.

### Trauma and psychological stress should not be assumed

Research may find trauma, stress, psychiatric symptoms, or adverse experiences relevant in some groups or individuals, but that does not establish them as necessary causes for every person. Pages should distinguish association, risk factor, trigger, maintaining factor, and cause.

### FND should not automatically explain every symptom

Research and clinical guidance recognize that people with FND may also have other neurological and medical conditions. Educational pages should repeatedly guard against diagnostic overshadowing and clearly identify situations that warrant medical reassessment.

### Treatment information should preserve uncertainty and individual differences

Treatment studies tell us what has helped groups of participants under particular conditions. They do not guarantee what will help an individual reader. We should explain both the evidence for an intervention and its limitations.

### Support should preserve autonomy

Practical advice for support persons should aim to improve safety, understanding, confidence, and independence rather than turn a family member into a clinician or supervisor.

# Course map

The course is designed as a **self-paced reference course**, not a rigid week-by-week program. Readers should be able to follow it from beginning to end or enter through the symptom or problem most relevant to them.

## Part One: Understanding FND

### Module 1 — What FND Is

How FND is described in current research and clinical guidance; functional neurological symptoms, common symptom families, involuntary symptoms, terminology, and common misconceptions.

### Module 2 — How FND Is Diagnosed

Research and guidance concerning positive clinical signs, neurological examination, appropriate testing, diagnostic uncertainty, and FND occurring alongside other neurological disease.

### Module 3 — Causes, Mechanisms, and Honest Uncertainty

Current scientific models, attention, expectation, prediction, automatic movement, illness or injury triggers, stress and trauma when relevant, and the difference between evidence, association, theory, and speculation.

### Module 4 — Mapping Your Individual Condition

A practical framework for separating primary FND symptoms, associated symptoms, separate diagnoses, medications, patterns, triggers, warning signs, helpful strategies, and preserved abilities.

## Part Two: Safety and Symptom Knowledge

### Module 5 — Medical Safety and New Symptoms

What clinical literature says about avoiding both diagnostic overshadowing and unnecessary emergency treatment; recognizing meaningful changes and creating an individualized emergency information sheet.

### Module 6 — Functional Seizures and Episodic Symptoms

Research and clinical guidance concerning functional seizures, dissociative seizures, psychogenic nonepileptic seizures (PNES), altered-awareness episodes, episode safety, recovery, and coexistence with epilepsy.

### Module 7 — Functional Movement, Weakness, and Gait Symptoms

Research on functional movement disorder, functional weakness, paralysis, tremor, jerks, spasms, dystonia, gait problems, falls, and movement retraining.

### Module 8 — Sensory, Visual, Balance, and Dizziness Symptoms

What is known about numbness, altered sensation, hypersensitivity, functional visual symptoms, dizziness, balance problems, sensory overload, accommodations, and rehabilitation.

### Module 9 — Speech, Voice, Swallowing, and Breathing Symptoms

Research and clinical approaches concerning functional speech and voice symptoms, word blocking, swallowing symptoms, globus, cough, breathing symptoms, communication strategies, and appropriate specialist assessment.

## Part Three: Common Non-Motor Difficulties

### Module 10 — Cognition, Memory, and Dissociation

Research concerning attention, processing speed, working memory, episodic memory, semantic retrieval, word finding, functional cognitive disorder (FCD), cognitive fatigue, dissociation, and other possible contributors to cognitive symptoms.

### Module 11 — Pain, Migraine, Fatigue, and Sleep

What research says about chronic pain, migraine, fatigue, sleep disruption, medication effects, overlapping conditions, pacing, and identifying treatable contributors.

### Module 12 — Autonomic and Whole-Body Symptoms

Research concerning sweating, temperature changes, nausea, dizziness, palpitations, overlap with other conditions, useful tracking, and the limits of attributing these symptoms automatically to FND.

## Part Four: Treatment and Rehabilitation

### Module 13 — Building an Individual Treatment Team

What clinical guidance says about the possible roles of neurology, primary care, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech-language therapy, psychology, neuropsychology, psychiatry, rehabilitation, pain, migraine, and sleep care.

### Module 14 — Rehabilitation and Neuroplastic Change

Research on practice, repetition, automatic movement, redirected attention, graded rehabilitation, meaningful activity, setbacks, and realistic expectations—including where popular explanations of “neuroplasticity” go beyond the evidence.

### Module 15 — Pacing, Activity, and the Boom-and-Bust Cycle

Research and clinical approaches to baseline activity, planned exertion, restorative rest, capacity building, flexible goals, and avoiding both excessive pushing and excessive avoidance.

### Module 16 — Psychological Treatment Without Blame

Evidence concerning psychological therapies in FND; why their possible usefulness does not establish that symptoms are imaginary or psychologically caused; and how cognitive-behavioural, acceptance-based, mindfulness, and trauma-focused approaches may fit different people.

## Part Five: Living With FND

### Module 17 — Daily Living, Accessibility, and Equipment

Evidence, rehabilitation principles, clinical guidance, and lived experience concerning personal care, household tasks, mobility aids, wheelchairs, environmental adaptations, communication aids, safety, and periodic reassessment.

### Module 18 — Relationships, Identity, and Grief

Research and lived experience concerning changes in roles, loss, intimacy, family life, friendship, isolation, identity beyond illness, pleasure, interests, and purpose.

### Module 19 — Healthcare Communication and Self-Advocacy

Research, clinical guidance, and practical strategies for preparing for appointments, keeping concise medical histories, requesting written instructions, correcting inaccurate records, seeking second opinions, reducing diagnostic overshadowing, and using a support person effectively.

### Module 20 — Work, Disability, and Community Participation

Evidence and practical information concerning return-to-work planning where appropriate, accommodations, disability, transportation, finances, volunteering, hobbies, and meaningful participation with variable capacity.

## Part Six: Long-Term Management

### Module 21 — Setbacks, Relapse, and Changing Symptoms

What research and clinical guidance say about symptom fluctuation, relapse planning, reassessment, returning to useful strategies, and adjusting rehabilitation after setbacks.

### Module 22 — Building Your Personal FND Handbook

A practical personal reference containing diagnoses, symptoms, warning signs, strategies, communication needs, episode plans, emergency criteria, medications, care team, rehabilitation goals, and supporter instructions.

### Module 23 — Reviewing Progress

Ways to evaluate knowledge, safety, independence, participation, communication, support-person confidence, symptom changes, and future goals without reducing recovery to a single symptom score.

# Every educational page will use the same core structure

Consistency is a central accessibility feature of this project, not a cosmetic preference.

Readers should not have to relearn how the website works every time they open a different symptom or treatment page.

Every substantive educational page will therefore use the same core structure:

## 1. Plain-language title

The ordinary term a reader is most likely to recognize.

## 2. Other names and search terms

Current clinical terminology, abbreviations, older terminology, and common phrases people may encounter in medical records, forums, or search engines.

## 3. In brief

A short plain-language summary for readers who do not have the energy or concentration to read the full page.

## 4. What the research says

The main findings from relevant research, systematic reviews, clinical guidelines, and consensus documents, written with certainty proportional to the evidence.

## 5. What researchers do not yet know

Uncertainty, conflicting findings, important limitations, and unanswered questions.

## 6. What this can feel or look like

Descriptions of the symptom or problem in everyday terms while making clear how much variation exists between people.

## 7. What else can cause similar symptoms?

Important differential diagnoses, overlapping conditions, medication effects, or other contributors that the literature identifies. This section is educational rather than diagnostic.

## 8. How clinicians may assess it

What the literature and professional guidance describe about assessment, positive signs, tests, or specialist evaluation.

## 9. What treatments have been studied?

Available treatment evidence, including the populations studied, likely limitations, and how confident researchers are in the findings.

## 10. What can I try at home?

Every symptom or practical-management page will end with usable ideas wherever safe self-management is possible.

This section should separate suggestions by source and evidence level, for example:

- strategies supported directly by research;
- strategies recommended in clinical guidance;
- low-risk ideas used in rehabilitation practice but not well studied independently;
- practical adaptations reported by people living with FND.

A lack of strong evidence does not make a practical problem disappear. When research has not answered an everyday question, we may look at patient communities and forums to learn what people say they actually do. Those reports can generate useful ideas, but they must be labelled clearly as **lived experience or community-reported strategies**, not presented as proven treatments.

Forum reports should be summarized rather than treated as representative surveys. Where possible, we should look for recurring themes across multiple people rather than highlight a single dramatic anecdote.

Practical suggestions should also explain relevant risks, reasons to stop, and situations in which professional guidance is more appropriate than self-management.

## 11. How can a support person help?

Specific ways a family member, partner, friend, caregiver, or other support person may help—along with things that may unintentionally make the situation harder.

## 12. When should this be medically reassessed?

Changes, red flags, injuries, or unusual features that should not simply be assumed to be part of established FND.

## 13. Questions you may want to ask a clinician

Short practical questions a reader can take to an appointment.

## 14. Related topics

Links to other relevant FND Education pages.

## 15. Sources and evidence notes

References, links to important papers or guidance, and brief notes explaining why particular sources were used.

## 16. Last reviewed

The date on which the evidence and external links were last checked.

This structure may contain an explicitly marked **Not applicable** section when a heading truly does not apply, but headings should not silently disappear from page to page. Predictability is part of the design.

# Evidence standard

Every substantial educational page will clearly distinguish among the following levels of information.

## 1. Established or well-supported evidence

Findings supported by comparatively strong research, replicated findings, major reviews, or established diagnostic evidence.

## 2. Clinical consensus or guideline-based practice

Recommendations supported by professional guidelines, consensus documents, or established specialist practice even when randomized-trial evidence remains incomplete.

## 3. Emerging or uncertain evidence

Promising findings that remain preliminary, inconsistent, limited, or under active investigation.

## 4. Clinical model or hypothesis

A proposed explanation or framework that may be useful but should not be presented as established biological fact.

## 5. Lived experience and community-reported practice

Experiences, adaptations, and self-management ideas reported by people living with FND or those who support them.

These reports can be valuable—especially for practical questions research has barely studied—but they are not proof that a strategy is effective, safe for everyone, or caused a reported improvement.

Pages should identify disagreement and uncertainty rather than hiding it.

# Search and usability standards

A major goal of FND Education is to make information easy to **find, understand, revisit, and share**.

Every educational page will include:

- a plain-language title;
- relevant clinical terminology and common synonyms;
- a short summary near the top;
- the standard page sections described above;
- short sections with descriptive headings;
- important terms defined where they first appear;
- links to related topics;
- a dedicated support-person section;
- a practical **“What can I try at home?”** section;
- a **“When should this be medically reassessed?”** section;
- references to the research and guidance used;
- a **last reviewed** date;
- descriptive alternative text for meaningful images;
- transcripts or captions for audio and video material;
- wording that avoids unnecessary jargon and stigma.

### Search terminology

Pages should deliberately include both modern and historically common terms when doing so helps people find reliable information. For example:

- Functional Neurological Disorder — **FND**
- Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder — **FNSD**
- Functional seizures — **dissociative seizures**, **psychogenic nonepileptic seizures**, **PNES**
- Functional Movement Disorder — **FMD**
- Functional Cognitive Disorder — **FCD**
- Functional weakness — **functional limb weakness**, **functional paralysis**

Older terms should be labelled as historical, outdated, disputed, or potentially stigmatizing when appropriate rather than silently treated as preferred terminology.

# Accessibility standards

FND itself can make reading and navigating educational material difficult. Cognitive symptoms, migraine, visual sensitivity, fatigue, pain, motor disability, hearing problems, and sensory sensitivity should be considered in the design.

The project will aim for:

- short, readable paragraphs;
- predictable page structure;
- descriptive headings;
- strong contrast without unnecessary visual clutter;
- meaningful link text rather than repeated “click here” links;
- keyboard-accessible navigation in any future website;
- image alternative text;
- captions and transcripts for video;
- printable summaries;
- content that remains understandable when read by screen readers;
- important information conveyed in text rather than colour alone;
- minimal unnecessary animation or flashing content.

# Research and citation policy

Educational claims should be traceable whenever practical.

Preference should generally be given to:

1. diagnostic criteria and authoritative clinical guidance;
2. systematic reviews and meta-analyses;
3. peer-reviewed primary research;
4. professional consensus statements;
5. specialist educational material when its claims can be independently evaluated.

Lived experience, patient forums, and community discussions may also be examined for questions such as day-to-day adaptations, coping strategies, accessibility, communication, and self-management that formal research has not adequately addressed.

When community material is used:

- it should be clearly labelled as lived experience rather than research evidence;
- recurring patterns across multiple reports are more informative than isolated anecdotes;
- safety-sensitive suggestions should be checked against medical evidence and clinical guidance where possible;
- claims of cures, mechanisms, or guaranteed treatment effects should not be inferred from testimonials;
- personal details should not be reproduced unnecessarily;
- the original community's rules and reasonable expectations of privacy should be respected.

Individual clinician theories, commercial treatment claims, and unsourced patient-education statements should not be promoted to the same evidentiary level as peer-reviewed evidence.

Research summaries should include enough information for readers to understand **what was actually studied**, the major limitations, and whether the findings justify a causal conclusion.

# Authorship approach

The educational pages will be written in a human voice, but the writer's voice should not be mistaken for the source of medical authority.

Wherever a statement could reasonably be challenged with “How do you know that?”, the page should make the answer discoverable.

The intended relationship with the reader is:

**“Here is what we found in the research. Here is how strong that evidence appears to be. Here is what remains uncertain. Here are practical ideas that may help. Here is where those ideas came from.”**

The project should be willing to say **“we do not know”** when the evidence does not provide a reliable answer.

---

# Safety and editorial rules

FND Education will not:

- diagnose visitors;
- declare a new symptom to be functional;
- promise recovery or a cure;
- require trauma or psychological stress as an explanation;
- describe functional symptoms as chosen behaviour;
- recommend stopping prescribed medication without the prescriber;
- present one treatment as appropriate for everyone;
- treat every symptom in a person with FND as part of FND;
- present anecdotes as proof;
- hide meaningful scientific disagreement or uncertainty.

Emergency and medical-safety limitations should be stated clearly wherever they matter.

## Medical disclaimer

FND Education provides general educational information only. It does not provide individual medical advice, diagnosis, emergency assessment, or treatment. Research findings describe groups and do not determine what is true or appropriate for a particular individual. A person's own healthcare professionals remain responsible for individualized medical decisions. New, severe, or substantially changed symptoms should not be assumed to be FND solely because a person already has an FND diagnosis.
