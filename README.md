# FND Education

**Free, evidence-informed education for people living with Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) and the people who support them.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This project is in early development. It is an educational resource, not medical advice, diagnosis, or a replacement for care from qualified healthcare professionals.

## Why this project exists

Functional Neurological Disorder can be difficult to understand, difficult to explain, and difficult to live with. Good information is often scattered across research papers, specialist clinics, professional guidelines, patient organizations, and individual clinicians.

This project has two primary goals:

1. **Help people with FND understand their condition well enough to cope, communicate, make informed decisions, and participate meaningfully in treatment and rehabilitation.**
2. **Give family members, caregivers, friends, and other support persons the knowledge and practical skills needed to help without blame, fear, overprotection, or taking away the person's independence.**

The long-term aim is a free, highly searchable FND education course and reference library that can grow to include worksheets, surveys, community discussion, and other support resources.

## Core principles

This project will follow several rules throughout the course.

### FND is real and involuntary

Functional symptoms are not fabricated, chosen, or simply a failure of willpower.

### FND should be explained using positive evidence

FND should not be presented merely as a diagnosis made because tests were normal or because another explanation could not be found.

### No single theory explains every person

FND is heterogeneous. A useful educational resource must distinguish established evidence from hypotheses, clinical models, and personal experience.

### Trauma and psychological stress are not required

They may be important for some people and irrelevant for others. They should never be assumed to be the hidden cause of someone's FND.

### FND does not explain every symptom automatically

A person with FND can also have migraine, epilepsy, structural neurological disease, sleep disorders, pain disorders, autonomic disorders, medication effects, and ordinary medical emergencies.

### Treatment should be individualized

Different people may benefit from different combinations of neurological care, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech-language therapy, psychological treatment, pain treatment, migraine care, sleep care, rehabilitation, accessibility support, and other medical care.

### Support should preserve autonomy

Helping is not the same as taking over. Support should improve safety, understanding, confidence, and independence whenever possible.

# Course map

The course is designed as a **self-paced reference course**, not a rigid week-by-week program. Readers should be able to follow it from beginning to end or enter through the symptom or problem most relevant to them.

## Part One: Understanding FND

### Module 1 — What FND Is

Functional neurological symptoms, common symptom families, involuntary symptoms, and common misconceptions.

### Module 2 — How FND Is Diagnosed

Positive clinical signs, neurological examination, appropriate testing, diagnostic uncertainty, and FND occurring alongside other neurological disease.

### Module 3 — Causes, Mechanisms, and Honest Uncertainty

Current scientific models, attention, expectation, prediction, automatic movement, illness or injury triggers, stress and trauma when relevant, and the difference between evidence and theory.

### Module 4 — Mapping Your Individual Condition

Primary FND symptoms, associated symptoms, separate diagnoses, medications, patterns, triggers, warning signs, helpful strategies, and preserved abilities.

## Part Two: Safety and Symptom Knowledge

### Module 5 — Medical Safety and New Symptoms

How to avoid both diagnostic overshadowing and unnecessary emergency treatment; recognizing significant changes and creating an individualized emergency information sheet.

### Module 6 — Functional Seizures and Episodic Symptoms

Functional seizures, dissociative seizures, psychogenic nonepileptic seizures (PNES), altered-awareness episodes, episode safety, recovery, and coexistence with epilepsy.

### Module 7 — Functional Movement, Weakness, and Gait Symptoms

Functional movement disorder, functional weakness, paralysis, tremor, jerks, spasms, dystonia, gait problems, falls, and movement retraining.

### Module 8 — Sensory, Visual, Balance, and Dizziness Symptoms

Numbness, altered sensation, hypersensitivity, functional visual symptoms, dizziness, balance problems, sensory overload, accommodations, and rehabilitation.

### Module 9 — Speech, Voice, Swallowing, and Breathing Symptoms

Functional speech and voice symptoms, word blocking, swallowing symptoms, globus, cough, breathing symptoms, communication strategies, and appropriate specialist assessment.

## Part Three: Common Non-Motor Difficulties

### Module 10 — Cognition, Memory, and Dissociation

Attention, processing speed, working memory, episodic memory, semantic retrieval, word finding, functional cognitive disorder (FCD), cognitive fatigue, dissociation, and other possible contributors to cognitive symptoms.

### Module 11 — Pain, Migraine, Fatigue, and Sleep

Chronic pain, migraine, fatigue, sleep disruption, medication effects, overlapping conditions, pacing, and identifying treatable contributors.

### Module 12 — Autonomic and Whole-Body Symptoms

Sweating, temperature changes, nausea, dizziness, palpitations, overlap with other conditions, useful tracking, and avoiding automatic attribution of every symptom to FND.

## Part Four: Treatment and Rehabilitation

### Module 13 — Building an Individual Treatment Team

Neurology, primary care, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech-language therapy, psychology, neuropsychology, psychiatry, rehabilitation, pain, migraine, and sleep care.

### Module 14 — Rehabilitation and Neuroplastic Change

Practice, repetition, automatic movement, redirected attention, graded rehabilitation, meaningful activity, setbacks, and realistic expectations.

### Module 15 — Pacing, Activity, and the Boom-and-Bust Cycle

Baseline activity, planned exertion, restorative rest, capacity building, flexible goals, and avoiding both excessive pushing and excessive avoidance.

### Module 16 — Psychological Treatment Without Blame

Why psychological treatment does not mean symptoms are imaginary; cognitive-behavioural, acceptance-based, mindfulness, and trauma-focused approaches when appropriate; recognizing unsuitable or overly certain explanations.

## Part Five: Living With FND

### Module 17 — Daily Living, Accessibility, and Equipment

Personal care, household tasks, mobility aids, wheelchairs, environmental adaptations, communication aids, safety, and periodic reassessment.

### Module 18 — Relationships, Identity, and Grief

Changes in roles, loss, intimacy, family life, friendship, isolation, identity beyond illness, pleasure, interests, and purpose.

### Module 19 — Healthcare Communication and Self-Advocacy

Preparing for appointments, concise medical histories, written instructions, inaccurate records, second opinions, diagnostic overshadowing, and effective use of a support person.

### Module 20 — Work, Disability, and Community Participation

Return-to-work planning where appropriate, accommodations, disability, transportation, finances, volunteering, hobbies, and meaningful participation with variable capacity.

## Part Six: Long-Term Management

### Module 21 — Setbacks, Relapse, and Changing Symptoms

Fluctuation, relapse planning, deciding when reassessment is appropriate, returning to useful strategies, and adjusting rehabilitation without catastrophizing setbacks.

### Module 22 — Building Your Personal FND Handbook

A practical personal reference containing diagnoses, symptoms, warning signs, strategies, communication needs, episode plans, emergency criteria, medications, care team, rehabilitation goals, and supporter instructions.

### Module 23 — Reviewing Progress

Knowledge, safety, independence, participation, communication, support-person confidence, symptom changes, and realistic future goals.

# Evidence standard

Every substantial educational page should clearly distinguish among the following levels of information.

## 1. Established or well-supported evidence

Supported by strong research, replicated findings, major reviews, or established diagnostic evidence.

## 2. Clinical consensus or guideline-based practice

Supported by professional guidelines, consensus recommendations, or established specialist practice even when randomized-trial evidence is incomplete.

## 3. Emerging or uncertain evidence

Promising findings that remain preliminary, inconsistent, limited, or under active investigation.

## 4. Clinical model, hypothesis, or lived experience

Potentially useful ways of understanding or managing symptoms that should not be presented as proven biological fact.

Pages should identify disagreement and uncertainty rather than hiding it.

# Search and usability standards

A major goal of FND Education is to make information easy to **find, understand, revisit, and share**.

Every educational page should eventually include:

- a plain-language title;
- the relevant clinical terminology and common synonyms;
- a short summary at the top;
- a table of contents for longer pages;
- short sections with descriptive headings;
- important terms defined where they first appear;
- links to related topics;
- a dedicated support-person section where relevant;
- a practical "What can I do?" section where appropriate;
- a "When should this be medically reassessed?" section where appropriate;
- references to primary research, systematic reviews, and professional guidance;
- a **last reviewed** date;
- descriptive alternative text for meaningful images;
- transcripts or captions for audio and video material;
- wording that avoids unnecessary jargon and stigma.

### Search terminology

Pages should deliberately include both modern and historically common terms when doing so helps people find reliable information. For example:

- Functional Neurological Disorder — **FND**
- Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder — **FNSD**
- Functional seizures — **dissociative seizures**, **psychogenic nonepileptic seizures**, **PNES**
- Functional Movement Disorder — **FMD**
- Functional Cognitive Disorder — **FCD**
- Functional weakness — **functional limb weakness**, **functional paralysis**

Older terms should be labelled as historical, outdated, or potentially stigmatizing when appropriate rather than silently treated as preferred terminology.

# Accessibility standards

FND itself can make reading and navigating educational material difficult. Cognitive symptoms, migraine, visual sensitivity, fatigue, pain, motor disability, hearing problems, and sensory sensitivity must be considered in the design.

The project should therefore aim for:

- short, readable paragraphs;
- predictable page structure;
- descriptive headings;
- strong contrast without unnecessary visual clutter;
- meaningful link text rather than repeated "click here" links;
- keyboard-accessible navigation in any future website;
- image alternative text;
- captions and transcripts for video;
- printable summaries;
- content that remains understandable when read by screen readers;
- important information conveyed in text rather than colour alone;
- minimal unnecessary animation or flashing content.

# Research and citation policy

Educational claims should be traceable whenever practical.

Preference should generally be given to:

1. diagnostic criteria and authoritative clinical guidance;
2. systematic reviews and meta-analyses;
3. peer-reviewed primary research;
4. professional consensus statements;
5. specialist educational material when its claims can be independently evaluated.

Individual clinician theories, commercial treatment claims, and unsourced patient-education statements should not be promoted to the same evidentiary level as peer-reviewed evidence.

Research summaries should include enough information for readers to understand **what was actually studied**, the major limitations, and whether the findings justify a causal conclusion.

---

# Safety and editorial rules

FND Education will not:

- diagnose visitors;
- declare a new symptom to be functional;
- promise recovery or a cure;
- require trauma or psychological stress as an explanation;
- describe functional symptoms as chosen behaviour;
- recommend stopping prescribed medication without the prescriber;
- present one treatment as appropriate for everyone;
- treat every symptom in a person with FND as part of FND;
- hide meaningful scientific disagreement or uncertainty.

Emergency and medical-safety limitations should be stated clearly wherever they matter.

## Medical disclaimer

FND Education provides general educational information only. It does not provide individual medical advice, diagnosis, emergency assessment, or treatment. A person's own healthcare professionals remain responsible for individualized medical decisions. New, severe, or substantially changed symptoms should not be assumed to be FND solely because a person already has an FND diagnosis.
